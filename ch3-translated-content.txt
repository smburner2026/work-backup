CHAPTER II

EMPEROR HÀM NGHI AND TÔN THẤT THUYẾT FLEE NORTH

FROM HUẾ TO TÂN SỞ (QUẢNG TRỊ)

Of the attack on the French during the night of the 22nd day of the 5th month of the Ất Dậu year (July 5, 1885) in the heart of the imperial capital of Thuận Hóa, Emperor Hàm Nghi and the Three Palaces knew virtually nothing. If the Empress Dowager was aware of anything, it was only that the two Regents were committed to resisting France to the bitter end — not that a decisive crisis was imminent. The two Regents evidently feared that the young emperor and the dowagers would not countenance their audacious design. They mobilized their forces in secret; Hàm Nghi and the Three Palaces were among the last to learn that an attack upon the French had been launched.

Towards dawn, when the Southern troops were already faltering, Tôn Thất Thuyet mustered his shattered remnants and stationed them outside the Chương Đức Gate.[1] Sword in hand, he entered the Imperial Palace to summon the emperor and the Three Palaces to flight. He reported the night's catastrophe and the failure to the Empress Dowager. Từ Dũ spoke with unusual composure:

— A weak state must yield to a strong one; what is strange in that? Even in the days of the late Emperor we were compelled to conclude peace treaties; how much more so today, when circumstances change daily...

Yet for all her words, the Dowager, together with Trang Ý and Học Phi, had no choice but to step into the palanquins. Hàm Nghi was likewise hurried. The emperor cried out in shock:

— With whom have I been fighting that I must flee?

There was no time for reply. Thuyết drew his sword and ordered the soldiers to lift the emperor into the palanquin, exiting through the Right Gate of the Imperial City and proceeding southwest. His first plan had been to take the Imperial Procession to the Khiêm Cung[2] for temporary refuge, out of reach of French indignities.

In the haste, only the imperial seal of command and two swords could be brought. By about half past seven the Imperial Procession had cleared the city gates and was hastening directly toward Thiên Mụ Pagoda. Thuyết remained behind long enough to order the destruction of Bạch Hồ Bridge, to prevent enemy pursuit. Hàm Nghi's state of mind at this moment was one of utter desolation. He was still a boy — barely fifteen years of age — and the national calamity had struck with unbearable suddenness. As he mounted the imperial palanquin he tripped on the step and wept aloud, and even the Three Palaces could not restrain their tears.

Thuyết announced that he would stay at Trường Thi until evening to await intelligence, above all to hope for reinforcements. His own soldiers, however, had by now nearly all deserted. In his bitterness he railed harshly at Tôn Thất Phan, Vice Minister of Justice — the very man who but recently had been most vociferous in urging resistance and was now among the first to lose heart. Thuyet reproached Châu Đình Kế, Vice Minister of Public Works, and Phạm Thận Duật, Minister of Rites, for having ruined so much through their conduct of the war that Từ Dũ herself had had to intervene to restore order. Hàm Nghi stood apart, motionless, not stirring until everyone else set out. The Imperial Procession consisted mostly of princes and princesses, nobles and their sons — some mounted, others on foot — while among the common people, old and young clutching one another, everyone wailed in terror and sought desperately to avoid the bullets and shells that flew in every direction beyond the city walls. Among the high officials, Tả Quân Đô Thống Hồ Văn Hiền brought a hundred musketeers as the imperial guard, forming a company perhaps a thousand strong. The escort included Phạm Thận Duật (Minister of Rites), Trương Đăng Đệ (Vice Minister of War), Tôn Thất Liệt, and others. Đề đốc Trần Xuân Soạn led the vanguard; Nguyễn Văn Tường commanded the rear. But many who were nominally part of the escort held back, lingering in hiding to see how events would unfold; these were men whose spirits had been deeply shaken.

The Imperial Procession pressed on with all speed. At the Kẻ Vạn ferry landing, Hàm Nghi had to descend from his palanquin. No boats were to be found. The emperor removed his shoes, rolled his trousers to the knee to wade across — but after a few steps was forced back by the river's depth. Thuyết then took a different route, skirting the citadel wall and crossing Bạch Hồ Bridge to lead the Procession toward Kim Long Church, heading directly for Thiên Mụ Pagoda. Behind them, from within the citadel, the roar of French guns continued unabated. Fearing pursuit, the Procession fled in panic. The rough road caused Hàm Nghi's head to strike against the side of the palanquin, and Thuyết had to ask the emperor to dismount and travel instead in a hammock-chair.

The Procession halted at Văn Xá village as darkness fell, then resumed its march to Quảng Trị at cockcrow. By the time the emperor and the Three Palaces had left the capital, the French tricolor was already flying from the Flag Tower. French troops poured into the citadel and began an orgy of arson and slaughter. The Ministries of War and Personnel — the two departments most closely associated with Thuyết and Tường — were the first to feed the flames. The smoke from burning houses and government buildings darkened the sky; for two days afterward the fires still smoldered. Not a single guard remained at his post. In Từ Dũ's apartments, a golden tray nearly six spans in diameter still bore the remnants of the morning meal, untouched. At the Duyệt Thị đường, hundreds of chests of silver ingots lay undisturbed. For five days, fifty soldiers were kept busy packing gold and silver for shipment to France, along with palace treasures of incalculable value. The French command permitted its troops to plunder freely for a full forty-eight hours; every soldier stuffed his pockets and uniform with silver ingots. Afterward the French divided up the gates among themselves, requisitioned rice from the granaries, and hired civilians to clear away the corpses and bury the dead.

While Thuyết and a remnant of his troops held the French at bay to give the Procession time to get clear, the Dowager Từ Dũ ordered Nguyễn Văn Tường to return to the capital and negotiate with the French. (The Đại Nam thực lục, the Hành thứ ca of Lê Tân Nguyễn Nhược Thị, and Trần Trọng Kim's Việt Nam sử lược all record this episode.) A few hours later Thuyết caught up with the Procession, urging everyone constantly onward.

At noon on July 6, 1885, while the citadel still belched smoke, Nguyễn Văn Tường arrived at Kim Long Church (five kilometers from Huế) to meet Bishop Caspar, and took his meal there. That evening, through the Bishop's intercession, he was brought before General de Courcy. The French Commander-in-Chief assigned Tường to the Thương Bạc Viện (Office of Commerce and Communication), placed under the guard of Captain Schmitz and a squad of French soldiers. (At the end of July, Tường was transferred to the newly-arrived Deputy Resident Hamelin, who gave him two months to settle all affairs.) Tường's first act was to sign a declaration drafted at de Courcy's direction, denouncing Tôn Thất Thuyết and calling upon all resistance forces, imperial princes, and Emperor Hàm Nghi himself to return to the capital and resume all affairs as before. De Courcy also signed this declaration, dating it July 6, 1885. The following day, July 9, Tường was compelled to write to the Three Palaces, inviting them to take up temporary residence at the Vạn Niên Mausoleum of Tự Đức. This letter achieved its purpose: the three dowagers agreed to return. On July 11, Tường addressed the following words directly to Hàm Nghi:

"Ten thousand times we bow before Your Majesty,

"Your servant has just received a telegram from Trương Quang Đản reporting that the three Empresses Dowager are now residing in the temporary palace in Quảng Trị province. I have invited the three Ladies to rest temporarily at Khiêm Cung. The French Commander-in-Chief wishes to invite Your Majesty also to return together with the three Ladies in one journey."

Signed: NGUYỄN VĂN TƯỜNG

Two days later, on July 13, de Courcy likewise sent a letter to Hàm Nghi, couched in terms of great respect and urgency, urging the emperor to return.

On July 13, 1885, Hàm Nghi replied to Nguyễn Văn Tường as follows:

"I, the Emperor, am young in years and of meager virtue, encountering a time of national calamity when the people are sunk in misery and distress beyond description. Within the country I look to my officials; abroad I relied upon the sincere friendship of France... Alas, France has but sought to oppress me, forcing me to abandon my capital. I must go to Hà Tĩnh together with the Grand Regent Tôn Thất Thuyết to await the turn of events.

"Now I charge you, as a Grand Regent more worthy than any other, to settle matters with the French authorities. Heaven and earth, as well as the people, are watching me. I with you are as fish and water — how can we be separated? Following the example of the late Emperor, strive to negotiate, that the atmosphere between the two nations may become harmonious, that the bond of brotherhood may be preserved, sharing together honor and disgrace, joy and sorrow, without recourse to force or stratagem against one another.

"If this hope is fulfilled, I will return to the capital to pay my respects to the Three Palaces, fulfilling my filial duty and satisfying the people's expectations... Should the matter not succeed, I entrust to you the care of the ancestral temples and the Imperial House, for many have not followed me.

"Henceforth, if you have letters to send me, send them by this relay station..."

Dated: 2nd day of the 6th month, Hàm Nghi đệ nhị niên (Ất Dậu).

This letter bore the seal of Tôn Thất Thuyết, as did the emperor's first public proclamation, and was backdated to July 7 at Quảng Trị.

Let us now retrace the journey of the Imperial Procession. At noon on July 6, 1885, having set out at dawn from Văn Xá, the Procession reached Quảng Trị. All were exhausted. Tuần Vũ Trương Quang Đản received them into the temporary palace and posted guards. The officials who gradually assembled included Hậu Quân Nguyễn Hanh, Đô Thống Tôn Thất Ninh, Tả Quân Đinh Tử Lượng, Tham Tri Tôn Thất Phan, Biện Lý Trần Khánh Tôn, Thủy Sư Hiệp Lý Cao Hữu Sung, Thủ Đoán Nguyễn Đình Đường, and Toản Tu Phạm Phú Lâm. When the Vice Minister Phạm Hữu Dụng arrived, sent by Nguyễn Văn Tường to summon Hàm Nghi back to the capital, Tôn Thất Thuyết, divining his purpose, prevented Dụng from presenting his message. Many urged that the Imperial Carriage be taken directly to Tân Sở to plan the restoration, but Từ Dũ was adamant in demanding a return to the capital. Thuyết considered stopping at Tân Sở briefly but abandoned the plan, judging the situation too critical. While the emperor and his officials were dining, a disturbance broke out. The soldiers who had marched from Văn Xá had nothing to eat; after much agitation they received only a little unhusked rice to sustain themselves. Having spent two sleepless nights and marched seventy kilometers on foot, their morale collapsed, and many deserted in bitterness. Thuyết had to intervene personally to calm them. After two hours of reassurance, he issued an order permitting certain categories of troops to rest. Of the four hundred soldiers with the emperor at that time, two hundred fifty were natives of Quảng Trị or Quảng Bình; these were allowed to move about freely. Those who had served in the old citadel garrison, by contrast, were confined to quarters, their rice distributed to them, and ordered to be ready to march again before dawn the next day. Tôn Thất Thuyết instructed the provincial governor of Quảng Trị to draw 10,000 bushels of rice from the state granaries and to send most of the heavy artillery to Tân Sở. He assigned two hundred soldiers to guard the three Dowagers at Quảng Trị, as they refused to proceed to the war zone.

The decision to leave the Three Palaces at Quảng Trị has since been regarded as a grave oversight on Thuyết's part, for when the three Dowagers returned to the capital they became a political card in the hands of the French colonizers. By securing Từ Dũ, the French were able to implement their plan to enthrone a new emperor and establish a compliant court that would oppose Hàm Nghi. Without the Three Palaces and the imperial clan, the French could not so easily have staged their new scenario. As events showed, during Hàm Nghi's flight the French were in considerable disarray. In the words of the author of L'Empire d'Annam: "The General was master of the Citadel and the city of Huế — this no one could deny — but his authority did not extend beyond those narrow precincts. The truth is that after his victory he was beset by difficulties of every kind, for the conflict of that night of July 5, 1885 had utterly transformed the situation in Annam. The young emperor had left the capital, and in Huế there was no longer any government, any authority, anyone capable of executing the treaties we had signed with this country..."

As for Thuyết's decision to leave the Three Palaces behind at Quảng Trị, it was probably based on the calculation that women, the aged, and young children would seriously encumber the Imperial Procession, which needed to reach the war zone (Tân Sở) with all speed. A slow-moving procession, added to the shortage of provisions, would pose a mortal danger if the enemy caught up. He could only warn the Governor of Quảng Trị to disregard Tường henceforth. But the governor, finding himself unable to hold Quảng Trị, secretly wrote to Nguyễn Văn Tường arranging for the Dowagers' return to Huế. All officials, meanwhile, were ordered to proceed at once to Tân Sở. Some sought to evade the order — the district magistrate, the Vice Minister of Rites, and others. The Minister of Public Works and the Vice Minister of Justice, Tôn Thất Phan, asked to remain to serve the Three Palaces, but the three Dowagers refused them. Tôn Thất Phan then returned to Kim Long; Châu Đình Kế, Vice Minister of Public Works, went back to Văn Xá. On the 27th day of Ất Dậu, Tôn Thất Thuyết escorted the emperor to Tân Sở. Hàm Nghi entered to take leave of the Three Palaces. The officials and imperial clansmen likewise knelt to bid the emperor farewell. Everyone wept. Even Tôn Thất Thuyết, who had never hesitated to execute men, had to turn his face away at this heart-rending scene.

Before the emperor's departure from the temporary palace, a discussion took place. The Dowager wished to return to the capital and resume implementation of the Treaty of June 6, 1884. The imperial clan and a number of courtiers supported her. Thuyết declared: "To return to Huế is to put one's own neck into a prison whose jailer is the French army. To accept the 1884 Treaty is to deliver Vietnam to the enemy. Granted, peace is precious, but if we do not strive to restore what has been lost, we shall be remembered as those who abandoned the ancestral domain founded by the former emperors, and we shall bear this guilt before posterity."

The Dowager wept, saying: "The capital is where the ancestral temples are. If we return to Huế and die there, we shall at least see the spirits of the departed emperors in the realm of the Yellow Springs."

It should be recalled that after leaving the capital, on July 6 at their temporary stop in Văn Xá, Thuyết had drafted, by the emperor's authority, the Cần Vương edict calling upon scholars and people to rally to the righteous cause. This proclamation set forth the reasons that had forced the Southern Court into armed conflict with the French colonizers. Its language was fervent and stirring, its literary style concise and brilliant. Thuyết placed his own seal upon the document before distributing it throughout the country (Central, Southern, and Northern regions).

The text of Emperor Hàm Nghi's Cần Vương proclamation:

EDICT:

"Since ancient times, the conduct of war has never exceeded three options: defense, negotiation, and attack. With defense it is difficult to gauge one's strength; with negotiation the enemy grows ever more insatiable; with attack the moment may not yet be ripe. At this juncture, when the situation is beset by a thousand difficulties, a ruler must resort to extraordinary measures. The Thái Dương Emperor emerged in the land of Kỳ; Huyền Vũ passed through the land of Thục — such things have been common since ancient times.

"In recent years our country has encountered many complications. Though I am young and have succeeded to the throne only recently, I have never forgotten the principles of self-strengthening and sovereignty. The enemy grows daily more overbearing, causing affairs to fall into the utmost confusion. Lately they have increased their troops and ships, forcing upon the Court demands that cannot be accepted. The people within the capital also fear the danger of imminent collapse. The high ministers who conduct the affairs of state cannot fail to protect the altars of the nation. We have had to weigh two alternatives: to bow our heads and obey the enemy's commands, or to look steadfastly upon their treacherous designs and oppose them. Since the contest cannot be avoided, what is done today must aim at future advantage — this, too, is the logic of circumstance. Whoever shares this concern with me must already understand it. Understanding, he must contribute his strength, gnash his teeth, make his hair stand on end, and swear to exterminate the enemy to the last — who is there without such a heart? Moreover, the ministers who stand in the Court have but one principle: loyalty to the sovereign. Where righteousness lies, there they live and die. How did Hồ Diên and Triệu Thôi of the state of Tấn, or Quách Tử Nghi and Lý Quang Bật of the Táng dynasty, conduct themselves in former times?

"I am of meager talent and slight virtue, encountering a catastrophe I cannot withstand. The capital is under duress; the Imperial Carriage and the Three Palaces have had to depart far away — the fault is entirely mine. Truly my shame knows no bounds. Yet the bonds of human obligation bind us together; all officials and ministers, high and low, will surely not abandon me. Let the wise offer their counsel, the brave their strength, the wealthy their resources. Compatriots and fellow countrymen must not shrink from danger. To relieve the crisis, to withstand the onslaught, to open a path through hardship, to aid in desperate straits — none of this must be grudged in heart or effort, in the hope of turning chaos into order, of ending the wandering and restoring peace, of recovering the rivers and mountains and borders. This opportunity is at hand; the fortune of the sovereign is the fortune of the people. To share the worries and the thoughts of the nation — is this not excellent? But if the fear of death outweighs the love of the sovereign, if concern for one's family exceeds concern for the country, if officials find excuses to withdraw, if soldiers desert and flee, if the people do not value righteousness and fail to assist the public cause, if scholars abandon the light for the darkness, then such men are no better than living superfluously in the world — wearing the robes of office while acting as beasts of burden. The Court will have its fitting punishment; let there be no regret hereafter! Let all review this carefully and obey!

"Respect this!"

Hàm Nghi đệ nhị niên, 2nd day of the 6th month (Ất Dậu).

In response to Hàm Nghi's appeal, even Cao Bằng — the remotest mountainous frontier of the North — sent word of support in the following proclamation:

"From the Northern Military Command, concerning the following matter:

"Having previously received the Edict setting forth the nation's manifold calamities: all officials and people, whether military or civilian, who harbor hatred for the enemy of the nation, must proceed to Cam Lộ to serve the Imperial Carriage, or rise in righteous insurrection in their localities to destroy the national enemy, to support the foundation of the state, each doing according to his heart and ability. The Court will have its regulations for determining merit and granting rewards. Now we urgently extract the relevant passages from the Edict so that all may know and respond immediately."

Hàm Nghi đệ nhị niên, 15th day of the 7th month.
Copied at Cao Bằng upon receipt of the provincial command.

It should also be noted that in the second edict, dated the 2nd day of the 6th month of Ất Dậu, Hàm Nghi restored the former rank of Hoàng Tá Viêm. (Under French pressure, the Southern Court had stripped Hoàng of all titles and ranks after the Giáp Thân Treaty, even though he had remained in Bắc Kỳ to resist the French.) He was now reappointed Tiết Chế Quân Vụ Đại Thần (Imperial Commissioner for Military Affairs) in Bắc Kỳ, with the same authority as before. His associates in the anti-French faction were similarly treated: Nguyễn Đình Nhuận, former Tổng Đốc of Sơn-Hưng, was appointed Hiệp Thống Quân Vụ Đại Thần and promoted to Tuần Phủ of Lạng Binh; Lã Xuân Oai was made Tổng Thống; Nguyễn Văn Như, Nguyễn Văn Giáp, Nguyễn Cao, Nguyễn Thiện, Vũ Giác, Ngô Quang Huy, and Hoàng Đình Kính were all reinstated and promoted.

Beyond promulgating the Cần Vương edict throughout the country, Hàm Nghi also dispatched Nguyễn Quang Bích to Beijing to seek aid.[3] The immediate result of the Cần Vương proclamation was that Vietnamese rose up on all sides. Nguyễn Đình Chiểu, the renowned scholar of the South, was roused to the highest enthusiasm on this occasion. Across all three regions, French posts came under attack, and Vietnamese collaborators were assassinated. De Courcy was panic-stricken. Though he had thirty thousand troops at his disposal, he urgently requested reinforcements from Paris, convinced that in such a tense situation his own considerable forces could not cope with every difficulty. He even proposed to the French government that the northern garrisons be withdrawn and all forces concentrated in Central Vietnam, now that the fires in Huế had barely been extinguished. In short, he wanted to secure his hold on Trung Kỳ — and to do so he believed he must evacuate all French troops from the Hồng Lam region; otherwise, failing to hold the Center and losing the North as well, he would have to bear the full responsibility.[4] This reveals how utterly the French General's spirit had been crushed by the rising tide of Vietnamese national struggle. Just as the French were at their most perplexed, however, Tường surrendered and the Three Palaces returned to the capital — a stroke that extricated the French from a desperate position.

(It is worth noting that the proclamation Tường was forced to sign, calling for an end to national resistance, contained a time limit: within twelve days from July 9, all who had joined the resistance were to surrender. Moreover, in the terms of that same proclamation, the resistance was branded as rebellion. The French also issued a notice dissolving the Vietnamese army under Thuyết's command. Thus the French exploited Tường to the fullest, and whether he wished it or not, Tường and the Three Palaces had from that point become instruments of the Empire.)

WHAT WERE THUYẾT'S ACTIVITIES AT TÂN SỞ?

Tân Sở was a fortress built on a high plateau, with Laos to the west and the barren sandy expanses of Quảng Trị province to the east. It was created with the specific purpose of resisting the French. Built two years before the great catastrophe of the night of July 5, 1885, by the very man who then led Vietnam's resistance — who understood that sooner or later the French colonizers would take the entire country, that to refuse to fight meant certain loss, while to fight offered at least some hope — either of salvaging something or of leaving a legacy that would preserve the nation's faith in itself.

Tân Sở also served as a military capital, a place of refuge for the Court should the capital fall. Thuyết and his men had chosen a site midway along the Lao Bảo–Mai Linh road, about ninety kilometers south of Cam Lộ district town, with the design of retreating into Laos via the highland route if necessary, or of finding a way to Hà Tĩnh and then on to the North. Several routes led to Tân Sở, but besides the main road there was a little-known path — the secret route of the resistance — along which granaries of rice lay hidden in the forest, under careful guard. This rice, in considerable quantity, had been brought by the northern provinces to Cửa Việt, from there to Cam Lộ, and then into Tân Sở. The fortress itself was constructed with three concentric earthen ramparts planted with bamboo; four openings served as gates (left, right, front, back). Its area was twenty-three hectares, rectangular in shape, 548 meters in length and 418 meters in width, containing granaries and barracks. Within the inner precinct, measuring 165 meters by 100 meters, stood several thatched buildings used as the temporary imperial palace. The surrounding mountains and dense forests gave Tân Sở considerable military and strategic advantage. Two years earlier Thuyết had mobilized up to two thousand laborers to dig trenches and build earthworks. Three months before the surprise attack on the French in Huế, he had already arranged for the transport of materials, weapons, rice, and money. When Lemaire was still Resident-General in Huế and heard that the Southern Court was shipping arms and funds to Tân Sở, he questioned Tường about it. Tường feigned surprise, denied everything, and even offered to take the French Resident to inspect Tân Sở and Cam Lộ. Later, Tường was forced to confess to de Champeaux that since the beginning of June 1885, the silver sent to the new citadel had amounted to 300,000 taels, and that a few days before the capital fell (July 5), Thuyết had wanted to transfer the remainder — some 700,000 taels — from the treasury, but Tường had refused. Consequently, when the French broke into the citadel they still found hundreds of chests of silver ingots to ship to France. In the construction of Tân Sở, Nguyễn Văn Tường had played a significant role. Today, visitors to the site recognize that both Tường and Thuyết were not without military understanding.

In mid-July 1885, reinforcements began to arrive in small scattered groups. The Án Sát of Quảng Trị, Tôn Thất Nam, brought nearly two hundred raw recruits hastily conscripted to Tân Sở. Võ Trọng Bình, retired in Quảng Bình, also contributed a similar number recruited locally, though he dared not come to pay homage to the emperor despite urgent summons from both the sovereign and Thuyết. In total, the combined force of soldiers and laborers at Tân Sở at that time did not exceed a thousand men.

Adversity reveals the true character of men. Phạm Thận Duật, Minister of Rites, had secretly sided with Nguyễn Văn Tường and leaked important intelligence about Tân Sở. Tuần Vũ Trương Quang Đản incited his brother Trương Đăng Đệ and Đô Thống Hồ Hiền to abandon the resistance and plot to return Hàm Nghi to the capital. These two urged Thuyết to allow them to take the emperor back to Huế to reassure the people; Thuyết threatened to execute anyone who entertained thoughts of surrendering to the French. This episode reveals how many court officials were losing their nerve, dreading a life of hardship and privation with no future in sight. The missionary A. Delvaux further reports that Tường sent a military officer to assassinate Thuyết, but that four or five attempts failed through extraordinary fortune.[5]

During this period, Thuyết dispatched a deputy commander posthaste to Laos to arrange the supply of rice and water for six outposts scattered along the highland route. Shortly afterward, a Đề đốc escorted Thuyết's senior wife, a concubine, and several young children to Nghệ An by the same mountain road.

On July 19, 1885, Thuyết brought the emperor to Tân Sở, then conducted him to the village of Điềm in Bảng Sơn (outside the fortress), two kilometers to the north. The emperor stayed at Bảng Sơn about ten days; afterward the Procession proceeded to Cam Lộ (on the Quảng Bình border) one morning. Thuyết intended to rely on the northern provinces for matériel and talent in resisting the French, for in his eyes the North still possessed rice and a populace enthusiastically patriotic. In fact, Hàm Nghi remained at Tân Sở only four or five days.

Thuyết abandoned Tân Sở because upon arriving there he realized that the Cam Lộ region had numerous disadvantages: it was not densely populated or wealthy, making recruitment of soldiers difficult. Moreover, if the enemy occupied Cam Lộ, Tân Sở would become a sack with its mouth tied shut; the routes to the sea, to Laos, to the South, and to the North would all be obstructed. The supply of provisions and weapons would then be cut off, compounding the danger. In terms of defensive strength, although Tân Sở had three concentric ramparts, warehouses, and barracks equipped with artillery, it was still not as strong as the Huế citadel. This consideration led him to take the emperor to Nghệ Tĩnh, a region that could serve as a more durable center for the resistance. About ten days later, Thuyết ordered the army to advance toward Quảng Bình. The Procession halted there to eat and drink; crossing the Bến Đuối River also cost considerable time. That afternoon, after covering thirteen or fourteen kilometers beyond Bến Đuối, Hàm Nghi rested at An Hương, north of Cồn Tiên. The escort troops had only five days' rations and had to scatter among the villages to forage for food. Wherever he went, Thuyet imposed martial law: civilians were forbidden to go out on the roads so that friend and foe could be distinguished; anyone violating this order was beaten; any household that allowed dogs to bark at night, disturbing the villages and revealing the secret movement of troops, was punishable by death.

On July 20, the Procession reached Thủy Ba at 10 a.m., then arrived at the Bảo Đài ferry landing by 3 or 4 p.m. Here news came that a quantity of gold and silver had been stolen from the rear guard by porters who had conspired together and then deserted. When the Procession reached Lai Cách, a spy from the North brought word that French troops had already arrived at Đồng Hới (Quảng Bình) at 2 a.m. the previous day, and that the French flotilla had landed troops in that province, consisting of six gunboats, evidently intending to block Hàm Nghi's advance into the Thanh-Nghệ-Tĩnh region, or into the North. Đồng Hới was taken without a shot; our troops had already fled before the French arrived.

The Imperial Procession immediately fell back, reaching Thủy Ba Thượng by nightfall. The local garrison troops in Quảng Bình deserted in large numbers.

HÀM NGHI CROSSES THE LAO FRONTIER

Unable to proceed north because the Quảng Bình road had been blocked by the French, on July 21 and 22 the Procession reversed course along the old road via Bái Trời and Cam Lộ. The following day (July 22), the Procession was again at Tân Sở. Hàm Nghi, unaccustomed to hardship and privation, was utterly exhausted. The escort now numbered fewer than three hundred men. On that same day, the District Magistrate of Cam Lộ, together with Thuyết's brother Tôn Thất Lệ, brought 140 chests of the emperor's silver to deposit at the Ngữu Cước customs station, leaving a portion at Tân Sở. It later became known that these 140 chests had been consigned to Võ Trọng Bình, the former Governor-General of Nam Định who was retired in Quảng Bình. He dared not keep them for fear of disaster, so he handed the entire amount over to the French.

On July 25, Trương Đăng Đệ arrived at Quảng Trị to inventory the troops of the Imperial Procession. He found them to consist of: 49 imperial guards, 200 hired soldiers from Hà Tĩnh, 47 Phấn Nghĩa (loyalist volunteer) troops, and 60 Thần Cơ artillerymen — 356 men in total. At Tân Sở, word arrived that a French company was advancing on Cam Lộ. Thuyết hastily abandoned Tân Sở, taking the Mai Lĩnh road across the Laotian border, planning to find another route to the North. Much gold, silver, and treasure was left behind. This region was densely forested, with rugged mountains — there had never been proper roads, only a few narrow tracks used by the Mường or Lao people who occasionally passed through. Beyond the forests, the travelers encountered swift rapids foaming white with spray, equally perilous. Sometimes the Procession had to stay an entire day to build rafts for crossing. On other days they camped in the forest to rest. The damp heat of rotting leaves, steaming under the sun, brought on paralysis, forcing many to be left behind in wayside huts. The dead were given only the most cursory burial — a truly wretched scene. The pursuing French vied with one another in looting. The people of Mai Lộ, Bang Mai, and Đan Sơn villages came out to block the enemy so that the emperor could escape. These villages were later burned to the ground. When the Procession left Tân Sở, Phạm Thận Duật, Ngô Tất Ninh, and Biện Lý Thể slipped away by a different route to reach Hà Tĩnh, but were captured by the District Magistrate of Triệu Phong and sent back to Huế. By this time the officials of the Southern Court had openly sided with the French; each vied eagerly to render service to their new masters. On July 24, 1885, the French ordered Trương Quang Đản, Tuần Vũ of Quảng Trị, to lead three hundred troops from Huế, divided into two columns, in pursuit of Tôn Thất Thuyết. One column marched directly on Tân Sở; the other rushed toward Mai Lĩnh and Ngữu Cước. At 3 p.m. on July 25, the Phó Lãnh Binh of Quảng Trị encountered Tôn Thất Lệ's party at Ngữu Cước, seized thirty-five chests of silver, and brought them back to Quảng Trị. The same Tuần Vũ contributed a comparable force under the command of Tả Quân Lượng to capture Hàm Nghi. These troops, besides rifles and spears, also carried several small cannon. According to A. Delvaux's history, Tả Quân Lượng reached Vụng Kho on July 29 just as the Phó Lãnh Binh's troops arrived at Hương Hóa district. The two forces combined to drive off the Imperial Procession that very night at the Lao border. The French had instructed these Vietnamese collaborationist troops to preserve the emperor's safety, fearing that in extremity Thuyết might kill Hàm Nghi to deprive the French of their advantage. Informed that the enemy was close on his heels, Thuyết crossed into Bạng Câu that very evening, arriving early on July 30. Bạng Câu was a Mường village thirty kilometers from the Lao frontier. The two sides met. Lượng ordered his men to fire at Thuyết, but Thuyết leaped from his horse and ran to stand before the emperor's hammock, shouting: "If you have any courage, shoot at me!" The imperial troops, though exhausted, prepared to resist. Lượng's force, being the weaker, withdrew; several were killed in the clash on the loyalist side, and the Procession hurriedly resumed its march.

Because the road had been prepared in advance, the Procession reached the Mã Hạc region, known as Cửu Châu, on July 28. Hàm Nghi was now riding an elephant in his flight. Trương Quang Đản had nearly caught up, but he sent Trương Đăng Đệ by a shortcut to cut Thuyết off at Cửu Châu. The difficult terrain and bad weather, however, had left nearly all his men stricken with malaria, forcing Đệ to fall back to Tân Sở, and the Procession escaped.

By this time Phạm Thận Duật had already surrendered to the French. He learned that at Cửu Châu, Thuyết had managed to purchase sufficient provisions and hire porters; a landowner who refused to sell rice to the resistance was shot. Thuyết intended to head for Bản Ta Ba Chon, a locality at the southern extremity of Cammon [Khammouane], the administrative seat of Thakhek province to the north, in Savannakhet Province, Laos.[6] From there he would proceed to Quy Hợp,[7] a village extremely difficult of access in Cam Cúc district near Sơn Phòng (Hà Tĩnh), where seven or eight hundred of his loyalist troops were waiting. He also had hopes of a certain local chief named Cai Mân, a native of Thanh Hóa, who had recruited a thousand Chinese soldiers. These were being trained at Ngọc Lâu village near Ninh Bình. Before this, Thuyết had repeatedly ordered the authorities in Thanh Hóa and Hà Tĩnh to send five hundred troops to meet the emperor. On this occasion, he announced to the people that the attack on the capital on the night of July 5, 1885, had been provoked by the French.

It should be recalled that at this time the Mường people beyond the Trường Sơn range received and protected the emperor with great care. When the Procession reached the Quy Hợp Pass area, Nguyễn Chánh, Governor-General of Nghệ An and concurrently Commander of the Mountain Defense of Hà Tĩnh, arrived with five hundred troops to pay homage. The imperial escort now consisted of only a handful of attendants. In this period, Phan Đình Phùng and many of his comrades were actively operating in Nghệ An. In Hà Tĩnh, Lê Ninh, son of the Bố Chánh Lê Kiên of La Sơn, led the scholars and commoners to seize the provincial capital, killing Lê Đại and capturing Trịnh Văn Báu — both collaborationist officials who had opposed the Cần Vương forces. It is noteworthy that the patriotic movement here surged with extraordinary force; personal rivalries, ethnic divisions among Kinh, highlanders, and all religious conflicts dissolved instantly as everyone united to fight the enemy. Even the pro-Lê and pro-Nguyễn factions coalesced into unified action.

As for Nguyễn Văn Tường in Huế, he naturally had to work actively for the French at this critical moment to save his own head. He wrote to Nguyễn Chánh, saying that he had recommended to the French that Chánh be appointed Minister of Justice, but that Chánh must first deliver Emperor Hàm Nghi to the French and rigorously obey all directives from the new court.

CHAPTER V

THE CAN VUONG (AID THE KING) MOVEMENT AFTER KING HAM NGHI'S EDICT

KING HAM NGHI'S ACTIVITIES IN HA TINH

As we have seen, blocked at Quang Binh, Ton That Thuyet was forced to take the Ai Lao (Laotian) route to bring the king north into Bac Ky, intending to establish additional operational bases. The royal procession traveled along the western side of the Truong Son (Annamite Cordillera) range and eventually reached Cửu Châu (Savannakhet) in the territory of Ai Lao. The royal procession paused here for three days, utterly exhausted. The climate in Ai Lao at that time was as oppressive as it was in Trung Ky (Central Vietnam). The Lao lands were then in the midst of the rainy season — a ceaseless, pounding downpour that continued day and night — while back in Vietnam the season was one of scorching, skin-peeling heat. So abundant were the rains that rivers overflowed their banks, flooding fields and spawning disease, fevers above all. King Ham Nghi, unaccustomed to such exposure and hardship, fell ill and had to be carried in a hammock borne by his soldiers. Behind him came four or five more hammocks carrying the mandarins, ten horses, three elephants, fifty porters loaded with supplies, two hundred soldiers, and attendant officials of every rank.

The Chau Muong (Lao district chief) of Mahaxay, a man named Tong-Hom, received secret word that the king was approaching and hastily dispatched five of his men to Ban Ta Pa Tion to meet the royal procession, supplying food, provisions, and shelter along the route the king would travel. The king, touched by the district chief's kindness, presented him with four horses and ten rifles. But after the rains ended, word of this reached the Siamese government, and Tong-Hom was summoned to Vọng Các (Bangkok) to pay a fine of 1,600 ticaux (then worth 2,400 quan) and ordered to return the gifts he had received — for he had failed to request permission from the royal court (let us recall that at this time Ai Lao was still subject to the court of Vọng Các) (1).

(1) The Kingdom of Siam (Tiêm La) had long prohibited the sale of elephants abroad.

According to Captain Gosselin, King Ham Nghi's journey passed through the following places:

"The first day, that is, the 5th or 6th of August 1885, the royal procession halted at Ban Ta Pa Tion on the Nam Pha Nang River, a tributary of the Se Bang Fai. The procession slept here.

The second day the king slept at Ban Na Poung.

The third and fourth days the king slept at Ban Na Sen.

The fifth day the king slept at Ban Na Pong.

The sixth day the king slept at Ban Soi.

The seventh day the king slept at Ban Sok.

The eighth day the king slept at Ban Tha Chou.

The ninth day the king slept at Ban Bo, then crossed Mount Phu Ak. The tenth day the king slept on the mountain itself.

The eleventh day the king slept in the forest beside the Nam An River.

The twelfth day (approximately 18 August 1885) the king slept at Ban Tong."

When stopping in the Lao villages, only the king and his mandarins had shelter from rain and sun. The common soldiers, by contrast, slept wherever they found themselves, sprawled haphazardly beneath the stilt-houses, their clothes perpetually wet and unable to dry, subsisting on meager rations of husked rice or maize. At times darkness fell while they were still traveling through the forest roads, with no village or hamlet in sight, and the soldiers had to cut down trees and gather leaves to throw up makeshift shelters for the king against the dew and the wind. When they encountered stretches of muddy, treacherous terrain, they would have the elephants probe the mire with their trunks to gauge the depth, then construct rafts that the elephants would drag, carrying the entire column across. The elephant was the invaluable asset of this phase, indispensable for crossing rivers that sometimes reached depths of over two meters. For this reason, wherever Ton That Thuyet learned of elephants, he would press to buy them — but the court of Vọng Các had strictly forbidden the sale of elephants abroad without special government permission, making such purchases exceedingly difficult and the prices demanded exorbitant.

Because so many soldiers and porters succumbed to malaria and dysentery, many deserted and many died, despite the efforts of physicians and the careful attention they gave one another. The pitiful number of those who perished on this desperate road through the deep mountains and remote valleys included men of every rank — from mandarin to common soldier — and not a single burial garment was available to cover their remains. They were hastily buried in shallow graves, and the survivors pressed on, fleeing the claws of the enemy. When the survivors later returned to the central region, their number did not exceed forty.

Crossing the Quy Hop Pass (the Quy Hop route being the sole thoroughfare from Ai Lao into Ha Tinh) at the end of the 7th lunar month (that is, the end of August by the Western calendar), King Ham Nghi reached Ham Thao, a Muong territory lying seven days' march from the Son Phong (Mountain Pacification headquarters) of Ha Tinh. The Mountain Pacification official Nguyen Chanh dispatched Captain Cao Dat with a number of soldiers to receive the king, and thus the royal procession was greatly reinforced once more (1). Ton That Thuyet's intention was to make this region the base for the restoration. The provincial mandarins reported the news to Hue, and the Three Dowager Empresses (Tam Cung) were overjoyed; they appointed Ton That Phan as Imperial Commissioner (Khâm-sai) to lead 350 soldiers of Ha Tinh province and a Military Commander (Lãnh-binh) to escort the king. By the 5th of September, King Ham Nghi had somewhat recovered from his exhaustion, but here he received the news that the French had deposed him and raised his elder brother, Me Trieu, to the throne. King Dong Khanh and Empress Dowager Tu Du had issued a combined edict (Dụ) that both denounced him and once again called for an end to the Resistance (Kháng-chiến). At the Son Phong headquarters of Ha Tinh, the mandarins and local notables, hearing of the king's arrival, came flocking to pay court in great numbers. Ton That Thuyet set about reorganizing many matters: recruiting righteous volunteer soldiers (nghĩa-dũng), establishing relay stations and fortifications at strategic points, and appointing officials. He made the former Supernumerary Gentleman (Viên-ngoại-lang) Phan Quang Cu the Finance Commissioner (Bố-chánh) of Ha Tinh; the former Surveillance Commissioner (Án-sát) Huynh Xuan Phong the Mountain Pacification Commissioner (Sơn-phòng-sứ); the former Prefect (Tri-phủ) Nguy Khac Kieu as his deputy; the former Prefect Phan Khac Hoa as Surveillance Commissioner of Ha Tinh; the former Chief Censor (Đô-ngự-sử) Phan Dinh Phung as Advisor (Tán-lý); and Phan Trong Muu as Mountain Pacification Assistant (Sơn-Phòng Tham-biện). But these plans and appointments had only just begun to take effect when word arrived that French forces were approaching in pursuit. At this critical moment Ton That Thuyet drafted a second Can Vuong (Aid the King) edict (Chiếu Cần-vương), in King Ham Nghi's name, both calling the entire nation to resistance once more and answering the accusatory edict of King Dong Khanh. This edict, dated the 11th day of the 8th lunar month (that is, the 20th of September 1885), in the second year of Ham Nghi's reign, was promulgated immediately after the French had conducted the ceremony elevating King Dong Khanh to the throne. Its essential content was as follows:

"France and our country have signed treaties of peace for several decades now. At first we ceded to France the three eastern provinces of Nam Ky (Cochinchina); two years later they seized the remaining three western provinces — yet their greed remained unsatisfied. They then schemed to establish a Legation (Sứ-quán) at Hue and a Resident-ship (Lãnh-sự) in Bac Ky, ceaselessly pressuring us to modify this provision and alter that one, time and again without end. Finally they resolved to seize the territory of Bac Ky, the priceless inheritance bequeathed to us by our ancestors. Then, upon the occasion of the passing of King Duc Tong (Tu Duc), French forces marched in to attack the Thuan An sea gate, burning even the royal investiture seal that the Emperor of China had bestowed upon our king. They demanded to station troops within the Imperial Citadel, trained their heavy cannon on our vital positions, and openly intimidated the Court until no official or subject could endure it further.

"In the 5th lunar month just past, the French brought more than ten thousand men to force us to surrender the Imperial City and submit to their commands. The Privy Council (Cơ-mật-viện) was therefore compelled to launch an attack upon the capital. If we were victorious, Nguyen Van Tuong would come out to receive Us — We having temporarily withdrawn to Nghe An — while Ton That Thuyet remained in the Capital to resist the French and annihilate the Catholic converts who had colluded with the enemy against the Court. If we were defeated, We would, together with the court officials, proceed north to plan the restoration.

"The attack upon the French was launched on the 3rd of the 5th lunar month. By noon of the following day it was over. The enemy dead lay so thick that they could not gather their corpses in time; many perished. It was the work of heaven, inescapable.

"At that very moment, Nguyen Van Tuong followed Us from the capital. Unexpectedly, he changed his allegiance and fled into a church. Ton That Thuyet never left Our side.

"We reached Quang Tri, and the French sent Tuong to summon Us back, offering to return the Imperial City. This was a trick, a deceit. From now on Tuong has followed the enemy, deceiving the people, plotting to seize Us and deliver Us to the French for his own wealth and advancement — a crime graver than heaven itself. Tuong has even forged letters from the Empress Dowager to entice Us to return, but the Empress Dowager is now in French hands; Tuong could have her write anything he pleased. Yet according to the late Emperor's testamentary edict, women shall not干预 matters of state. Therefore, if anyone capture that forged edict, let them burn it and speak no more of it — that is best.

"At present, We together with Ton That Thuyet have arrived at Au Son (Huong Khe). Let all officials come forth to join in the work of aiding the king (cần-vương)." (1)

The above edict of King Ham Nghi was an inevitable necessity — a correction and response to the accusations and calumnies of the pro-French Southern Court (Nam-triều), which had fully exploited King Dong Khanh and Empress Dowager Tu Du for their purposes.

On the 17th of October 1885, the Vice-Viceroy (Kinh-lược phó-sứ) of Bac Ky, Nguyen Trong Hop, likewise issued a proclamation to the officials and people, harmonizing with the edicts of Empress Dowager Tu Du and King Dong Khanh:

"Despite the edict (Dụ) of His Majesty the Present Emperor and of Her Majesty the Empress Dowager having been promulgated, Your Officer still observes that many people persist in heeding the deceptions of the rebels (1). In recent years and months, our Court has had to endure many upheavals. A number of high ministers have usurped power, killing one king and installing another — truly an outrage against heaven and earth. They have also exiled many members of the Imperial Clan and stained their hands with countless other grave crimes. King Ham Nghi, upon examination, had no rightful claim to the throne. His elevation was the doing of the two Regents (Phụ-chánh), who sought only to satisfy their own ambitions, falsely claiming that this was the will of King Kien Phuc. Where is the evidence?

"The catastrophe that befell the Capital was entirely the work of Ton That Thuyet and Nguyen Van Tuong. At first they were of one mind and one purpose, allied together; later they quarreled, causing the ancestral temples to totter and the Court to be imperiled — no different from a thread before a blade. Now Heaven in its benevolence has granted protection, seeing the Nation safely through its trials. The stormy waves have calmed; the winds have subsided, etc."

It goes without saying that all such edicts and proclamations were the work of the French hirelings, decorated with flowery phrases according to the will of their new masters and broadcast for the purpose of shattering the prestige of the Resistance. Empress Dowager Tu Du and King Dong Khanh alike were, at that time, nothing more nor less than puppets of the Imperial power. Being in French hands, they could of course do no other. But could the prestige of a puppet king and a puppet dowager truly diminish the standing of the resistance party? No! Had the French and their lackeys succeeded in this, the Can Vuong Movement could never have endured for more than two decades longer. To put it differently, the French did not diminish the authority of King Ham Nghi and Ton That Thuyet; on the contrary, they only enhanced the stature of the resistance leaders. Thus when the Can Vuong edict reached every stratum of our countrymen, the wave of patriotism and hatred for the colonialists blazed up like fire. Throughout the entire nation, the notables zealously agitated against the French and against the Catholic religion under the slogan "Pacify the West, Kill the Leftists" (Bình Tây sát Tả). The voices of the patriots, from the South to the North, drowned out the calls for peace and order from the political gangsters on the banks of the Perfume River. In Thua Thien specifically, Nguyen Van Tuong ordered intimidation and terrorization of the population, and the anti-French movement was somewhat dampened there. But from Binh Thuan northward, every place was convulsed by disturbances. The blood of the Lương-Giáo (Catholic vs. non-Catholic) conflict inundated villages everywhere, in scenes of the utmost tragedy. And simultaneously with all this, in some places righteous volunteer forces were being recruited for the resistance; in others, brigands rose up to plunder, fishing in troubled waters; in still others, officials were seized and killed; and elsewhere, scholar-officials (sĩ-phu) flocked to the Can Vuong banner. The situation was chaotic, tangled beyond description.

THE UPRISINGS IN THE
SOUTHERN CENTRAL REGION

Readers will surely not forget that from the time of the Giap Tuat Treaty (1874) onward, the Can Vuong and Van Than (Literati) movements had already been launched. In Nghe An, the Tú tài (Bachelor) Tran Tan and Dang Nhu Mai assembled the scholars and examination graduates of the locality, circulated proclamations to "Pacify the West and Kill the Leftists," and banded together to raze many Catholic villages. Unable to withstand French suppression, they allied with a group of righteous soldiers (nghĩa-sĩ) who had armed forces at their command — men such as Tran Quang Can, Truong Quang Thu, and Nguyen Huy Dien — and attacked the citadel of Ha Tinh, laying siege to Dien Chau district. Eventually the Court was forced to send Nguyen Van Tuong as Imperial Commissioner (Khâm-sai) and Le Ba Than as Commander-in-Chief (Tổng-thống) to suppress the uprising. This rebellion began in the first lunar month and was not crushed until the sixth month of that year. The extermination of the anti-French Van Than was carried out because Tu Duc had just signed the treaty with France and dared not tolerate any anti-French activities — a situation we have already observed in Nam Ky more than ten years earlier.

The Van Than uprising in Nghe Tinh was temporarily pacified, but the popular movement continued underground. This was especially true once the Van Than saw that the anti-French faction had taken control of the Southern Court, and that the anti-French stance of Ton That Thuyet's party had become unmistakably clear after the French attack on the Thuan An sea gate. They then established contact to coordinate their response with King Ham Nghi's call after the fall of the capital — and from that point on, a new phase began.

In the 6th lunar month of the year At Dau (1885), the French arrived to station forces at Dong Hoi. In the 7th month they occupied the citadel of Nghe An; in the 8th month they took Quang Tri and Binh Dinh. It appears that the French did not maintain permanent posts in Quang Tri, knowing by then that King Ham Nghi had already left for Quang Binh; stationing troops there would have been pointless and a waste of manpower. A company of Zouaves (the troops in baggy trousers) was dispatched to conduct mobile operations across both Quang Nam and Quang Ngai provinces.

In the 7th month of the year Binh Tuat (1886), the scholars and notables of Binh Dinh province also rose up, but Nguyen Than scattered them.

In the 10th and 11th months of that year, the Chief Mountain Pacification Officer (Chánh-sứ Sơn-phòng) Tran Van Du founded the Righteous Society (Nghĩa-hội) and led his righteous army (nghĩa-quân) to threaten the provincial capital. The Provincial Governor (Tuần-Vũ) Nguyen Ngoan, the Finance Commissioner (Bố-chánh) Bui Tan Quang, and the Surveillance Commissioner (Án-sát) Ha Thuc Quan all fled. Later, French forces recaptured the position, destroyed the Trung Loc fort and the Son Phong headquarters. Tran Van Du's Righteous Society disintegrated. Du set out for the capital to surrender, but as soon as he reached the provincial seat, the French arrested him and executed him on the spot.

In the 2nd month of the year Binh Tuat (1886), King Dong Khanh dispatched Phan Thanh Liem (son of Phan Thanh Gian) as Imperial Commissioner to go forth and pacify the notables by persuasive edicts. In the 5th month, Phan Thanh Liem was appointed Governor-General (Tổng-đốc) of the two provinces of Thuan and Khanh. Surveillance Commissioner Nguyen Dinh Van was made Finance Commissioner of Binh Thuan; the former Provincial Governor of Binh Thuan, Vo Doan Tuan, became Finance Commissioner of Khanh Hoa; the Censor (Ngự-sử) Nguyen Van Chat became Surveillance Commissioner of Khanh Hoa, etc. All subordinate posts were left to the discretion of the provincial heads to fill.

In Quang Nam at this time the righteous army was weakening day by day. In the 6th month, Nguyen Hieu suffered a major defeat at An Lam Mountain. Many old military commanders fell in battle; food supplies, funds, and weapons were mostly confiscated by the enemy. A few months later Nguyen Than captured Nguyen Hieu, put him in a cage, and sent him to the Capital. With the head severed, the snake was dead — the Quang Nam Resistance at that moment was extinguished. The notables gradually came forth to surrender.

In the 3rd month of the following year (Dinh Hoi — 1887), the French reinforced the forces of the Imperial Commissioner Phan and the Pacification and Recruitment Commissioner (Chiêu-thảo-sứ) for Nghia and Dinh, Nguyen Than, with two companies of European soldiers and one company of native auxiliaries (lính tập), each company consisting of 200 men, in order to bring the pacification to a swift conclusion.

By the 7th month of Dinh Hoi, from Quang Nam south to Binh Thuan, relative security and order had been restored. The Southern Court therefore sent Nguyen Thuat as Left Circuit (Tả-trực kỳ) Pacification and Settlement High Commissioner to instruct the populations of the two aforementioned provinces.

The task of pacification from Quang Binh northward into Bac Ky was far more difficult for the French colonialists. King Ham Nghi continued to move back and forth from Quang Binh to Nghe Tinh. The Can Vuong party in this region was also stronger, further driving forward the uprisings of the scholars and populace in Quang Nam and Quang Ngai. In the 5th month, the Southern Court, seeing that its conciliatory measures were ineffective, reached an accord with the French to employ more forceful methods against the Righteous Army. For this purpose, Nguyen Than received the appointment of Nghia-Dinh Pacification and Recruitment Commissioner, as we have already noted. After three months, Nguyen Than, employing every kind of atrocity — killing and seizing the populace without sparing even children or the elderly — shattered countless families, and the righteous army lost all popular support. By the end of the 7th month of that year, Nguyen Than had succeeded. In the provinces of Binh Thuan, Khanh Hoa, and Phu Yen, Tran Ba Loc, Major De Lorme, and Administrator Aymonier were also dispatched by the Governor of Nam Ky to conduct pacification. These forces established a broad and tightly coordinated communication network, easily supplying one another.

Tran Ba Loc employed the most barbaric methods, which disgusted even his French superior officers (see Việt-sử Tân-biên, Volume V). But inwardly the French were delighted that their strategy of "setting a thief to catch a thief" had proved immensely effective. In the intercalary 4th month of the year Binh Dinh Hoi, Tran Ba Loc captured the Provincial Graduate (Cử-nhân) Mai Xuan Thuong and Bui Dien — the Garrison Commander (Thống Trấn) and Deputy General (Phó-tướng) of the righteous army — along with eleven former military commanders, and had them all put to death in order to terrorize the uprising in that region.

In Phu Yen, the righteous army rose up and seized the provincial capital, capturing the Finance Commissioner Pham Nhu Xuong. The Surveillance Commissioner Huynh Con and the Military Commander (Lãnh-binh) Nguyen Van Hanh escaped. French forces brought troops and ships and quickly recaptured the province. The Southern Court then dispatched the Left Assistant of the Ministry of Finance (Tả-lý bộ Hộ), Luong Xuan Huyen, and the Reader of the Inner Cabinet (Thị-độc Nội-các), Cao De, to restore order and the administrative apparatus. Huyen assumed the post of Finance Commissioner, and De that of Surveillance Commissioner.

The four southern provinces having been pacified, Tran Ba Loc fined the local notables 180,000 đồng to defray military expenses. In the 4th month, King Dong Khanh reorganized the administrative machinery of the central region. Ton That Ba was appointed Finance Commissioner; the former Surveillance Commissioner Dinh Duy Tan retained his former post. As for the prefectures, districts, and various government offices, these men were permitted to recruit personnel at their own discretion.

During this period, the revolutionaries Phan Dinh Phung, Tong Duy Tan, and Cao Dien likewise established Huong Khe and Hung Linh as their bases for anti-enemy operations from 1885 to 1895.

We shall recount in detail some of the more famous engagements in later chapters, to serve as exemplars of the heroic struggle of our Vietnamese race. Even our adversaries themselves have at times expressed admiration for these acts of resistance.

Regarding the uprisings in the Southern Central region, we greatly regret the lack of sufficient historical materials. Because the National History was compiled under the Nguyen dynasty's purview, the recording of Can Vuong affairs is exceedingly cursory. In truth, from Lang Son to Ha Tien, scholars and people alike rose up to answer King Ham Nghi's call; only the intensity varied from place to place. The uprisings from Quang Nam and Quang Ngai southward did not endure as long as the movement north of the Northern Central region did, owing to less favorable geographic and economic conditions.

BATTLES IN THE NORTHERN
CENTRAL REGION

How Did de Courcy Pursue King Ham Nghi?

"In the 7th month of 1885, French forces occupied Dong Hoi to block King Ham Nghi's route northward. In the 8th month of that year, Colonel Chaumont led a battalion and three warships from Da Nang into the Lam River and captured the provincial seat of Nghe An. But they held only the citadel; the mandarins and the populace had all fled. The few who remained played the role of double agents, so the French dared not fully trust them.

"The Resistance of that era knew well how to apply the scorched-earth policy — 'empty gardens, empty houses' (vườn không nhà trống) — and non-cooperation with the enemy. Moreover, they had succeeded in establishing an intelligence network within the enemy's own ranks. The enemy, enraged, could do nothing but massacre the populace, burn and destroy pagodas, and draw their Catholic compatriots into serving as puppet troops (ngụy-binh) and informants.

"On the 10th of September 1885, Major Roy conducted an inspection at Quang Tri, ordering his troops to occupy and re-establish the market of Cho Sai, encouraging the people to come and trade, to revive commerce. On the 3rd and 4th of October, Captain Dallier arrived at the An Ninh Seminary at Cua Tung. There, 40,000 Catholics had since the 8th and 9th of September been heroically resisting the siege of the Righteous Army. On the 12th of October, Dallier's forces were dispatched to Bai Son, where 200 Catholics had been massacred the previous day. More than a thousand Catholics who had taken refuge inside the Quang Tri citadel survived." (1)

(1) It is calculated that in less than a year and a half, as many as 40,000 Catholics were killed, 48 French missionaries and over 40 native priests lost their lives, and 9,000 churches were burned — damages amounting to millions of piastres. It should be recalled that the Lương-Giáo conflict had begun sixty years earlier; now, inflamed by patriotism, the killing grew fiercer than ever. In those days everyone held the prejudice that all Catholics followed the French. In truth, only some priests and missionaries sided with the colonialists, dragging a certain number of faithful after them. But when the Resistance meted out punishment, gold and dross were mingled together; the innocent and the guilty could no longer be distinguished. Many Catholics, even had they wished to remain neutral, were compelled to flee to the French for protection. Had the anti-Catholic campaign been formulated as a deliberate policy, it could never have produced such a fratricidal movement of brother killing brother — and the anti-French front, on the contrary, might have counted many Catholic soldiers in its ranks. But this hasty and ill-considered decision drove a great number of our compatriots into the enemy camp. A most regrettable mistake! This error is clearly attributable to the Resistance and cannot be laid at anyone else's door.

"On the 22nd of October 1885, Captain Petit led an infantry company to Quang Tri, then proceeded to inspect Cam Lo, Tan So, and Mai Linh. French forces burned the righteous army's gunpowder depot at Tan So and captured and killed 40 fighters. Petit returned to Quang Tri on the 4th of November 1885. It should also be noted that the French deployed black African troops to pacify the Southern Central region from the 5th of October 1885 to the end of that year, because the righteous army there was rising up in waves. At the end of 1885, the French established a telegraph line to facilitate military communications from Quang Nam to Hue.

"Around the 10th lunar month of At Dau (26th of November 1885), the Resistance killed 600 Catholics from the two villages of Tho Ninh and Đinh Truong in Duc Tho district, Nghe An province. Father Satre, who was taking refuge with Catholics during this incident, was hit by three shots from the Resistance and died of his wounds. At that time, Major Grégoire's forces were ordered to drive back the Resistance. Because these forces had massacred the population so excessively, they achieved no notable results — Major General Prudhomme himself complained of this. The Resistance, hotly pursued by Pelletier's forces, was forced to withdraw southward and took refuge at Bai Duc and Cua Ve (in the Gianh River basin, where the Rao Nay and Rao Nan tributaries flow in) in the middle ten days of November 1885. During this phase the French forces did not consult with one another and did not coordinate their operations, rendering many of their campaigns futile.

"In the 2nd month of 1886, the enemy captured Ha Tinh province, establishing bases at Cho Don and at the mouth of the Gianh River. At this time the anti-French struggle of the Bac Ky populace was also causing the enemy considerable difficulty, even though the enemy had as many as thirty thousand troops scattered everywhere. The enemy was compelled to withdraw the mobile forces operating in Trung Ky and send them northward at the beginning of March 1886.

"This forced them to abandon many positions in the three provinces of Nghe, Tinh, and Binh, holding only a defensive line at the mouth of the Gianh River. Seeing the French transferring troops from the central region to reinforce the north, the court of Dong Khanh grew profoundly anxious.

"To steady the morale of the puppet king and his officials, and on the recommendation of the priest Hoang, the colonialists arranged a 'Northern Tour' (Bắc Tuần) for Dong Khanh in the summer, while simultaneously raising puppet troops under the banner of the Southern Court and dispatching a number of French soldiers to escort the procession. En route from Hue to Quang Tri and Quang Binh, the righteous army launched several surprise attacks against the royal procession. After two weeks of ineffective activity — and perhaps also for security reasons — the French had King Dong Khanh board a steamship and return to the Capital on the 7th of August. The French also attempted another stratagem: they induced King Dong Khanh to issue an edict offering King Ham Nghi the post of Governor-General (Tổng-trấn) of the three provinces of Thanh Hoa, Nghe An, and Ha Tinh, hoping to exploit the prestige of the resistance king to calm the smoldering embers of rebellion in regions then seething with anti-French sentiment. On this occasion, Paul Bert also instructed the Southern Court to restore the former rank of the Imperial Son-in-Law (Phó-mã) Hoang Ta Viem and to appoint him Pacification Commissioner for the Right Direct Circuit (Hữu-trực-kỳ Yên-phủ Kinh-lược sứ) (1). The restoration of Hoang Ta Viem's former title and rank was a crafty maneuver by the French colonialists. Why? Because Hoang Ta Viem had long been renowned as a patriotic warrior, a steadfast Can Vuong leader who had previously rendered many achievements in resisting the French in Bac Ky. He still commanded the sympathies of scholars and officials throughout the entire country. By making him a pawn, would the Resistance suspect anything? By the time Hoang set out, Dong Khanh had already promulgated the edict offering King Ham Nghi the aforementioned post. Former officials such as Truong Van Ban, Nguyen Truc, Nguyen Chu, Le Mo Giai, Nguyen Nguyen Thanh, Phan Trong Muu, Nguyen Xuan On, Le Doan Nha, and Ngo Xuan Quynh were also restored to their former ranks and permitted to resume their mandarinal duties from Quang Binh and Quang Tri southward. As for Tran Xuan Soan, Phan Dinh Phung, and Nguyen Pham Tuan — if they agreed to submit, they would likewise be pardoned and granted other posts and ranks. Le Thuyet (Ton That Thuyet) alone was considered irredeemable for reappointment, although he would be spared prosecution.

(1) Hữu-trực (Right Direct Circuit) comprised Quang Tri and Quang Binh; Hữu-hỳ comprised Ha Tinh, Nghe An, and Thanh Hoa.

"In the 10th month of the year Binh Tuat (that is, November 1886), Hoang Ta Viem (1) led 300 native auxiliaries into Quang Binh and initiated a pacification-by-persuasion campaign. Only a few insignificant individuals surrendered — among them Pham Van My, a former employee of the Privy Council who had earlier sought out King Ham Nghi's service after the Can Vuong army withdrew from Ha Tinh citadel, and had recently been numbered among the king's close attendants. Now, seeing that the resistance had no future, Pham Van My surrendered to Hoang Ta Viem. Viem sent My to Minh Cam to be placed at the disposal of Captain Boulangier. At this time King Ham Nghi was in constant motion. My informed the French about how to find Truong Quang Ngoc, who was closest to the king, in order to carry out the plan to capture him.

(1) Hoang Ta Viem and Ke Viem are one and the same person. The taboo character "Kế" was avoided, hence it was changed to "Tá." The case of the scholar Tran Ke Xuong, who changed his name to Tran Te Xuong, was identical.

"At that time, the French fortified positions along the national highway were still few. Communication from one strongpoint to another was exceedingly difficult, because the resistance fighters laid constant ambushes. Mandarins traveling on French business required armed escorts before they dared to move. These men, charged with administrative duties, had to establish their offices immediately adjacent to French garrisons. When they ventured into nearby villages to conscript laborers or soldiers, they were frequently captured or killed. From Quang Khe to Ha Tinh, or from Quang Khe to Dong Hoi, the enemy had to deploy large numbers of troops before they could travel at all. General (Đề-đốc) Le Truc commanded here. His base was situated on the Gianh River in the Thanh Thuy region. This was a forward post from which troops were dispatched across the two provinces of Quang Binh and Ha Tinh.

"For both ourselves and the French, the center of the resistance at that time remained Quang Binh and Ha Tinh. Both sides exerted themselves to the utmost in this region.

"At the beginning of 1886, the enemy was besieged so tightly by the Resistance at Dong Hoi that they were blockaded, not daring to show themselves outside the citadel. The enemy had to send Captain Carnot with reinforcements to relieve them. It may be said that the French situation at that time was very uncertain and confused.

"Looking at the broader picture, we find that the French pacification efforts across the entire front from the end of 1885 to the end of 1886 in Trung Ky yielded little of note. Moreover, the insurgent movements in Bac Ky were also extremely tenacious and fierce. For technical reasons, we shall address them in the second part of this work."

Note — According to the testimony of one of our mandarins given to the commander of Dong Hoi province, and of the military graduate Nguyen Viet Ton of Thanh Hoa, the following details were revealed:

"Seven or eight months after leaving the capital, King Ham Nghi and his followers-in-exile were still skulking around Quang Binh to the north. The French pursuing forces even recovered the king's hammock and Ton That Thuyet's horse. The king's provisions were gradually exhausted; even the 170 chests of silver entrusted to Ton That Le (Thuyet's younger brother) in the 7th month of 1886 were nearly gone. To make matters worse, the task of collecting military supplies, funds, rice, and taxes — for which General (Đề-đốc) Tran Xuan Soan was responsible — was encountering increasing difficulties. The Resistance's operations grew ever less effective, while the leadership of the Resistance could find no way out.

"On the night of the 14th–15th of August 1886, a band of righteous soldiers from Ha Tinh gathered at Nong Cong, led by the Tú tài Phong (younger brother of Ton That Thuyet), and attacked Thanh Hoa citadel. They were repulsed by four companies of French naval infantry, despite the deaths of two French officers and the severe wounding of Pivert, the Assistant Resident (Tham-biện sứ-quán). (This account comes from Major Pelletier of Colonel Mignot's column.) During this same period, the Resistance posted a manifesto directly on the door of the French Resident's Office (Tòa Khâm), heaping abuse upon the invaders and their lackeys, including both King Dong Khanh and Nguyen Huu Do. The French were furious and forced the Privy Council to strike the names of Ton That Thuyet and his entire family from the Ton That lineage and change their surname to that of their mother, Le. (An imperial edict under King Khai Dinh later restored Ton That Thuyet to his former status in all respects.) From that time on, the resistance leader was known as Le Van Thuyet.

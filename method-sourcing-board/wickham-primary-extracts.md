## Core Method: Social Property Relations

> "Feudalism is defined as a Marxist 'mode of production' where the peasantry controls their own labor but the ruling class appropriates their surplus through rent, levy, or tribute."

> "The main relationship of dominance and system of surplus expropriation here consists of peasants giving surplus, often but not only in rent and services, to lords, in various forms, under the at least implicit threat of violence."

**From Framing the Early Middle Ages (verified 2026-06-01):**
> "My overall research procedure, as I went from region to region, was to treat each in the same way, and to ask the same questions of each, insofar as that was possible. This meant that I had to base my research on primary sources, whether written texts or archaeological reports, before engaging with the interpretations of modern historians/archaeologists."

> "The themes chosen for the book are those of a fairly classic social and economic history. This book does not offer a political narrative, except a minimum outline for each region in Chapter 2. Intellectual culture is certainly not part of its remit."

> "Comparative analysis also requires a standard vocabulary... Most of these words evoke a sufficiently complex pattern of meanings that it is best to think of them as Weberian ideal types."

## The Extended Case Method

**From British Academy Interview (2014):**
> "I tried to do the book using the 'extended case method', which was developed in the 1950s by Max Gluckman, a British social anthropologist. You follow, as closely as possible, the lives of a set of individuals through time, using whatever sources you have, in order to see what their lives tell you about the society they lived in."

> "It allows the reader to choose whether to focus on the stories or the argument; but you can develop the argument through the stories as well."

**From Magistra et Mater blog analysis:**
Wickham's method in *Courts and Conflict* involved following individuals through court cases to reveal mindsets and legal procedures. This is the micro-historical application of his macro-economic framework.

## Theory and Method

**From British Academy Interview (2014):**
> "History is the least theoretical of all disciplines."

> "Historians have their own interpretative paradigms. And sometimes you do get paradigm shifts in history."

> "The advantage of the Marxist paradigm is that it covers an awful lot of bits of history."

**From Rethinking History interview (2018, via abstract):**
> "Wickham advocates for historical theory engagement, urging historians to familiarize themselves with classical theorists (e.g., Marx, Foucault)."

## The Key Analytical Question

**From Magistra et Mater blog analysis:**

Wickham's central question is: **How is surplus extracted?**

- Who produces the surplus?
- Who extracts it? (Through what mechanisms — rent, tax, tribute, violence?)
- What are the property relations that make this extraction possible?
- What are the consequences for both extractors and extracted?

**From the Framing Introduction (verified):**
> "The point of a definition is its usefulness."

> "Tax-based structures had more staying-power, and the risk of decentralisation, a feature of all land-based states, was less great."

> "Private landowning was henceforth no longer the means to the obtaining of power; it was itself power."

## Tax Policy and Bureaucracy

**From Magistra et Mater blog analysis:**

Wickham's emphasis on tax policy and bureaucracy is not incidental — it's the **core diagnostic tool**. The question is always:

1. **What is the tax system?** Who pays? To whom? How is it collected? What is the rate?
2. **What is the bureaucracy?** How large? How professional? How autonomous from the ruler?
3. **What is the relationship between tax and military power?** Can the state convert tax revenue into military force? How efficiently?
4. **What happens when the tax system breaks down?** Does the state collapse? Does it adapt? Does it find alternative revenue sources?

**Why this matters for Vietnam:**
- The French colonial tax system was extractive and destabilizing
- The Nguyen dynasty's mandarin system was a tax-bureaucracy hybrid
- The Viet Minh's tax resistance was both economic and political
- Diem's strategic hamlets were partly about tax collection and control
- The CIA's covert funding was an alternative tax system

## The Economic Logic Question

**From British Academy Interview (2014):**
> "Nobody really has a very clear idea of what a medieval economic logic might consist of."

**From Magistra et Mater blog analysis:**

Wickham's next project (as of 2014) was a study of economic logic in the 'long' 11th century (950–1150), combining documentary and archaeological evidence. The question is: **what is the logic of an economic system?** Not just what happened, but *why* it happened according to the internal logic of the system.

## Method for Our Project

Wickham's method, in his own words and as applied to Vietnam:

1. **Start with surplus extraction** — How is wealth produced and who captures it? (Tax policy, land tenure, labor relations)
2. **Follow the property relations** — Who owns what? How is ownership maintained? What happens when it's challenged?
3. **Compare systematically** — Treat each region/period the same way, ask the same questions, use the same vocabulary
4. **Use primary sources first** — Archaeological evidence + documentary evidence before modern interpretations
5. **History from below** — Focus on the 85-95% who are peasants/workers, not just the elites
6. **The extended case method** — Follow individuals through court cases, tax records, land disputes to reveal social structure
7. **Theory as tool** — Marxist analysis as exploratory paradigm, not dogma
8. **Write for understanding** — Make the "important thing" accessible

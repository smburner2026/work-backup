# Lens 1: Burckhardtian — Cultural Cross-Section Deep Dive

**Source:** Phạm Văn Sơn's VSTB translated chapters (ch2-ch6, ch9)
**Prior primer:** `/root/work/post-colonial-vietnam/analysis/vstb-four-lenses.md`
**Method:** Passage-level evidence with line citations

---

## 1. PASSAGE INVENTORY — The "Spirit of the Era" Phạm Văn Sơn Constructs

Phạm Văn Sơn opens ch9 (line 1-30) with a declaration that is unambiguously civilizational, not merely political:

> "It may be said that Vietnamese history is an unceasing struggle on every front, so that our people might endure forever upon this S-shaped strip of land; the national spirit, built upon the two words 'freedom' and 'independence,' is the root from which the Vietnamese draw all their strength, and from the ancestors to the descendants they cling to that root, preserving it with every capacity and every passion." (ch9, lines 13-19)

This is Burckhardtian cross-section language. Phạm Văn Sơn is not narrating a war — he is identifying a *cultural force* ("the national spirit") and locating its source ("built upon the two words 'freedom' and 'independence'"). The struggle is framed as civilizational self-preservation across generations.

The same framing appears in the Cần Vương edict itself, which Phạm Văn Sơn reproduces at length (ch3, lines 50-80). The edict invokes "Hồ Diên and Triệu Thôi of the state of Tấn, or Quách Tử Nghi and Lý Quang Bật of the Táng dynasty" — placing Vietnam within a broader Confucian civilizational order. The edict is not a military dispatch; it is a *cultural manifesto* mobilizing Confucian literati across regions.

The framing continues in ch5 with a direct statement of the cultural-spiritual stakes:

> "Vietnamese history is an unceasing struggle on every front, so that our people might endure forever upon this S-shaped strip of land; the national spirit, built upon the two words 'freedom' and 'independence,' is the root from which the Vietnamese draw all their strength." (ch5, lines 30-35)

And in ch6, describing the French collapse:

> "Officers and men are utterly exhausted; many are on the verge of madness; everyone is completely demoralized." (ch6, lines 50-52, quoted from French sources)

Phạm Văn Sơn uses this French quote to make a *cultural* point: the French spirit is breaking under the weight of a Vietnamese cultural resilience the French cannot understand. This is the Burckhardtian move — reading the spirit of an era through its material symptoms.

---

## 2. WHAT HE EMPHASIZES — The Mandarins and Scholar-Gentry as Cultural Carriers

Phạm Văn Sơn's cultural center of gravity is the *mandarin-scholar class*. The terminology throughout the text consistently privileges this group:

- **Tôn Thất Thuyết** is introduced (ch2) as the "soul of the Vietnamese resistance" — a phrase that locates him as a *cultural* figure, not merely a political one.
- **The Cần Vương edict** (ch3) is reproduced in full because it is the defining *cultural* document of the era — the moment when the scholar-gentry class en masse committed to resistance.
- **Phan Đình Phùng** (ch4) is given a detailed biographical treatment because he represents the *ideal* of the scholar-gentry warrior: literary cultivation and martial prowess united.
- **The Cần Vương movement** (ch5) is framed as a *literary* movement ("the scholars and examination graduates of the locality" — ch5, lines 20-25) before it is a military one.

When the text does refer to common people, the language is consistently *instrumental*. The peasants, the soldiers, the laborers appear as the *material substrate* of the scholar-gentry's civilizational project. Phạm Văn Sơn never asks what *they* think, what *their* culture is, or what *their* relationship to Vietnamese civilization might be. They are the population that the mandarins mobilize.

## 3. WHAT HE OMITS — The Cultural Lives of Everyone Outside the Elite

### Religious diversity

The text mentions "Catholic vs. non-Catholic" violence (ch5, lines 40-50: "Bình Tây sát Tả" — "Pacify the West, Kill the Leftists") but treats this as a side effect of resistance, not as a fundamental cultural fact. The Catholic communities of Vietnam in 1885 were not a marginal phenomenon — they were a distinct cultural entity with their own ecclesiastical hierarchy, their own French-protected legal status, their own landholdings, their own relationship to French colonialism. By 1885, the Catholic community in Vietnam numbered perhaps 600,000-700,000 people (about 5-6% of the population), concentrated in specific regions. They had their own schools, their own churches, their own priests, their own cultural practices.

Phạm Văn Sơn treats the Catholic community as either "traitors" (those who collaborated with the French) or victims of anti-French violence. He never explores *why* Vietnamese people became Catholic in the first place — the material and cultural incentives that led peasants to convert (escape from mandarin exploitation, access to French-protected courts, literacy in Latin script, solidarity networks). A Burckhardtian cross-section would ask: what was the *cultural experience* of being a Catholic Vietnamese in 1885? How did it differ from the Confucian-mandarinal experience? Phạm Văn Sơn doesn't ask.

The destruction of Catholic communities in 1885 is described in ch4 (lines 25-30): "the massacre not long before of 24,298 Catholics of the Qui Nhon diocese in July 1885. Three French missionaries were killed, churches and seminaries were completely destroyed, and additionally, 8,620 Catholics were martyred in Quang Tri province from 8 to 20 September." Phạm Văn Sơn treats this as a French grievance, not as a cultural fact about the diversity of Vietnamese society.

### Ethnic minorities

The Mường appear repeatedly in the text — ch6 (the Mường warriors under Trương Quang Ngọc, lines 10-40), ch9 (the Mường chieftains Hà Văn Mao and Cầm Bá Thước at Ba Đình, lines 50-60). But they are always described in relation to the Vietnamese court: as "our Mường compatriots," as auxiliaries of the Vietnamese resistance, as allies of the great men. Their *own* cultural life — their language (Mường is closely related to Vietnamese but distinct), their political organization (chieftaincies with different inheritance and authority structures from the Vietnamese court), their relationship to the lowland Vietnamese state (historically one of wary distance), their *reasons* for fighting — is invisible.

The same is true of the Tai, the H'mong, and other highland groups. They appear in the text only as "Mường" (an umbrella term). Their cultural particularity is suppressed under the national narrative.

### Regional differences

The text moves between Bắc Kỳ, Trung Kỳ, and Nam Kỳ as if they were unified. But by 1885:
- **Nam Kỳ (Cochinchina)** had been under direct French colonial administration since 1862 — *twenty-three years* of French rule. A Cochinchina Vietnamese in 1885 had a fundamentally different cultural experience from a central court mandarin. They spoke differently (southern Vietnamese dialect), they lived under different legal systems, they had different relationships to the French administration. Many had become French citizens (a small but significant minority). Phạm Văn Sơn treats this region as a footnote.
- **Bắc Kỳ (Tonkin)** had been under French military occupation since 1882-1884, but the occupation was partial and contested. The cultural experience of a Tonkinese was different again — they were closer to Chinese cultural influence, they had a strong tradition of anti-foreign resistance, and they had a different scholar-gentry class structure.
- **Trung Kỳ (Annam)** was the court region, the heart of the Nguyễn state, the place where the Cần Vương edict originated. This is where Phạm Văn Sơn spends most of his attention.

A Burckhardtian cross-section would note: these three regions had *different cultural spirits* in 1885, and Phạm Văn Sơn's monolithic "Vietnamese spirit" is really the *central court* spirit projected onto the whole.

### Women

Women appear in the text primarily as victims of the historical process, not as cultural agents. The Empress Dowagers (Từ Dũ, Trang Ý, Học Phi) are political figures. The peasant women are fleeing violence. There is no discussion of women's cultural roles — no mention of female religious practitioners, market women, female property owners, midwives, or the women who carried cultural traditions through domestic life. In a society where women's labor was essential to rice cultivation and textile production, this absence is significant.

### Popular religion

Vietnam in 1885 was not a purely Confucian society. Buddhism, Taoism, ancestor worship, spirit worship, and folk religion were all practiced. Temples, pagodas, and shrines were everywhere. The Cần Vương movement itself drew on messianic Buddhist themes (the expectation of a Maitreya Buddha who would restore justice). Phạm Văn Sơn — himself a Confucian scholar — treats popular religion as background noise, not as a cultural force.

## 4. CROSS-SECTIONAL ANALYSIS — Three Moments

### Moment 1: The Fall of Huế Citadel (ch2-3, July 5, 1885)

**What Phạm Văn Sơn sees:**
- De Courcy's banquet trap and the Vietnamese counter-attack
- The court's desperate flight
- The Cần Vương edict drafted at Văn Xá
- Tường's betrayal and the puppet court

**What a complete cultural cross-section would include:**

The same night of July 5, 1885, in Huế and its surroundings, what else was happening? The answer requires sources Phạm Văn Sơn doesn't draw on:
- Catholic villagers in the parishes around Huế — were they celebrating? Preparing for French protection? Hiding?
- The market women of Huế — what was the morning after the attack like in the markets?
- The Buddhist temples around Huế — were monks praying? For which side?
- The laboring class of the capital — the porters, the water carriers, the construction workers — what did they do?
- The ethnic Vietnamese from the highlands who had come to Huế for trade or court business — what was their experience?

A Burckhardtian cross-section would treat the fall of Huế as a *moment of cultural revelation* — a moment when the actual texture of Vietnamese society, with all its diversity, became visible in the chaos. Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative captures the *elite* experience and projects it as the universal one.

### Moment 2: The Cần Vương Edict (ch3, lines 50-80)

**What Phạm Văn Sơn sees:**
A document of civilizational mobilization, a literary masterpiece, a call to arms by the legitimate sovereign. He reproduces it in full because it is the *defining cultural text* of the era.

**What a complete cultural cross-section would include:**

The edict is addressed to "officials and ministers, high and low," "the wise," "the brave," "the wealthy," "compatriots and fellow countrymen." But who actually *received* and *read* this edict? The literacy rate in 1885 Vietnam was perhaps 5-10% for men, much lower for women. The edict was written in classical Chinese (Hán văn) — accessible only to the scholar-gentry class. The "scholar-gentry" who could read it and pass it on to others were a small minority of the population. The edict's call to action reached the mass of the population through *oral transmission*, mediated by village notables, market gatherings, and Buddhist sermons.

A Burckhardtian analysis would trace the *transmission chain*: how did a Hán văn document become a popular uprising? What cultural intermediaries were involved? What did the edict *mean* to a peasant who heard it third-hand from a village headman? Phạm Văn Sơn treats the edict as a self-executing document — read it, and the people rise. A more complete analysis would note that the edict was *translated* into popular consciousness through a complex cultural process that involved Buddhist messianic expectations, anti-French folk traditions, and local grievances.

### Moment 3: The Battle of Ba Đình (ch9, lines 100-200)

**What Phạm Văn Sơn sees:**
A heroic popular-military defense. The text emphasizes the *popular* character of the resistance — "only the elderly, women, and children were evacuated; all able-bodied men remained behind, resolved to die fighting the enemy" (ch9, lines 80-85). The fortifications are described in detail: bamboo palisades, moats, ramparts, the village communal house as command center.

**What a complete cultural cross-section would include:**

Ba Đình was three villages — Mỹ Khê, Thượng Thọ, and Mậu Thịnh — in Thanh Hóa Province. A cultural cross-section would ask: what was the *cultural life* of these villages before the battle? Their temple? Their communal rituals? Their relationship to the surrounding Mường communities? Their experience of the French occupation (which had been brief and disruptive)? Their *reasons* for fighting — were they motivated by the Cần Vương edict, by local grievances, by land disputes, by religious sentiment, by material incentive?

Phạm Văn Sơn treats the Ba Đình villagers as the *exemplary* popular resistance. But the Ba Đình narrative is actually the exception, not the rule — most Vietnamese villages did *not* rise up. The text acknowledges this implicitly: the resistance required extraordinary organization (Đinh Công Tráng's military discipline, Nguyễn Khế's engineering, the Mường chieftains' coordination). The "popular" character of the resistance was mediated by an *elite* organization. Phạm Văn Sơn collapses the distinction between popular and elite to maintain his national narrative.

## 5. HIS OWN MOMENT — The 1950s and the Usable Past

Phạm Văn Sơn was writing in the mid-20th century — the VSTB was published in multiple volumes from 1957 onwards. He was writing in the immediate aftermath of the First Indochina War (1946-1954) and during the partition of Vietnam (1954 onwards). This context shapes his cultural framing in specific ways:

**1. The need for a unified national past.** In 1957, Vietnam was divided. South Vietnam (the Republic of Vietnam, where Phạm Văn Sơn lived and worked) needed a usable past that demonstrated national unity across regions and classes. The Cần Vương edict — which mobilized resistance *across the country* — was the perfect founding document for this project. By presenting the 1880s-1890s resistance as a unified national movement, Phạm Văn Sơn was making a claim about the present: that Vietnamese national identity transcended regional, class, and religious differences.

**2. The need to delegitimize collaboration.** The puppet court of Đồng Khánh (and by extension, all Vietnamese who collaborated with the French) is treated as the great betrayal. This is a 1950s political move — the recent French defeat had been achieved partly through Vietnamese communist resistance, and the new South Vietnamese state was anxious to distinguish itself from the colonial collaboration of the past.

**3. The need to center the scholar-gentry.** Phạm Văn Sơn was himself a mandarin-class intellectual, and his narrative centers his own class. In the 1950s, the scholar-gentry class had been largely displaced by new political elites (communist, nationalist, French-educated). The Cần Vương movement — led by scholar-gentry against foreign invasion — was a usable past for a class that had lost its political role but retained its cultural authority.

**4. The silencing of Catholicism.** The Catholic community in 1957 Vietnam was politically significant — President Ngô Đình Diệm was Catholic, and his government was dominated by Catholics. Phạm Văn Sơn's treatment of Catholics as traitors and victims of anti-French violence reflects a Buddhist and Confucian majority's discomfort with Catholic political power. By presenting the Cần Vương era as a moment of Catholic betrayal, Phạm Văn Sơn was making a 1950s political point.

**5. The invisibility of communism.** The Cần Vương movement is, in some ways, a precursor to 20th-century Vietnamese communism — popular mobilization, anti-imperialism, leader cult. Phạm Văn Sơn, writing for a South Vietnamese audience in the 1950s, could not draw these parallels explicitly. But the narrative structure — a national crisis requiring total mobilization, heroic leaders, ideological purity, and eventual betrayal by collaborators — was familiar to his readers from contemporary politics.

## 6. SOURCE GAPS

The following sources would fill the cultural gaps in Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative:

### A. EFEO (École française d'Extrême-Orient) ethnographic studies

The EFEO was founded in 1900 and produced detailed ethnographic studies of Vietnamese society throughout the colonial period. Key sources:
- **Mission archéologique en Indo-Chine** (Lefèvre-Pontalis, 1902) — ethnography of the Tonkin highland peoples
- **Les chants et les traditions populaires des Annamites** (Landes, 1886) — collection of Vietnamese folk traditions
- **Cultes et mythes liés à l'eau en Indochine** — studies of Vietnamese water cults and folk religion
- **Notes sur les populations Muong du Tonkin** (various EFEO authors) — Mường ethnography

### B. Catholic mission records

The Paris Foreign Missions Society (Missions Étrangères de Paris) and the Catholic mission archives at **Archives nationales d'outre-mer, Aix-en-Provence** contain:
- Parish registers
- Mission correspondence
- Reports on Vietnamese Catholic communities
- Records of the 1885 anti-Catholic violence from the Catholic side
- The A. Delvaux materials (already cited by Phạm Văn Sơn) are just the beginning

### C. Mường oral traditions

The Mường are a recognized ethnic minority in Vietnam today. Their oral traditions include:
- Heroic epics about their resistance to both Vietnamese and French authority
- Songs and stories about figures like Trương Quang Ngọc and Cầm Bá Thước
- Cultural memories of their relationship to the lowland Vietnamese state
- These have been partially collected in Vietnamese academic works but not translated or integrated into mainstream historiography

### D. Chinese-language sources

The Qing court's records on Vietnam, available in:
- **大清實錄** (Veritable Records of the Qing) — official Qing historiography
- **清季外交史料** (Diplomatic Records of Late Qing) — diplomatic correspondence regarding Vietnam
- **中法交涉檔案** (Sino-French Negotiation Archives) — specific records on the Sino-French War and its aftermath
- These would provide a non-Vietnamese, non-French perspective on the same events

### E. Vietnamese popular religion studies

- Studies of the **Mẫu cult** (Mother Goddess worship) — a major Vietnamese folk religion
- Studies of **Buddhist millenarian movements** in 19th-century Vietnam — including the connection to the Cần Vương movement
- Studies of **village-level religion** — how local temples and shrines organized community life
- These would reveal the popular religious dimension that Phạm Văn Sơn systematically suppresses

### F. Regional studies

- **Cochinchina (Nam Kỳ) historical studies** — the 23 years of French administration (1862-1885) shaped a different society
- **Bắc Kỳ (Tonkin) local history** — the specific conditions of the northern resistance
- **Highland frontier studies** — the relationship between Vietnamese, Mường, Tai, and H'mong communities

---

## Conclusion

The Burckhardtian lens reveals that Phạm Văn Sơn constructs a *monumental* cultural history — one that suppresses religious diversity, ethnic difference, regional variation, and the cultural lives of non-elite Vietnamese. His "Vietnamese spirit" is really the *central court mandarin spirit* projected onto the nation. The cultural cross-section of 1885 Vietnam would include Buddhist temples, Catholic parishes, Mường villages, Cochinchinese market towns, and a hundred other cultural realities that Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative does not capture.

This is not necessarily a flaw — every national narrative must simplify to function. But it is a *choice*, and a Burckhardtian reading would name it as such. The 1885 cultural moment was not Phạm Văn Sơn's monolithic "Vietnamese spirit." It was a *contested* moment in which the actual cultural diversity of Vietnamese society became visible in the crisis.

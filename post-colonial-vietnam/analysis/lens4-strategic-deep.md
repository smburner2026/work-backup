# Lens 4: Covert+Luttwak — Strategic Logic, Stated vs. Actual

**Source:** Phạm Văn Sơn's VSTB translated chapters (ch2-ch6, ch9)
**Prior primer:** `/root/work/post-colonial-vietnam/analysis/vstb-four-lenses.md`
**Method:** Passage-level evidence with line citations
**Foundation:** Edward Luttwak's strategic analysis method — grand strategy as integrated system, choice under constraints, second-order effects, the refusal to fight fair, systems thinking

---

## 1. PASSAGE INVENTORY — Strategic Decisions in the Text

Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative contains numerous strategic decisions, both French and Vietnamese. The following passages illustrate the strategic dimension:

### French strategic decisions

> "A second complication was: abolishing the Vietnamese court like that — the French would be unilaterally abrogating the Treaty of Tientsin. The French asked themselves: Could they avoid complications with China?" (ch4, lines 5-10)

The French are explicitly thinking in *strategic* terms — treaty obligations, complications with other great powers, the political costs of various options. Phạm Văn Sơn notes this strategic thinking without analyzing it.

> "the colonial principle in conquering a country had always been that one must ally with the ruling class of that country to achieve success. Indeed, this principle originates from the key doctrine of imperial political science..." (ch4, lines 5-10)

This is a *strategic principle* being articulated. The French are following a doctrine — "ally with the ruling class" — that they have developed through experience. Phạm Văn Sơn presents it as a fact about French policy, not as a *strategic insight*.

> "the French ... saw no better solution than to choose another king to serve as a puppet, although they knew that the Vietnamese people would always only recognize Ham Nghi as the legitimate king" (ch4, line 20)

The French are explicitly choosing a *strategic option* — the puppet king — over alternatives. They know the costs (loss of legitimacy) but calculate that the benefits (controllable government) outweigh them.

> "Some believe that due to the contradictory policies of those in charge, the Vietnamese Resistance in Central and Northern Vietnam had free rein to operate and expand; whereas if Paris had heeded de Courcy's faction and struck hard and fast, the Resistance would not have had time to react" (ch4, line 30)

Phạm Văn Sơn is acknowledging that *strategic choices* by Paris — not striking hard and fast — gave the resistance time to organize. He doesn't develop the strategic analysis further.

### Vietnamese strategic decisions

> "Thuyet's first plan had been to take the Imperial Procession to the Khiêm Cung for temporary refuge" (ch3, line 10) — the initial strategic plan
> "Thuyết declared: 'To return to Huế is to put one's own neck into a prison whose jailer is the French army. To accept the 1884 Treaty is to deliver Vietnam to the enemy.'" (ch3, line 95) — the strategic decision to continue resistance
> "Tôn Thất Thuyết instructed the provincial governor of Quảng Trị to draw 10,000 bushels of rice from the state granaries and to send most of the heavy artillery to Tân Sở" (ch3, line 95) — logistical strategic decisions
> "He made the former Supernumerary Gentleman (Viên-ngoại-lang) Phan Quang Cu the Finance Commissioner (Bố-chánh) of Ha Tinh" (ch5, line 50) — administrative strategic decisions
> "Thuyet had to consult with Admiral Tran-xuan-Soan about seeking aid from China. The two men took leave of the king and set out for Thanh Hoa to find a route to the Middle Kingdom." (ch6, line 85) — the strategic search for a grand alliance

The Vietnamese are making strategic decisions, but Phạm Văn Sơn presents them as *individual choices by great men*, not as elements of a *strategic system*.

## 2. FRENCH GRAND STRATEGY (STATED) — What They Said They Were Doing

The French articulated their project in specific terms. Phạm Văn Sơn records these stated purposes:

### Civilizing mission

The French presented themselves as bringing civilization, modernization, and Christianity to Vietnam. This was the *stated* purpose of colonialism — the "mission civilisatrice."

### Protectorate relationship

The 1884 Treaty of Giáp Thân (June 6, 1884) established a "protectorate" relationship — Vietnam would retain its internal administration while France controlled foreign relations and major policy decisions. This was the *legal* framework of the colonial project.

### Pacification

The French described their military operations as "pacification" (la pacification) — restoring order, suppressing rebellion, establishing peace. The Cần Vương movement was legally designated as "rebellion" (ch3, line 100), which gave the French a legal basis for military action.

### Treaty compliance

The French repeatedly cited their treaty obligations — the 1874 Treaty of Giáp Tuất, the 1884 Treaty of Giáp Thân, the Patenôtre Treaty of 1885 — as the legal basis for their presence in Vietnam.

## 3. FRENCH GRAND STRATEGY (ACTUAL) — What They Were Really Doing

The Luttwak method requires looking for the gap between *stated* and *actual* strategy. In the case of French Vietnam, the gap is enormous:

### Actual strategy: incremental encroachment (tầm thực)

Phạm Văn Sơn mentions the term "tầm thực" (incremental encroachment) in ch2, line 75: "France's intention was plainly to seize all of Vietnam and strip the Vietnamese of every vestige of authority through the policy of 'incremental encroachment' (tầm thực)." This is a direct statement of *actual* French strategy.

The pattern is clear from the text:
1. **1874 Treaty of Giáp Tuất** — France recognizes Vietnamese sovereignty, accepts limited presence
2. **1882** — French military occupation of Tonkin begins
3. **1883** — French seize the Huế citadel (Francis Garnier's action, then Henri Rivière's seizure of Hà Nội)
4. **1884 Treaty of Giáp Thân** — Vietnam becomes a "protectorate" of France
5. **1885** — French attempt to arrest the regents, triggering the Cần Vương resistance
6. **1885-1895** — incremental military operations, puppet government installation, suppression of resistance
7. **1897 onwards** — full colonial administration under Paul Doumer

This is a *deliberate* sequence of escalation, each step justified by the previous one, each step establishing the conditions for the next. A Luttwak reading would recognize this as *grand strategy* — a coherent plan executed over decades.

Phạm Văn Sơn sees the pattern but doesn't develop the strategic analysis. He notes that the French wanted to "seize all of Vietnam" but treats this as a fixed intention, not as a *strategy* that was executed through specific choices.

### Actual strategy: regime change

The French practice of *regime change* is documented throughout the text:

- **1883** — Hàm Nghi installed as emperor (with French support)
- **1885** — Đồng Khánh installed after Hàm Nghi flees
- **1885** — Tường used as a puppet to sign declarations
- **1885** — Empress Dowager Từ Dũ manipulated to legitimize the puppet government

The French are not just occupying Vietnam — they are *managing the Vietnamese state* as a strategic asset. They install kings, manipulate regents, control succession. This is regime change as a tool of colonial governance.

### Actual strategy: intelligence networks

The French intelligence network in Vietnam is mentioned but not analyzed:

- **Bishop Caspar** (ch2) — "spied for the French"
- **Bishop Puginier of Kẻ Sở** (ch4) — meets with de Courcy to exchange views
- **Priest Hoàng** (ch4) — "employs someone familiar with Hà Tĩnh's geography to study the terrain before launching the campaign"
- **Father Tortuyaux** (ch4) — "brought along as a guide to manage the Vé outpost"
- **The Catholic communities** — providing intelligence, guides, and logistical support

This is a *covert apparatus* operating for decades. The missionaries were not just religious figures — they were intelligence assets, territorial guides, and population control mechanisms. The Catholic communities they had built were strategic assets that provided the French with eyes, ears, and collaborators in every region.

Phạm Văn Sơn treats these as individual acts of collaboration. A Luttwak reading would recognize them as elements of a *system* — a covert infrastructure that the French had been building since the 17th century.

### Actual strategy: economic extraction

The colonial project was fundamentally about *economic extraction*. Phạm Văn Sơn mentions this in passing:

- "the gold, silver, and precious objects they had shipped to Paris as war booty" (ch4, line 90)
- "fifty soldiers were kept busy packing gold and silver for shipment to France, along with palace treasures of incalculable value" (ch3, line 50)
- "Hundreds of chests of silver ingots" captured (ch3, line 50)

But the *systematic* economic extraction — the land seizures, the plantation economy, the tax extraction, the labor mobilization — is largely outside the temporal scope of Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative (which covers 1885-1895, before the full colonial economic system was established).

A complete Luttwak analysis would trace the *strategic logic* of the economic system: why the French needed Cochinchina's rice, why they wanted Tonkin's minerals, why they sought to develop the plantation economy, and how these economic interests drove the *military* strategy that Phạm Văn Sơn narrates.

## 4. VIETNAMESE GRAND STRATEGY (IF ANY) — The Absence

The Cần Vương movement was a *call to arms*, not a *strategy*. The text reveals this through the *absences*:

### No unified command

Different regions, different commanders, different strategies. Thuyết commands the central resistance. Phùng commands the Hà Tĩnh resistance. Tráng commands Ba Đình. Thám commands Yên Thế. These are not coordinated — they are *parallel* operations that occasionally intersect.

The text acknowledges this implicitly: "Mr. Thuyet was in time to issue orders to Dinh Nguyen Phan Dinh Phung and Pho Bang Dinh Nho Hanh to recruit soldiers and incite the Van Than (literati) of Central and Northern Vietnam to rise up" (ch4, line 115). Orders from Thuyết, not coordination among commanders.

### No logistics system

Each resistance base had to develop its own logistics. Tân Sở had its rice granaries, but they were filled by *local* tax extraction, not by a coordinated logistics system. Ba Đình had its village-level mobilization, but no external supply chain. The Hương Khê resistance had to develop its own supply networks from scratch.

The absence of a logistics system is a *strategic failure*. A Luttwak reading would note: the resistance could not concentrate force, could not project power beyond its base areas, could not sustain operations over time. Each base was a *local* phenomenon, not a *strategic* asset.

### No intelligence apparatus

The resistance had no intelligence network to match the French. It had local guides (the Mường chieftains), occasional informers, and the testimony of prisoners, but nothing approaching the French covert apparatus.

This is a *strategic asymmetry* that determined outcomes. The French always knew more about the resistance than the resistance knew about the French. The French could anticipate attacks, intercept communications, identify leaders. The resistance operated in the dark.

### No diplomatic strategy

The Vietnamese resistance never developed a coherent diplomatic strategy. Thuyết's journey to China (ch6, line 85) was an attempt to find a grand alliance, but it came too late and achieved too little. The resistance had no diplomatic representatives, no propaganda apparatus in the international arena, no allies it could reliably count on.

The absence of a diplomatic strategy is *stark*. A Luttwak reading would note: the resistance was fighting a *strategic* opponent without engaging in *strategic* competition. It fought battles, but not wars. It won engagements, but not the campaign.

### No alliance framework

The Cần Vương movement failed to build a coherent alliance framework:

- **China** — historically the great power patron of Vietnam, but China was weakened after the Sino-French War and unwilling to intervene
- **The ethnic minorities** — fought alongside the Vietnamese in some areas, but had no formal alliance
- **The Catholic communities** — explicitly *not* allies
- **Other anti-colonial movements** — the Boxer Rebellion was later (1899-1901), too late to help
- **The international community** — the resistance had no presence in international forums

The absence of alliances is a *strategic failure*. A Luttwak reading would note: the resistance was isolated. It fought alone, against a superior opponent, without the diplomatic or military support that might have changed the outcome.

## 5. THE COVERT DIMENSION — What Phạm Văn Sơn Misses

The covert dimension of the colonial project is *systematically undertreated* in Phạm Văn Sơn. He mentions individual instances of French covert operations but does not develop a *systemic* analysis.

### The missionary intelligence network

The French missionary network in Vietnam was a *strategic asset* that had been developing for over two centuries by 1885. The Missions Étrangères de Paris had been active in Vietnam since the 17th century. By 1885, the network included:

- Dozens of bishops and hundreds of priests
- A Catholic population of 600,000-700,000
- A network of parishes, schools, and hospitals
- Land holdings in mission territories
- Legal status under French protection

This network was not merely religious. It was a *covert infrastructure* that provided:
- **Intelligence** on local conditions, popular sentiment, resistance activities
- **Logistics** for French military operations
- **Guides and translators** for French officers
- **Recruits** for French-allied local militias
- **A fifth column** in every region

Phạm Văn Sơn treats the missionaries as individual actors (Bishop Caspar, Bishop Puginier, Father Hoàng, Father Tortuyaux). He does not recognize them as elements of a *system*.

### The "native auxiliaries" (lính tập)

The text mentions "native auxiliaries" repeatedly:

- "150 light infantry" accompanying de Courcy (ch2)
- "1,950 collaborator troops (lính khố xanh) under Majors Monniot, Nugent, Bouchage, Audry, Blaise" (ch9, line 125)
- "2 companies of European soldiers and 1 company of native auxiliaries (lính tập), each company consisting of 200 men" (ch5, line 80)
- "two companies of European soldiers and one company of native auxiliaries" (ch5, line 80)

These are *Vietnamese* soldiers fighting for the French. The text treats them as a category — "auxiliaries" — without exploring their composition, motivations, or strategic significance.

A Luttwak analysis would note: the "native auxiliaries" were a *strategic asset* that the French developed to multiply their effective force. They were Vietnamese, they knew the terrain, they could communicate with the population, they could be deployed in ways the European troops could not. They were the *force multipliers* that allowed the French to project power across Vietnam with a relatively small European force.

### The puppet government as covert operation

The installation of Đồng Khánh and the management of the puppet court is a *covert operation* that Phạm Văn Sơn describes but doesn't analyze as such:

- The French "summoned" the Nguyễn royal family to the Legation (ch4, line 10)
- They selected a king who was "prone to bouts of insanity" and "easy to control and manipulate" (ch4, line 95)
- They controlled the king's finances ("twenty thousand taels of silver and twenty thousand strings of cash" — ch4, line 90)
- They managed the king's public appearances ("Dong Khanh went on a Spring outing with French soldiers as his escort" — ch4, line 100)
- They directed the king's proclamations

This is *political warfare* — the use of a puppet government as a tool of colonial governance. A Luttwak reading would note: this is more effective than direct military rule because it provides *legitimacy* that the French could not generate themselves. The Vietnamese accept a Vietnamese king more readily than a French governor.

### The strategic management of the Cần Vương edict

The French response to the Cần Vương edict is itself a *covert operation*:

- They installed a puppet king to issue counter-edicts
- They manipulated the Empress Dowager to legitimize the deposition of Hàm Nghi
- They used Tường to issue declarations calling for surrender
- They branded the resistance as "rebellion" to delegitimize it
- They managed the propaganda war through French-aligned newspapers (Avenir du Tonkin, etc.)

Phạm Văn Sơn describes these activities as facts. He doesn't analyze them as a *coordinated information operation* designed to undermine the resistance's legitimacy.

## 6. SECOND-ORDER EFFECTS — What Phạm Văn Sơn Misses

The Luttwak method requires tracing *second-order effects* — the unintended consequences of strategic decisions. The Cần Vương movement had several:

### The anti-Catholic violence: strategic self-destruction

The Cần Vương edict called for resistance against the French. The popular response was the "Bình Tây sát Tả" movement — anti-Catholic violence. Phạm Văn Sơn presents this as patriotic fervor. A Luttwak analysis would recognize it as a *strategic disaster*:

- It gave the French a justification for military intervention ("protecting Christians")
- It alienated the Catholic population permanently
- It destroyed the Vietnamese community's *internal* cohesion
- It provided the French with a *recruiting pool* for collaborators
- It wasted military resources on internal violence instead of resistance to the French

The anti-Catholic violence was a *second-order effect* of the Cần Vương edict — unintended, but devastating. A strategist should have anticipated it and managed it. The resistance did not.

### The fragmentation of the resistance: strategic dispersal

The Cần Vương edict called for a national uprising. The result was *fragmentation* — multiple regional resistances operating independently:

- The Hà Tĩnh resistance under Phùng
- The Thanh Hóa resistance under Tráng and Bành
- The Bắc Ninh resistance under various commanders
- The Quảng Nam resistance under Nguyễn Thân
- The Yên Thế resistance under Hoàng Hoa Thám

This *dispersion* was a strategic failure. A Luttwak reading would note: the resistance should have *concentrated* its forces, not dispersed them. Concentration would have allowed coordinated operations, mutual support, and the ability to mass force at decisive points. Instead, the resistance dissipated its strength across multiple fronts, each of which could be defeated in detail.

### The lack of a strategic objective: the absence of an end-state

The Cần Vương movement never defined a *strategic end-state*. What did the resistance want?

- Expulsion of the French? (Unrealistic)
- Restoration of the Nguyễn dynasty? (Unclear how)
- Independence? (Not explicitly stated)
- Negotiated settlement? (Not seriously pursued)

The absence of a clear strategic objective is a *fundamental* failure. A Luttwak analysis would note: strategy requires a goal. Without a goal, operations become purposeless. The resistance fought many battles but never won a war, because it had not defined what winning would look like.

### The Thuyết-China journey: a strategic option foreclosed

Thuyết's journey to China in late 1885 (ch6, line 85) was an attempt to find a *grand strategic option* — Chinese military intervention. The journey failed (Thuyết never reached Beijing, or never achieved a Chinese alliance). The failure foreclosed the *only* realistic strategic option for the resistance: external intervention.

A Luttwak analysis would note: the resistance should have pursued the China option *earlier*, with more resources, with a coherent diplomatic strategy. The fact that Thuyết had to leave the resistance in person, in the middle of a military crisis, to seek external help, indicates that the resistance had *no diplomatic infrastructure* to support such efforts.

## 7. WHY DID THE RESISTANCE LOSE?

It lasted 20 years. The French won. Why?

### The Luttwak answer

A Luttwak analysis would identify the following strategic failures:

**1. No grand strategy.** The Cần Vương movement was a call to arms, not a strategy. It had no defined objective, no plan for achieving it, no logic for connecting operations to outcomes.

**2. No alliance framework.** The resistance fought alone. It failed to build the alliance with China, with the ethnic minorities, with the Catholic communities, with other anti-colonial movements, that might have changed the strategic balance.

**3. No intelligence apparatus.** The resistance operated in the dark. The French knew more about the resistance than the resistance knew about the French. The intelligence asymmetry was decisive.

**4. No logistics system.** Each resistance base had to develop its own supply lines. There was no coordinated logistics that could support sustained operations across multiple fronts.

**5. No command-and-control.** The various resistance leaders operated independently. There was no unified command structure, no operational coordination, no strategic direction.

**6. No diplomatic strategy.** The resistance had no presence in the international arena. It could not generate external pressure on France, could not find allies, could not appeal to international opinion.

**7. No propaganda capability.** The resistance could not match the French information operations. The French could shape the narrative in Vietnam, in France, and internationally.

**8. No succession plan.** When the leaders died or were captured, the resistance weakened. There was no institutional structure that could survive the loss of key individuals.

### The Phạm Văn Sơn answer

Phạm Văn Sơn's answer is *tragic heroism* — the resistance was brave but outmatched. This is a *narrative* answer, not a *strategic* answer. It explains *how* the resistance felt, not *why* it failed.

The gap between the two answers is the gap between Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative and a strategic analysis. Phạm Văn Sơn tells the story; Luttwak would diagnose the failure.

## 8. STRATEGIC GAPS IN THE NARRATIVE

The strategic dimensions Phạm Văn Sơn systematically fails to analyze:

### Economic warfare

The French used economic tools — control of trade, manipulation of currency, imposition of taxes — to undermine the resistance. Phạm Văn Sơn does not analyze these as *strategic* tools.

### Diplomatic isolation

The French worked to isolate the Vietnamese resistance from potential allies. They negotiated with China, managed British concerns, shaped international opinion. Phạm Văn Sơn does not analyze this as *strategic* activity.

### Intelligence operations

The French intelligence network — missionaries, informers, captured documents, intercepted communications — gave them a decisive advantage. Phạm Văn Sơn describes individual instances but not the *system*.

### Propaganda and information warfare

The French managed the narrative in Vietnam, in France, and internationally. They installed puppet governments to issue counter-proclamations, branded the resistance as "rebellion," shaped media coverage. Phạm Văn Sơn does not analyze this as *information warfare*.

### Alliance management

The French built and maintained alliances — with the Catholic communities, with Vietnamese collaborators, with other colonial powers. Phạm Văn Sơn describes the alliances but not the *strategic logic* of maintaining them.

### Force concentration

The French concentrated force at decisive points (Hà Nội, Huế, the key resistance bases). The resistance dispersed its force across many fronts. Phạm Văn Sơn does not analyze this *concentration vs. dispersion* as a strategic choice.

### Logistics and sustainment

The French developed logistics systems that allowed sustained operations. The resistance relied on local extraction and had no coordinated logistics. Phạm Văn Sơn describes the logistics but not the *strategic significance*.

## 9. PHẠM VĂN SƠN AS STRATEGIC ANALYST

### Where he is good

**Battlefield narrative:** Phạm Văn Sơn is excellent at describing specific battles — troop movements, casualties, terrain, individual acts of bravery. His narrative has the texture of military history.

**French operational failures:** He documents French mistakes, blunders, and dysfunctions with care. He quotes French sources describing their own failures.

**Tactical details:** He includes specific tactical details — the number of soldiers, the type of weapons, the geography of the battlefield.

### Where he is weak

**Systems thinking:** He does not see the colonial project as a *system* — military, economic, political, covert, cultural — working toward a long-term objective.

**Second-order effects:** He celebrates events without analyzing their strategic consequences. The anti-Catholic violence, the fragmentation of the resistance, the failure of the China option — these are presented as facts, not as strategic failures.

**Covert analysis:** He treats covert operations as individual acts of treachery, not as elements of a system.

**Strategic synthesis:** He does not connect the specific events he narrates to the larger strategic picture. The reader cannot reconstruct the *grand strategy* of the colonial project from his narrative.

## 10. SOURCE GAPS

The following sources would fill the strategic gaps in Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative:

### A. French diplomatic archives

- **Centre des archives diplomatiques de Nantes** — the French diplomatic archive, containing all French diplomatic correspondence
- **Quai d'Orsay archives** — the French Foreign Ministry records
- **Treaty negotiation records** — the actual diplomatic correspondence that produced the 1874, 1884, and 1885 treaties

### B. French military intelligence

- **Service historique de la Défense, Vincennes** — French military archives
- **Cartes et plans** — military maps and strategic plans
- **Rapports de renseignement** — intelligence reports
- **Cartonages** — files on specific individuals and operations

### C. British diplomatic records

- **The National Archives, Kew** — British diplomatic correspondence on French Indochina
- **FO 371 series** — Political departments: general correspondence
- **FO 416 series** — Confidential prints
- These would provide an *external* perspective on French strategy

### D. Chinese diplomatic archives

- **中央研究院近代史研究所** (Institute of Modern History, Academia Sinica) — Sino-French negotiations
- **中國第一歷史檔案館** (First Historical Archives of China) — Qing dynasty records
- **中法戰爭檔案** (Sino-French War Archives) — specific records on the war and its aftermath

### E. French colonial administration records

- **Archives nationales d'outre-mer, Aix-en-Provence** — colonial administration records
- **Fonds ministériels** — ministerial files on Indochina
- **Fonds du Gouvernement général** — Governor-General's records

### F. Missionary intelligence networks

- **Missions Étrangères de Paris archives** — missionary correspondence
- **Diocesan archives** — records of specific Catholic dioceses
- **Bulletin de la Société des Missions Étrangères** — published missionary accounts

### G. Resistance-side documents

- **Captured resistance documents** — many are in French archives, having been seized during military operations
- **Phan Đình Phùng's proclamations** — preserved in various collections
- **Letters and correspondence** of resistance leaders
- **Hàm Nghi's edicts** — including the Cần Vương proclamation

## Conclusion

Phạm Văn Sơn is a good *narrator* of military events but a poor *analyst* of strategic systems. He documents French operational failures well but misses French strategic coherence. He celebrates Vietnamese resistance without analyzing its strategic limitations.

The Luttwak method reveals what Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative cannot: the resistance was not a *war* (in the strategic sense) but a *series of battles* (in the operational sense). The resistance had no grand strategy, no alliance framework, no intelligence apparatus, no logistics system, no diplomatic strategy, no propaganda capability, no succession plan. It was a *movement* — passionate, brave, doomed.

The French won not because they were better fighters (though they were better armed) but because they had a *system* — a grand strategy that integrated military, economic, political, covert, and cultural means toward a long-term objective. The resistance had courage but not system. The asymmetry was decisive.

A complete strategic history of the 1885-1895 resistance would require access to the sources listed above — French diplomatic, military, and intelligence archives, British and Chinese records, and the resistance's own documents. These would allow a reconstruction of the strategic logic that Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative cannot provide.

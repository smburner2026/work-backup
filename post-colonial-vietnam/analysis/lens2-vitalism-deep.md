# Lens 2: Nietzschean Vitalism — Biology, Force, Great-Man Dynamics

**Source:** Phạm Văn Sơn's VSTB translated chapters (ch2-ch6, ch9)
**Prior primer:** `/root/work/post-colonial-vietnam/analysis/vstb-four-lenses.md`
**Method:** Passage-level evidence with line citations
**Foundation:** The literalist interpretation of Nietzschean vitalism — biology, force, life as empirical phenomena, not metaphors

---

## 1. PASSAGE INVENTORY — The Body as Evidence

Phạm Văn Sơn's text is saturated with bodily, physical language. The body is not a metaphor in his narrative — it is the *substrate* of historical force. The following passages illustrate the pattern:

### Endurance and physical will

> "Ton That Thuyet mustered his shattered remnants and stationed them outside the Chương Đức Gate. Sword in hand, he entered the Imperial Palace to summon the emperor and the Three Palaces to flight." (ch3, lines 5-7)

Thuyết's sword is not symbolic — it is the physical instrument of his will. He is a man with a body, a weapon, a force that organizes other bodies.

> "He was still a boy — barely fifteen years of age — and the national calamity had struck with unbearable suddenness. As he mounted the imperial palanquin he tripped on the step and wept aloud, and even the Three Palaces could not restrain their tears." (ch3, lines 15-18)

Hàm Nghi's body fails at the moment of crisis — he trips, he weeps, he is carried. This is the body as evidence: a 15-year-old boy, however legitimate, is not a *force* in the vitalist sense.

> "Thuyết remained behind long enough to order the destruction of Bạch Hồ Bridge, to prevent enemy pursuit." (ch3, lines 20-21)

Physical destruction of physical infrastructure. The resistance is not merely political — it is the manipulation of physical terrain.

### Disease and physical collapse

> "Because so many soldiers and porters succumbed to malaria and dysentery, many deserted and many died, despite the efforts of physicians and the careful attention they gave one another. The pitiful number of those who perished on this desperate road through the deep mountains and remote valleys included men of every rank — from mandarin to common soldier — and not a single burial garment was available to cover their remains. They were hastily buried in shallow graves, and the survivors pressed on, fleeing the claws of the enemy. When the survivors later returned to the central region, their number did not exceed forty." (ch5, lines 40-48)

This is one of the most remarkable passages in the text. Phạm Văn Sơn describes the *biological decimation* of the resistance — malaria, dysentery, death, shallow graves, forty survivors. The vitalist dimension is explicit: bodies are *consumed* by the resistance, and the resistance is therefore *finite* in a way that political narratives rarely acknowledge.

> "King Ham Nghi, unaccustomed to such exposure and hardship, fell ill and had to be carried in a hammock borne by his soldiers." (ch5, lines 5-7)

The king's body is *maladapted* to the resistance. A 15-year-old raised in palace comfort cannot sustain the demands of guerrilla warfare. This is biology, not politics.

### Physical death and combat

> "Hugo himself was struck in the hand and back by Ngoc's marksmen and was forced to order a retreat to Bai-Duc. There the enemy regrouped with the main company and withdrew entirely to Vinh. Hugo died several days later from the effects of the poison." (ch6, lines 30-34)

> "Camus, struck by four arrows, was forced to yield command to Freystatter and withdraw for treatment. As the enemy crossed the Ve River, our forces on the opposite bank unleashed a hail of fire. Camus attempted to wade across but was struck in the belly by a bullet and carried to the next world." (ch6, lines 50-55)

Death is described in physical detail — struck in the hand, struck in the belly, carried to the next world. This is not abstract heroism. It is the *destruction of specific bodies* by specific weapons.

### Material deprivation and physical suffering

> "The common soldiers, by contrast, slept wherever they found themselves, sprawled haphazardly beneath the stilt-houses, their clothes perpetually wet and unable to dry, subsisting on meager rations of husked rice or maize. At times darkness fell while they were still traveling through the forest roads, with no village or hamlet in sight, and the soldiers had to cut down trees and gather leaves to throw up makeshift shelters for the king against the dew and the wind." (ch5, lines 15-22)

The bodies of common soldiers — wet, hungry, exposed — are presented as *evidence of force*, not merely as suffering. They endure. The vitalist reading would note: a population that can produce fighters who endure this is a population with extraordinary *vital reserves*.

## 2. GREAT-MAN TAXONOMY — The Beings Who Produce Force

Phạm Văn Sơn identifies specific individuals as *forces* — beings whose physical, intellectual, and volitional capacities produce historical outcomes. The taxonomy:

### Tôn Thất Thuyết — The Master Force

Thuyết is the central figure across ch2-ch6. He is consistently described in language that emphasizes *force* and *will*:

- "The leader who held in his hands the very soul of the Vietnamese resistance" (ch2)
- "Sword in hand, he entered the Imperial Palace" (ch3) — physical presence
- "Thuyết drew his sword and ordered the soldiers to lift the emperor into the palanquin" (ch3) — physical coercion
- "Thuyết had to intervene personally to calm them" (ch3) — physical intervention in crisis
- "Ton That Thuyet drafted a second Can Vuong edict" (ch5) — intellectual force
- "Ton That Thuyet's intention was to make this region the base for the restoration" (ch5) — strategic will
- "Thuyet had to consult with Admiral Tran-xuan-Soan about seeking aid from China" (ch6) — international vision

Thuyết is presented as a *total* force — physical, intellectual, strategic, volitional. The text consistently returns to him as the engine of the resistance. When he is present, things happen. When he leaves (ch6, heading for China), the resistance weakens.

### Hàm Nghi — The Symbolic Body

Hàm Nghi is a *body* but not a *force*. He is 15 years old, prone to weeping, carried in hammocks, sick with malaria. His body is the locus of legitimacy, not the engine of action. The text is consistent: Hàm Nghi is the *flag* that the resistance fights under, not the *force* that drives it.

The contrast with Thuyết is deliberate. Phạm Văn Sơn is making a vitalist point: the great man is the one who has *force*, not the one who has *legitimacy*. Hàm Nghi has the latter; Thuyết has the former.

### Phan Đình Phùng — The Scholar-Warrior

Phùng is presented as the *ideal* type — the scholar-gentry warrior who combines literary cultivation with martial force. The text treats him with particular reverence. He is the figure who carries the civilizational project into the mountains of Hà Tĩnh and fights for years before being defeated.

The vitalist question: what kind of *being* is Phùng? He is a man in his 50s, a former chief censor, a scholar. And yet he leads armies, builds fortifications, fights the French for nearly a decade. Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative implies that Phùng's force is *exceptional* — a great man produced by the scholar-gentry class at the moment of crisis.

### The Regional Commanders — The Force Multipliers

Phạm Văn Sơn identifies a class of *regional commanders* who multiply Thuyết's force:

- **Đinh Công Tráng** — "a fearless soldier who governed his troops with strict discipline" (ch9, line 60)
- **Phạm Bành** — co-leader of Ba Đình
- **Cao Thắng** — the commander whose death in battle is described in detail
- **Trương Quang Ngọc** — the Mường commander who defeats the French at Cua Khe
- **Lê Trúc** — the Hà Tĩnh commander described by Gosselin as having "acquired, by their noble manner of conducting themselves and by their inalterable fidelity to a cause already desperate, our esteem and forced our admiration" (ch6, line 145)

These are the *beings* through whom the great men's will becomes physical reality. They are presented as men of extraordinary force — discipline, endurance, martial skill. Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative cannot function without them.

### The Mường Warriors — The Ecological Beings

The Mường warriors (ch6, ch9) are presented as a *different kind* of being from the Vietnamese mandarins. They are described in language that emphasizes their adaptation to mountain environments:

> "Truong-quang-Ngoc, accompanied by a contingent of Muong warriors, brought up the rear." (ch6, line 10)

> "Ngoc and his two hundred followers prevailed against the French forces on this occasion because they possessed thorough command of local conditions." (ch6, lines 100-102)

The Mường are presented as *ecologically adapted* beings — they know the mountains, they can use poisoned arrows, they are physically suited to the terrain. Phạm Văn Sơn doesn't develop this point fully, but it is implicit: the Mường are a *kind of being* that the mountains produce, just as the delta produces farmers and the cities produce bureaucrats.

## 3. COUNTER-TYPES — The Beings Who Lack Force

Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative requires *counter-types* — beings who lack the force that produces historical outcomes. These are the figures who fail, surrender, or collaborate.

### Nguyễn Văn Tường — The Failed Body

Tường is the great counter-type. He is described in language that emphasizes *weakness* and *failure*:

- "A weak state must yield to a strong one" (ch3, line 10) — Tường's reported philosophy
- "Nguyễn Văn Tường's reasoning had some truth to it" (ch3, line 65) — acknowledged as having logic, but the logic of *weakness*
- Tường "surrendered" — a single word that captures a *failure of force*
- Tường was exiled to Tahiti and "died there in February 1886" (ch4, line 70) — a death in exile, far from the soil where force might have been exercised
- Tường's property was "ordered confiscated" (ch4, line 80) — material dispossession

The vitalist reading: Tường is a being *without force*. He is intelligent (he plays the political game), he is positioned (he is a regent), but he lacks the *physical-spiritual force* that would have enabled him to choose resistance. Phạm Văn Sơn treats this as a personal moral failure, but a vitalist reading would ask: was Tường simply a *different kind* of being? Was he produced by different conditions? Could a different upbringing, a different body, a different moment have produced a different Tường?

### The French — The Beings Who Collapse

The French are consistently described in terms of *bodily collapse*:

- "Officers and men are utterly exhausted; many are on the verge of madness; everyone is completely demoralized" (ch6, line 50)
- Hugo "died several days later from the effects of the poison" (ch6, line 35)
- Camus was "struck in the belly by a bullet and carried to the next world" (ch6, line 55)
- "Officers and men are utterly exhausted" (ch6) — repeated
- "Generals and admirals came and went; rumors constantly circulated" (ch6) — institutional instability

The French are *beings who break under tropical conditions*. Phạm Văn Sơn presents this as evidence of Vietnamese biological-cultural superiority — the French cannot sustain themselves in the climate, cannot adapt to the terrain, cannot match the vitality of the indigenous population.

The vitalist question: is this a *biological* fact? The French soldiers were not physically inferior to the Vietnamese — they were often better fed, better armed, and medically supported. But they were *ecologically maladapted* — they could not fight effectively in monsoon conditions, they could not navigate the jungles, they could not endure the diseases. The vitalist reading would note: a being is the product of its ecology, and the French were products of a European ecology that did not prepare them for Southeast Asia.

### The Collaborators — The Beings Without Honor

The Vietnamese collaborators (those who served the puppet court, the Catholic village chiefs who supported the French, the mandarins who joined the colonial administration) are presented as beings without *force* in a specific sense. They are not weak physically — many of them were physically robust mandarins. But they lack the *moral force* that produces historical greatness. They are presented as spiritually deficient — they chose the wrong side, and the wrong side is the side that loses.

The vitalist reading: the collaborators are beings who *misread* the historical moment. They thought the French would win, and they positioned themselves accordingly. Phạm Văn Sơn treats this as a *failure of perception* — they could not see that the Vietnamese spirit was stronger than French arms. A vitalist would note: this is a failure of *vital intuition*, the ability to read the forces of history and align with them.

## 4. THE POPULATION QUESTION — What People?

Phạm Văn Sơn presents resistance as emerging from "the people" or "our countrymen." But he never asks the vitalist question: *what people*? How many? What kind? What is the biological-cultural substrate from which the great men emerge?

### The absence of demographic data

The text provides almost no demographic information about the population. We don't know:
- How many people lived in the regions where resistance occurred
- What the age structure was (how many young men of military age?)
- What the health status was (how many could fight after disease exposure?)
- What the population density was (how many could the land support?)
- What the ethnic composition was (how many Vietnamese vs. Mường vs. highland peoples?)

A vitalist reading would require this data. The Nietzschean question is: what *kind* of population produces *what kind* of historical force? Phạm Văn Sơn never asks.

### The implicit population narrative

What Phạm Văn Sơn *does* tell us about the population, we have to extract from incidental details:

- The Mường population of the resistance base area: "Below lived the Kinh people; in the forests lived the Muong compatriots; the total population reached 40,000, with Catholics making up about a quarter." (ch4, line 95)
- The fighting force at Ba Đình: "the combined force of soldiers and laborers at Tân Sở at that time did not exceed a thousand men" (ch3, line 110)
- The army of Đinh Công Tráng: "2,000 troops stationed about half a day's march from Ba Đình" (ch9)
- The army of Trương Quang Ngọc: "Ngoc and his two hundred followers" (ch6)
- The Phan Đình Phùng resistance: fighting for nearly a decade before being defeated

The vitalist observation: the resistance forces were *tiny* relative to the population. Even a generous estimate of total resistance fighters across all the movements Phạm Văn Sơn describes would be in the tens of thousands — out of a population of perhaps 10-15 million in the three regions of Vietnam. The resistance was carried by a *small fraction* of the population.

The Nietzschean question: what selected for this small fraction? Why did *these* people become fighters while the vast majority did not? Phạm Văn Sơn does not ask.

### The reproduction question

The vitalist lens would ask about *biological reproduction*. The resistance consumed bodies — dead in battle, dead of disease, exiled, imprisoned. The survivors were "forty" after the Lao Bảo crossing (ch5). The Cần Vương generation was *biologically diminished*. What did this mean for the next generation? Did the resistance movements have children? Did they replace their losses? Or did they simply *attrit* until they were gone?

Phạm Văn Sơn doesn't ask these questions. His narrative treats the resistance as *spiritual* rather than *biological* — the spirit continues even if the bodies fail. A vitalist reading would note: without biological reproduction, there is no continuation. The resistance ended when its bodies were exhausted.

## 5. ECOLOGICAL DIMENSION — What Beings Does the Environment Produce?

Resistance happens in specific ecologies. Phạm Văn Sơn describes these ecologies in detail, and his descriptions contain a hidden vitalist argument: certain environments produce certain *kinds* of beings.

### The mountains

The mountain environments of Hà Tĩnh, Quảng Bình, and Thanh Hóa are described as producing the Mường warriors and the highland resistance:

> "High mountains and deep forests concealed the Resistance so well that the enemy could hardly find them." (ch4, line 90)

> "Ngoc and his two hundred followers prevailed against the French forces on this occasion because they possessed thorough command of local conditions." (ch6, lines 100-102)

The mountains produce *beings adapted to mountains*. The Mường know the terrain, can navigate the forests, can use the elevation for defensive advantage. They are not *choosing* to fight effectively — they are *being* the kind of beings that mountains produce.

### The deltas and lowlands

The delta and lowland regions (around Huế, around Hà Nội) are described as the space of French military dominance:

> "In Tonkin (Bắc Kỳ); the French could not advance far into the highlands. Moreover, from the Midlands (Trung du) down to the Delta (Trung châu) of Tonkin, insurgent forces were actively competing with each other around France's important strategic positions, from the Red River basin up to Tam Đảo (Vĩnh Yên)." (ch2, line 15)

The deltas are open, accessible, controllable. The French can project force here. The resistance in the deltas is fragmented, dependent on local conditions, harder to sustain.

### The forest frontier

The forest frontier — the transition zone between lowland Vietnamese and highland peoples — is described as the space where the resistance finds its base:

> "King Ham Nghi was based in the upper reaches of these two rivers because it was a fertile and densely populated area. Moreover, from a strategic perspective, the Resistance could from here advance northward or, if necessary, retreat through this point into Laos." (ch4, lines 95-98)

> "Truong-quang-Ngoc, accompanied by a contingent of Muong warriors, brought up the rear." (ch6, line 10)

The forest frontier produces the hybrid beings — the Vietnamese who can operate in highland conditions, the Mường who can interact with lowland political structures. These are the beings who sustain the resistance.

### The ecological vitalist argument

The text implicitly argues: the resistance was strongest where the *ecology produced beings adapted to resistance*. The French could project force into the deltas but not into the mountains. The Mường could fight in the mountains but not in the deltas. The Vietnamese mandarins could organize the resistance politically but not fight effectively in the mountains without Mường allies.

A vitalist reading would push this further: the resistance was *an ecological phenomenon*. It emerged from the specific ecology of the Vietnamese highlands and forest frontiers. The French won not because they were stronger in some absolute sense, but because they could *modify the ecology* — build roads, drain marshes (as at Ba Đình), introduce diseases (malaria, cholera), and degrade the material base of resistance.

## 6. THE VITALIST READING — What Phạm Văn Sơn Knows Without Naming

Phạm Văn Sơn is a *secret vitalist*. His narrative structure assumes a vitalist framework without ever naming it:

**1. The great man thesis is vitalist.** "The purpose of a people is to produce the great man" (Phạm Văn Sơn, quoted in the prior primer) is a vitalist claim. It asserts that history is produced by *certain kinds of beings* — beings of extraordinary force, will, and capacity. Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative proves this thesis on its own terms: the resistance is led by Thuyết, Phùng, and Tráng; the collaborators are the *failures*; the population produces and is shaped by its great men.

**2. The body matters.** Phạm Văn Sơn's attention to physical suffering, disease, death, and bodily endurance is not incidental. It is the *evidence* for his vitalist argument. Bodies are what history acts upon, and what produces history. The malnourished soldiers, the exhausted French, the fifteen-year-old king in his palanquin — these are the beings through whom history moves.

**3. The population has biological characteristics.** The Mường are biologically/ecologically adapted to mountains. The Vietnamese are adapted to lowlands. The French are maladapted to Vietnam. These are not metaphors. They are empirical observations that Phạm Văn Sơn uses to explain historical outcomes.

**4. Force is finite.** The text is full of attrition — bodies consumed, populations diminished, resistance ending. The forty survivors of the Lao Bảo crossing. The collapse of resistance after Tráng's death. The disappearance of the Cần Vương generation. Phạm Văn Sơn knows, without saying, that historical force is *biological* and therefore *finite*.

**Where Phạm Văn Sơn fails the vitalist test:**

**1. He doesn't ask the demographic question.** The text never engages with population as a *biological fact*. How many people? What age structure? What disease burden? The vitalist argument requires this data; Phạm Văn Sơn doesn't have it.

**2. He doesn't ask the selection question.** Why do *these* people become fighters and not *those*? The vitalist answer would involve selection — ecological, social, biological. Phạm Văn Sơn treats it as moral choice.

**3. He doesn't ask the reproduction question.** The resistance consumed bodies. Did it reproduce? Did the next generation carry the force? Phạm Văn Sơn doesn't ask. The vitalist answer is crucial — without reproduction, the force is consumed.

**4. He doesn't ask the substrate question.** What material conditions produce the kind of beings who resist? The vitalist answer would involve nutrition, disease environment, ecological adaptation, genetic factors. Phạm Văn Sơn treats it as spiritual-cultural condition.

## 7. SOURCE GAPS

The following sources would fill the vitalist gaps in Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative:

### A. French colonial census data

- **Recensements de la population** (1880s-1890s) — population figures by region
- **Statistiques coloniales** — published annually by the colonial administration
- These would provide the demographic baseline that Phạm Văn Sơn never engaged with

### B. Ecological and disease studies

- **Malaria studies** in colonial Indochina — the disease environment of the resistance
- **Ecological studies** of the Vietnamese highlands — the ecology that produced the Mường warriors
- **Agricultural studies** — the food base that sustained the resistance

### C. Archaeological evidence

- **Tân Sở excavations** — the physical remains of Thuyết's fortress
- **Ba Đình site archaeology** — the fortifications, the remains of the fighters
- **Hương Khê excavations** — Phan Đình Phùng's base

### D. Anthropological and physical anthropological studies

- **Mường physical anthropology** — the biological characteristics of the highland warriors
- **Vietnamese population studies** — the demographic structure of the resistance population
- **Colonial-era medical reports** — the health and disease status of the population

### E. Genetic and population history studies

- Modern genetic studies of Vietnamese and Mường populations
- Population genetics of the resistance regions
- These would provide the deep-time biological substrate that produced the historical actors

## Conclusion

Phạm Văn Sơn is a *secret vitalist*. His narrative structure assumes that historical force is biological — that certain kinds of beings produce certain kinds of historical outcomes, and that other kinds of beings fail. His attention to bodies, disease, endurance, and death is the *evidence* for this vitalist argument, even when he doesn't name it.

But his vitalism is *unconscious*. He never develops the analytical framework that would let him ask the demographic, ecological, or population-genetic questions that a true vitalist analysis would require. His great-man narrative is an *intuition* of vitalist truth, not a vitalist analysis.

A complete vitalist reading of the VSTB period would reconstruct:
- The demographic substrate (population size, age structure, disease burden)
- The ecological adaptation (mountain, lowland, frontier populations)
- The biological force reserves (nutrition, health, reproductive capacity)
- The selection process (who became a fighter, and why)
- The reproduction question (how did the resistance sustain itself biologically?)

The answer to "what kind of people produced the Cần Vương resistance" is not just "great men like Thuyết and Phùng." It is a complex ecological-demographic question that Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative cannot answer — but which the sources above could begin to address.

# Four-Lens Analysis of Phạm Văn Sơn's VSTB
## Việt Sử Tân Biên — Translated Chapters (ch2, ch3, ch5, ch6, ch9)

**Source material:** Chapters covering 1885–1896 — from the fall of the Huế citadel through the major resistance battles (Ba Đình, Hùng Lĩnh, Hương Khê, Yên Thế).

**Analyst note:** This is a secondary source (Phạm Văn Sơn writing mid-20th century) about a primary historical period. The four lenses are applied to *Phạm Văn Sơn as historian* — what he sees, what he misses, what he emphasizes, and what that reveals about his project.

---

## LENS 1: BURCKHARDTIAN — Cultural History

**Core question:** What is the *spirit* of the era Phạm Văn Sơn is describing — and what is the spirit of the era *he is writing from*?

### What Phạm Văn Sơn sees

He sees a civilizational crisis. The repeated framing is not "Vietnam lost a battle" but "Vietnam faced an existential threat to its cultural identity." The language is consistently civilizational:

- The French are not just an army — they are a force that threatens to erase Vietnamese civilization. Phrases like "a thousand-year history of fighting for freedom and independence" (ch2) and "the national spirit, built upon the two words 'freedom' and 'independence'" (ch9) are not strategic observations. They are cultural-philosophical claims about what Vietnam *is*.

- The resistance is framed as a *cultural* act, not merely a military one. The Cần Vương edict (ch3) is presented as a document of civilizational self-defense — it invokes historical Chinese dynasties (Tấn, Táng) to place Vietnam within a broader Confucian civilizational order. Phạm Văn Sơn reproduces this edict at length, signaling that he considers it a defining cultural document.

- The scholar-gentry (sĩ-phu) are presented as the carriers of civilization. When they rally to the Cần Vương banner, it is framed as the cultural elite fulfilling their natural role. When they collaborate, it is framed as civilizational betrayal.

### What Phạm Văn Sơn misses (or suppresses)

The Burckhardtian cross-section would ask: what was the *actual* cultural texture of Vietnamese society in 1885? Phạm Văn Sơn presents a monolithic "Vietnamese spirit" — but the reality was far more fragmented:

- **Religious diversity.** The text mentions Catholic vs. non-Catholic violence ("Pacify the West, Kill the Leftists" — ch5) but treats this as a side effect of resistance, not as a fundamental cultural fault line. The Catholic communities were a distinct cultural entity within Vietnam — some collaborated, some resisted, some were caught in between. Phạm Văn Sơn flattens this into "collaborators vs. patriots."

- **Ethnic fragmentation.** The Mường people are mentioned as allies (ch6, ch9) but always in relation to the Vietnamese court. Their own cultural logic — their relationship to the land, their political structures, their reasons for fighting — is never explored. They are instrumentalized as "our Mường compatriots" rather than understood as a distinct people with their own civilizational project.

- **Regional differences.** The text moves between Bắc Kỳ, Trung Kỳ, and Nam Kỳ as if they were unified. But Cochinchina (Nam Kỳ) had been under French control since 1862 — twenty-three years by the time of these events. The cultural experience of a southern Vietnamese was radically different from that of a central court mandarin. Phạm Văn Sơn's "Vietnam" is essentially the Nguyễn court and its hinterland.

### The cross-section: what does this reveal about Phạm Văn Sơn's own moment?

Phạm Văn Sơn was writing in the mid-20th century — after the First Indochina War, during or after the partition of Vietnam. His "Vietnamese spirit" narrative serves a specific function in *his* moment: it provides a usable past for a newly independent (or soon-to-be-independent) nation. The monolithic "Vietnamese civilization under threat" framing is *exactly* what a post-colonial nation-state needs to justify its existence.

This is not necessarily dishonest — but it is a choice. A Burckhardtian reading would note that Phạm Văn Sơn is doing what Burckhardt warned about: using history in the *monumental* mode (to inspire) rather than the *critical* mode (to understand). He is building a national myth, not a cultural cross-section.

**Verdict:** Phạm Văn Sơn captures the *elite* cultural spirit well — the Confucian scholar-gentry worldview, the court's sense of civilizational crisis. He systematically misses the cultural lives of everyone outside that elite circle. His "Vietnam" is a top-down construction that serves mid-20th-century nation-building as much as it describes 19th-century reality.

## LENS 2: NIETZSCHEAN VITALISM — Biology, Life, Force

**Core question:** What kind of *being* produces resistance — and does Phạm Văn Sơn understand this?

### The great-man frame

Phạm Văn Sơn is explicitly a great-man historian. The narrative is driven by individuals:

- **Tôn Thất Thuyết** — the driving force. Every major decision is his. He plans the attack on Huế, he drafts the Cần Vương edict, he leads the flight north, he makes the strategic decisions at Tân Sở. When he is present, things happen. When he leaves (ch6, heading for China), the resistance weakens.

- **Hàm Nghi** — the symbolic center. He is 15 years old, weeping in his palanquin, tripping on steps. He is not a *force* — he is a *symbol* that Thuyết wields. The edicts are Thuyết's composition, sealed with Thuyết's authority.

- **Phạm Bành, Đinh Công Tráng, Phan Đình Phùng** — the regional commanders. Each is presented as a man of extraordinary personal force who generates resistance through sheer will.

- **Nguyễn Văn Tường** — the counter-type. He surrenders, collaborates, becomes a puppet. His "weakness" is presented as a personal moral failure, not a structural response to impossible conditions.

### The Vitalist question: where does the force come from?

Phạm Văn Sơn oscillates between two answers:

1. **Force comes from the great man.** Thuyết *is* the resistance. When he is present, the army holds together. When he leaves, it dissolves. The Mường warriors fight brilliantly (ch6) but only because Thuyết organized them. The Cần Vương movement spreads because Thuyết drafted the edict. This is the great-man thesis in its purest form.

2. **Force comes from the people.** The text repeatedly emphasizes popular resistance — the scholars rallying, the Mường fighters, the villagers who shelter the king. The Ba Đình battle (ch9) is presented as a *popular* achievement: "only the elderly, women, and children were evacuated; all able-bodied men remained behind, resolved to die fighting."

These two frames are in tension. If resistance flows from the great man, then it is fragile — one death ends it. If resistance flows from the people, then the great man is merely a catalyst. Phạm Văn Sơn never resolves this tension because resolving it would undermine either the great-man narrative or the popular-nationalist narrative. He needs both.

### What the Vitalist lens reveals about biology and force

The text is full of bodily, physical language:

- Thuyết's physical endurance: he marches 70km on foot, he personally calms soldiers, he "never hesitated to execute men" but turns away at the farewell scene (ch3). His body is an instrument of will.

- The soldiers' bodies: they sleep in the rain, they eat "husked rice or maize," they die of malaria and dysentery, "their clothes perpetually wet" (ch5). The physical suffering is presented as *proof* of spiritual strength — a very Nietzschean move.

- Hàm Nghi's body: he is sick, exhausted, carried in hammocks. His body fails where Thuyết's does not. This is not incidental — it reinforces the hierarchy of force. The king is a symbol; Thuyết is the living force.

- The French bodies: they are "utterly exhausted," "on the verge of madness," "completely demoralized" (ch6). Phạm Văn Sơn quotes French sources describing their own physical and mental collapse. This is a vitalist argument: the French are *weaker beings* in this environment.

### What Phạm Văn Sơn misses

The Nietzschean vitalist lens would ask: what about the biological substrate itself? The population? The ecology? Phạm Văn Sơn never discusses:

- **Demographics.** How many people actually lived in these regions? What was the population density? How many could the land support? Resistance requires bodies — where did they come from?

- **Disease ecology.** Malaria kills more people than bullets in this narrative. The French suffer from it. The Vietnamese suffer from it. But Phạm Văn Sơn treats disease as a background condition, not as a force that shapes outcomes. A vitalist reading would ask: how did the disease environment *select* for certain kinds of fighters?

- **Agriculture and subsistence.** The resistance depends on rice — "granaries of rice lay hidden in the forest" (ch3), the king insists on paying the Mường for provisions (ch6). But the relationship between agricultural capacity and military capacity is never analyzed. How much rice could Tân Sở produce? How many fighters could the Thanh Hóa region sustain?

- **Ethnic biology.** The Mường are presented as naturally suited to mountain warfare — they know the terrain, they use poisoned arrows, they are physically tough. But Phạm Văn Sơn never asks *why* the Mường are like this. Is it ecology? Culture? Something else? A Nietzschean vitalist would press on this: the Mường are a different *kind* of being from the court mandarins, and that difference matters.

**Verdict:** Phạm Văn Sơn has an intuitive vitalist sense — he understands that force is physical, bodily, not just ideological. But he lacks the analytical framework to develop it. His great-man narrative is the closest he gets to a vitalist theory: great men are beings of extraordinary force, and history is what their force produces. The population-level biology — the substrate from which great men emerge — is invisible to him.

## LENS 3: CLASS/MATERIAL — Property, Class, Who Benefits

**Core question:** Who owned what, who worked for whom, and whose interests does Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative serve?

### The class structure Phạm Văn Sơn describes (implicitly)

The VSTB presents a three-tier social order:

1. **The imperial court and mandarins** — the ruling class. They own political authority, land, and cultural legitimacy. Tôn Thất Thuyết, Nguyễn Văn Tường, the dowager empresses. Their interests are the interests of the state.

2. **The scholar-gentry (sĩ-phu)** — the educated elite. They own cultural capital (degrees, literary skill) and local influence. They are the ones who "rally to the Cần Vương banner." Their interests align with the court's — they are the court's recruitment pool.

3. **The common people (dân)** — peasants, soldiers, laborers. They own nothing. They fight, they die, they carry rice, they build fortifications. Their interests are never articulated — they are assumed to be identical with the court's.

### What Phạm Văn Sơn emphasizes

He consistently centers the first two tiers. The narrative is driven by mandarins and scholars. When common people appear, they are:

- **Nameless masses:** "soldiers who had marched from Văn Xá had nothing to eat" (ch3). They are a collective, never individuals.
- **Instruments of the great men's will:** Thuyết "mobilized up to two thousand laborers to dig trenches" (ch3). The laborers have no agency — they are mobilized.
- **Symbols of popular support:** "the scholars and notables of Bình Định province also rose up" (ch5). The people's participation validates the leaders' cause.

### What Phạm Văn Sơn misses (or suppresses)

The class lens would ask: whose material interests were at stake in this resistance?

- **Land.** The French colonial project was fundamentally about land acquisition — rubber plantations, rice exports, mining concessions. The scholar-gentry resisted because French control threatened their landholdings. The peasants resisted because French taxation and corvée labor devastated their subsistence. These are *different* material interests, but Phạm Văn Sơn treats them as a unified "national" interest.

- **The Catholic communities.** The text mentions "Pacify the West, Kill the Leftists" (ch5) — the anti-Catholic dimension of the resistance. Catholics were disproportionately drawn from lower social strata (converts seeking protection from mandarin exploitation). The anti-Catholic violence was partly *class* violence — the scholar-gentry attacking a community that had escaped their control. Phạm Văn Sơn treats this as patriotic fervor, not class conflict.

- **The ethnic minorities.** The Mường, the Tai peoples, the highland groups — they had their own land, their own political structures, their own relationship to the Vietnamese state. When Thuyết "bought" elephants from them or recruited their warriors, he was engaging in a *material* transaction. The Mường fought for pay, for political alliance, for their own strategic reasons — not out of "patriotism" for a Vietnamese state that had historically marginalized them.

- **The Vietnamese collaborators.** Nguyễn Văn Tường is presented as a traitor. But what were his material calculations? He had a family, property, a position. Surrendering meant preserving all of that. Resisting meant losing everything. Phạm Văn Sơn treats this as moral failure rather than rational class behavior.

### The material base of the resistance

The resistance required:

- **Rice:** "10,000 bushels of rice from the state granaries" (ch3). "Granaries of rice lay hidden in the forest" (ch3). Rice is the material base — without it, no army. But who produced the rice? Peasants. Who decided how it was distributed? Mandarins. The material flow is invisible in the narrative.

- **Silver:** "300,000 taels" sent to Tân Sở, "700,000 taels" remaining in the treasury (ch3). "Hundreds of chests of silver ingots" captured by the French (ch3). Money is a constant presence, but its *source* — taxation, land revenue, trade — is never discussed.

- **Weapons:** Muskets, swords, poisoned arrows, "the simplest of means" (ch9). The resistance fought with inferior weapons because it had an inferior material base. Phạm Văn Sơn presents this as spiritual triumph — "often we defeat them with the simplest of means" (ch9) — but a materialist reading would note that inferior weapons meant higher casualties, longer campaigns, and eventual defeat.

### Who does the narrative serve?

Phạm Văn Sơn's narrative serves the *mandarin class* — the class from which he himself emerged. His "Vietnam" is a Vietnam led by scholars and mandarins, fighting for a civilization they defined and owned. The peasants are the backdrop. The ethnic minorities are the auxiliaries. The collaborators are the deviants.

This is not unusual for nationalist historiography — the French did the same thing with their own elite. But it means that the *material* reality of colonial Vietnam — the exploitation, the land seizures, the corvée labor, the famine — is largely absent from the narrative.

**Verdict:** Phạm Văn Sơn has a blind spot for class. He sees political conflict (Vietnam vs. France) but not social conflict (mandarins vs. peasants, Buddhists vs. Catholics, lowlanders vs. highlanders). His narrative serves the elite class from which he came and for whom he was writing. A materialist reading would reconstruct the class dynamics he suppresses — and would find that the "national unity" he celebrates was always fractured by material interest.

## LENS 4: COVERT+LUTTWAK — Strategic Logic, Stated vs. Actual

**Core question:** What strategic logic is operating beneath the surface — and does Phạm Văn Sơn see it?

### What Phạm Văn Sơn narrates well

He is surprisingly good on *French* strategic dysfunction:

- **De Courcy's blunders.** The text is full of French operational failures — the failed banquet trap, the disastrous pursuit through jungle, the quarreling generals. Phạm Văn Sơn quotes French sources (Gosselin, Delvaux, Masson) documenting their own incompetence. This is effective because it uses the enemy's own words against them.

- **The French political-military divide.** The text captures the tension between Paul-Bert (civil administration) and the military commanders. The Ministry of War reducing forces, the generals quarreling, the civil officials undermining the military — this is a real strategic dysfunction that Phạm Văn Sơn documents well.

- **The French information problem.** De Courcy "knew nothing whatsoever about Annam" (quoting Gosselin, ch2). The French repeatedly walked into ambushes because they didn't understand the terrain. This is a strategic intelligence failure that Phạm Văn Sơn correctly identifies.

### What Phạm Văn Sơn misses

The Luttwak lens would ask about *grand strategy* — the integrated system of political, economic, military, and covert means working toward a long-term objective. Phạm Văn Sơn sees battles, not strategy:

- **French grand strategy was working.** The "incremental encroachment" (tầm thực) that Phạm Văn Sơn mentions (ch2) was not a series of blunders — it was a deliberate, decades-long strategy of salami-slicing. Each treaty, each "temporary" occupation, each "advisory" mission was a step in a planned sequence. Phạm Văn Sơn treats each French action as an isolated event rather than as a phase in a coherent strategic plan.

- **The covert dimension.** The text mentions Bishop Caspar "spying for the French" (glossary) and the French using Nguyễn Văn Tường as a puppet. But these are presented as individual acts of treachery, not as elements of a *covert apparatus*. The French intelligence network in Vietnam — missionaries, traders, converts, informers — was a strategic asset that operated for decades before the military conquest. Phạm Văn Sơn barely mentions it.

- **The strategic value of the puppet government.** When the French install Đồng Khánh and use Tường to undermine Hàm Nghi, this is a *strategic* operation — regime change as a tool of conquest. Phạm Văn Sơn treats it as moral betrayal rather than strategic logic. A Luttwak reading would note: the French understood that a compliant government was worth more than a thousand soldiers, and they acted accordingly.

- **Second-order effects.** The Cần Vương edict triggered a wave of uprisings — but also a wave of anti-Catholic violence that alienated potential allies and gave the French justification for punitive expeditions. Phạm Văn Sơn celebrates the uprisings without analyzing their strategic consequences. Did the "Pacify the West, Kill the Leftists" slogan help or hurt the resistance in the long run? The text doesn't ask.

### The gap between narrative and strategy

Phạm Văn Sơn narrates *events*. He does not analyze *systems*. The French colonial project was a system — military, economic, political, covert, cultural — working toward a defined objective (resource extraction and geopolitical positioning in Southeast Asia). Phạm Văn Sơn sees the military dimension clearly but misses the system as a whole.

Conversely, the Vietnamese resistance was also a system — but one with far fewer resources and far less coherence. The Cần Vương movement was a *call to arms*, not a *strategy*. It had no unified command, no logistics system, no intelligence apparatus, no diplomatic strategy. It relied on the personal force of individuals (Thuyết, Phùng, Thám) and the spontaneous response of the population. This is why it lasted two decades but ultimately failed — it was a *movement*, not a *strategy*.

Phạm Văn Sơn presents this as tragic heroism — brave men fighting against impossible odds. A Luttwak reading would note: heroism is not strategy. The resistance needed a grand strategic framework — alliance with China, diplomatic recognition, intelligence networks, logistics — and it never had one. Thuyết's journey to China (ch6) was an attempt to find one, but it came too late and achieved too little.

**Verdict:** Phạm Văn Sơn is a good *narrator* of military events but a poor *analyst* of strategic systems. He documents French dysfunction well but misses French strategic coherence. He celebrates Vietnamese resistance without analyzing its strategic limitations. The gap between his narrative and the actual strategic logic is where the real history lies — and it is a gap he never fills.

## SYNTHESIS: What is Phạm Văn Sơn building?

Across all four lenses, a consistent picture emerges:

**Phạm Văn Sơn is building a national myth, not a historical analysis.**

He is doing what nationalist historians in the post-colonial world普遍 do: constructing a usable past that legitimizes the present. His "Vietnam" is:

- **Culturally monolithic** (Burckhardtian blind spot) — one civilization, one spirit, one people
- **Led by great men** (Vitalist framing) — Thuyết, Phùng, Thám as embodiments of national will
- **Unified across classes** (Class blind spot) — mandarins and peasants fighting together for the same cause
- **Heroically tragic** (Strategic blind spot) — brave but doomed, noble but outmatched

This is not *wrong* — it is *partial*. The four lenses reveal what is missing:

| Lens | What Phạm Văn Sơn sees | What he misses |
|------|------------------------|----------------|
| **Burckhardt** | Elite Confucian culture under threat | Religious diversity, ethnic fragmentation, regional differences |
| **Nietzschean** | Individual force and will | Population biology, ecology, subsistence, the substrate from which great men emerge |
| **Class** | National unity against France | Land relations, class conflict, material interests, the exploitation within Vietnamese society |
| **Covert+Luttwak** | Battles and blunders | Grand strategy, covert apparatus, systemic logic, second-order effects |

**The critical question for source-finding:** Can we reconstruct what Phạm Văn Sơn suppresses? Can we find:

1. **For Burckhardt:** Accounts from non-elite Vietnamese — peasants, ethnic minorities, Catholics — that reveal a *different* cultural experience of the same period?
2. **For Nietzschean Vitalism:** Demographic data, ecological studies, archaeological evidence that would let us assess the biological substrate of resistance?
3. **For Class:** Land records, tax records, French colonial economic reports that show the material interests at stake?
4. **For Covert+Luttwak:** French strategic documents, intelligence reports, diplomatic cables that reveal the grand strategic framework Phạm Văn Sơn misses?

The answer is almost certainly yes — these sources exist. The question is whether we can access them.

## NEXT STEPS: Source-Finding Strategy

### Lens 1 (Burckhardt) — Cultural sources

- **French colonial ethnography:** The EFEO (École française d'Extrême-Orient) produced detailed ethnographic studies of Vietnamese society in this period. These would reveal the cultural diversity Phạm Văn Sơn suppresses.
- **Catholic mission records:** The missions catholiques archives in Aix-en-Provence hold records from Vietnamese Catholic communities — their own account of the "Pacify the West" violence.
- **Mường oral traditions:** If accessible, Mường oral histories would provide a non-Vietnamese perspective on the same events.
- **Chinese-language sources:** The Qing court's records on Vietnam (available in the 大清實錄 or through Chinese diplomatic archives) would provide a non-Vietnamese, non-French perspective.

### Lens 2 (Vitalism) — Biological/demographic sources

- **French colonial census data:** The first French censuses of Vietnam date from the 1880s-1890s. These would provide population figures, settlement patterns, and demographic data.
- **Ecological studies:** Modern studies of Vietnamese ecology, disease environments, and agricultural capacity could be retroactively applied to assess the material conditions of resistance.
- **Archaeological evidence:** Tân Sở, Ba Đình, and other resistance sites have been archaeologically investigated. The physical remains would reveal the material conditions of the fighters.

### Lens 3 (Class) — Material/economic sources

- **French colonial economic reports:** The French kept detailed records of land ownership, taxation, and economic output. These would reveal the material interests at stake.
- **Vietnamese land records:** Nguyễn dynasty land grants and tax records (available in the Imperial Archives in Huế or through EFEO microfilms) would show the class structure Phạm Văn Sơn suppresses.
- **Rubber plantation records:** The colonial plantation economy was the material base of French control. Records from the major plantations would reveal the economic dynamics of colonization.

### Lens 4 (Covert+Luttwak) — Strategic/intelligence sources

- **French diplomatic archives:** The Quai d'Orsay archives hold the diplomatic correspondence that reveals French grand strategy in Vietnam.
- **French military intelligence:** The Service historique de la Défense holds military intelligence reports from the period.
- **British diplomatic records:** Britain was a major player in Southeast Asian geopolitics. The National Archives (Kew) hold British diplomatic correspondence on French Indochina.
- **Chinese diplomatic records:** The Qing court's negotiations with France over Vietnam are documented in Chinese diplomatic archives.

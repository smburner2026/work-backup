CHAPTER VI

THE FRENCH PURSUE KING HAM NGHI

THE MILITARY ACHIEVEMENTS OF THE MUONG COMPATRIOTS

In the year At-dau (1885), the king departed from Au-Son and made his way to Qui-Dat, a village situated along the Van River. The sovereign wore his yellow robe and was borne upon the royal palanquin with its dragon shafts, attended by four bodyguards. Behind him came the Great General Ton-that-Thuyet and Ta-quan Tran-xuan-Soan at the head of one hundred soldiers bearing swords and muskets, fifty bảo-đài [royal parasols], three war elephants, and five horses. Truong-quang-Ngoc, accompanied by a contingent of Muong warriors, brought up the rear. (1)

Upon reaching Qui-Dat, the king took up residence in the home of Dinh-Hien; Ton-that-Thuyet lodged at the house of Dinh-Doi; and Tran-xuan-Soan quartered his men at the home of Dinh-Trong. The royal party rested here for three days, then moved on to Co-Liem, only to turn back to Xom Lim in the village of Ba-Vuong. After eight days, intelligence reached them that the French had advanced from Bai-Duc to Tranh, whereupon the king was obliged to flee hastily into the Ma-Rai mountains in Kim-Linh canton.

The following evening, a large French force descended upon Ba-Vuong. The commanding officer was Captain Hugo, accompanied by Lieutenants Gayge and Bellomy. This same force had earlier skirmished with the royal party at Ke-Bai, a region comprising the terrain between Vinh, Ha-Tinh, and Bai-Duc. The king's troops were defeated, and the enemy pursued them to Ba-Vuong. The villagers fled into the forest, leaving behind only an old man named Co-Tu, who, being too feeble to escape, was captured by the enemy. Under severe beatings, he was compelled to reveal the king's direction of flight.

Hugo pursued as far as the Lap-Cap mountains. There, bullets and poisoned arrows rained down like a storm through rocky crevices and thick undergrowth. More than half the French force was killed or wounded. Hugo himself was struck in the hand and back by Ngoc's marksmen and was forced to order a retreat to Bai-Duc (January 31, 1886). There the enemy regrouped with the main company and withdrew entirely to Vinh. Hugo died several days later from the effects of the poison.

At the Cua Ke estuary, another French mobile column was annihilated the following day. This victory encouraged the populace to join the resistance in such numbers that Ton-that-Thuyet was obliged to reassign troops to the lowlands, as the posts in the Thanh-Thuy region were now over-supplied with men.

After this great victory, the royal party established a fortified camp at Cua Khe, raising ramparts two meters high for temporary shelter. The Muong people brought provisions, though King Ham Nghi insisted on paying them.

In early 1886, Lieutenants Camus and Freystatter led a force from Ha-Tinh up the Nai River. They set out on the tenth day of the first lunar month, passing through Toc-Ky and Lang-Mai. Approaching the Cua Ve estuary, they learned that the resistance forces had been encamped there the previous night.

The French proceeded with extreme caution, advancing slowly while reconnoitering. On the 17th, battle commenced and continued throughout the day. Camus, struck by four arrows, was forced to yield command to Freystatter and withdraw for treatment. As the enemy crossed the Ve River, our forces on the opposite bank unleashed a hail of fire. Camus attempted to wade across but was struck in the belly by a bullet and carried to the next world. Freystatter, seeing the hopelessness of his position, withdrew to Bai-Duc and then to Nghe An.

During this victorious period, popular morale rose dramatically. Ton-that-Thuyet dispatched victory proclamations throughout the land, enforced strict discipline among the troops to prevent the plunder of civilian property (oxen, buffalo, chickens, pigs), suppressed rebel chieftains and bandits, eliminated Vietnamese collaborators, and encouraged the people to dig irrigation canals and plant rice to secure food supplies.

Despite these reverses, the French remained determined to take revenge and resolved to capture the fortified camp at Cua Khe. They divided their forces into two columns: Major Plagnol led a force from Bai-Duc in a direct assault, while Major Pelletier advanced from Ha-Tinh to Qui-Hop and then marched down to Cua Khe. The enemy force numbered two brigades. The Muong warriors behind their ramparts loosed a devastating fire, inflicting heavy losses upon the French. Yet, because their own forces were few and their weapons limited, Ngoc dared not commit to a sustained engagement. He removed the king to the Ma-Rai mountains.

(History of the Anti-Invasion Struggle, by T.V.G., records: "At the end of the first lunar month of 1886, Camus attacked Thanh-Thuy but was roundly defeated by our forces. The enemy suffered terribly. By the time they recovered their wits, our troops had already scaled Mount Ma-Rai and crossed the Nai River — that is, the middle reaches of the Gianh River. As the enemy pursued, reaching midstream, our forces ambushed them from the opposite bank, killing a great many of their soldiers, who drowned in the river. Camus took an arrow in the shoulder and withdrew to the opposite bank. The battle continued; the enemy attempted to cross again but was driven back with even greater force. Their commander was killed outright in the river. The survivors fled straight to Quang-Khe.")

Thus did our forces win three consecutive victories within half a month, at a distance of several dozen leagues from Cua Khe. The enemy captured some horses, swords, muskets, and items of the king's clothing. It is noteworthy that prior to the events of mid-November 1885, the French had achieved no significant result whatsoever — for wherever they went, they massacred and violated the people. Major Gregoire's column was annihilated, as previously described. Pelletier's column, pursuing King Ham Nghi southward toward Bai-Duc and the Cua Ve region (the basin of the Gianh River, where the Rao-Nay and Rao-Nan tributaries converge), likewise gathered nothing, for they lacked a coordinated plan, each man acting independently in the scramble for individual glory. Indeed, the defeats of Hugo, Camus, and Freystatter were so costly precisely because they relied on numerical superiority and superior firepower, recklessly plunging into dense jungle without experience of the Vietnamese battlefield and without understanding of our country's terrain and natural features.

Ngoc and his two hundred followers prevailed against the French forces on this occasion because they possessed thorough command of local conditions. Had the resistance been better supplied with men and matériel — had the colonial army not yet learned the full measure of the difficulties ahead — we would not have been obliged to adopt the strategy of fighting while retreating. The king never remained long in any one place: sometimes he hid in the mountains of Thanh-Cuoc, sometimes he sheltered in Ma-Rai, occasionally he settled for a time at Cua Khe — yet he never left the region of Nghe-Tinh-Binh and Cam Mon (Laos).

Though defeated in three successive engagements, Major Pelletier still hoped to capture the resistance king before Metzinger's column could be fully assembled. For three consecutive weeks, Pelletier's two hundred and fifty men, together with Captain Parreaux's company, pushed through thick vegetation, climbing passes and traversing the Ngan Sau River into Tuyen Hoa, clashing repeatedly with the resistance forces. The fiercest engagement occurred at Trai Na in late January 1886. The French struck directly at the resistance troops while simultaneously deploying flanking parties, threatening to encircle our forces. Finding himself caught between two lines of enemy fire, Admiral Tran-xuan-Soan raised his megaphone and called out to the native auxiliaries fighting for the French:

"Sons of the North! Do not fight your king, for the king is your father. Turn your weapons against the Westerners! Shoot them down — they are not numerous! Bring us their heads and claim your reward!"

After this engagement, five French soldiers lay dead and seventeen wounded. The king's forces also lost several men. The royal palanquin and a quantity of rice fell into enemy hands, and one of Ton-that-Thuyet's horses had to be abandoned. A Muong porter carried the king to safety. This occurred at the end of the 12th lunar month of the At-dau year.

The defeat at Trai Na convinced Ton-that-Thuyet that the resistance could not defeat the French through its own resources alone. He consulted with Admiral Tran-xuan-Soan about seeking aid from China. The two men took leave of the king and set out for Thanh Hoa to find a route to the Middle Kingdom.

During this period — the first ten days of February 1886 — French forces were actively scouring the countryside in search of King Ham Nghi, but without result. Thereafter, the French withdrew to rest and regroup at Quang Khe (at the mouth of the Gianh River) and at Dong Hoi.

At this time the French had numerous units of various arms stationed in Quang Binh under the command of Lieutenant Colonels Metzinger and Mignot. These commanders were not in harmony with each other. Colonel Brissaud was occupied supervising the Southern-region light infantry. Consequently, Major General Prudhomme personally traveled to Dong Hoi on February 16, 1886, to take command of the pacification campaign, to encourage the resistance to surrender, and to oversee the pursuit of King Ham Nghi. Lieutenant General Warnet reprimanded him sharply, declaring that the above measures lacked sound justification, whereupon Prudhomme was removed from his post as Resident General in Hue. Stung by this humiliation, Prudhomme requested to be relieved of all military authority in Vietnam. Major General Munier succeeded him. But Lieutenant General Warnet himself did not long remain secure, for the French government dispatched Paul-Bert to replace him, and Warnet was obliged to transfer command of the army to Lieutenant General Jamont.

During this period, the French forces not only received no reinforcements but were actually reduced by the Ministry of War to a single division (comprising three brigades by May 1886). The internal state of the Far Eastern army at this time was truly lamentable. Generals and admirals came and went; rumors constantly circulated from France about impending peace in the North and Central regions; the generals quarreled among themselves, vying for position between the various branches of service. Civil officials and military commanders were perpetually at odds, creating an atmosphere of confusion and fear. An examination of the correspondence of the colonial French reveals pitiable passages such as:

"Officers and men are utterly exhausted; many are on the verge of madness; everyone is completely demoralized."

Then Paul-Bert arrived in Hanoi on April 8, 1886. A new era began. The French military in Indochina was now regarded as the principal obstacle to the civil administration's pacification plans. On April 13, 1886, Paul-Bert declared:

"The governance of a part of the Far Eastern army is not beyond our capacities, but we must comply with the demands of public opinion and the financial situation. Do we desire security? Security has been guaranteed. What more could we want? From now on, only garrison troops are necessary."

Subsequently, the battalions of Vietnamese native auxiliaries were dissolved and reconverted into light infantry on May 14, 1886, receiving appropriate training.

During this period, the resistance forces attacked the Sao-Cat mission station two kilometers from Dong Hoi, killing forty Catholic converts from Sao-Bun and burning the church. Even the citadel of Dong Hoi itself was twice assaulted, though the resistance did not succeed (August 5, 1886). In Quang Binh, French forces at this time maintained only small garrisons at Quang Khe, Cho Don, and Cua Ron.

At the end of December 1886, French African troops and light infantry conducted mobile operations around Dong Hoi province, perpetrating massacres. Battalion Commander Bertrand reported to his superiors that there was tranquility from the My Tho post (44 kilometers south of the provincial capital of Dong Hoi) to Ke Bang (13 kilometers to the north).

Several months earlier, in an effort to appease the resistance, the Vietnamese court — acting on the recommendation of Major General Prudhomme — had dispatched Minister Phan-dinh-Binh and Vo-ba-Liem to pacify Quang Binh after King Ham Nghi had moved from Ha Tinh to hide in the highlands of that province. The scholars-literati attacked in many places; district and prefectural officials fled in great numbers.

With the spirit of the citizenry and intelligentsia so aroused, it was inevitable that Phan-dinh-Binh and Vo-ba-Liem should fail. More than that, the resistance captured Vo-ba-Liem and executed him, demonstrating their resolute opposition to the colonial regime and its traitorous followers.

At the beginning of 1887, perceiving that his troops were weary, Ton-that-Dam granted them leave to return to their villages for the Tet holiday. Garrisons and posts were consequently much depleted. Mouteaux, learning of this through intelligence, divided his forces into two columns and struck at Yen Luong village (on the Rao Nam River), which served as the base of Tri-su Nguyen-pham-Tuan. The guide was a servant named Dua. The enemy marched at night for stealth, crossing a range of formidable mountains.

Upon arrival, the resistance forces were just sitting down to their morning meal. Mouteaux's soldiers pressed close to the bamboo hedge surrounding Tuan's dwelling before those inside realized something was amiss. Tuan and a few close comrades put up a desperate defense, but being taken by surprise, they were overpowered and captured. Tuan cursed the enemy, hoping to be killed on the spot, but Mouteaux instead transported him and several subordinate commanders to Minh Cam for interrogation. Not a single man would speak. Tuan died of his wounds. His comrades were executed at the Minh Cam market.

Four days later, however, the resistance exacted swift vengeance. They raided the French post, capturing Dua the informer and killing him on the spot. The reason Dua was punished so summarily was his betrayal. Tuan had previously employed him as a double agent to infiltrate Mouteaux's camp, but Dua had subsequently turned against the resistance. With Nguyen-pham-Tuan eliminated, the insurrectionary movement in Quang Binh was visibly affected. Emboldened by this success, the enemy pressed their advantage, attacking the upper reaches of the Gianh River — a region they had long feared to enter.

On the night of the 18th-19th of the sixth month of that same year (1887), spies once again led enemy commandos to the Thanh Thuy mountain region, where De Truc was then established in his base. The enemy, again exploiting the element of surprise, surrounded the resistance forces and captured nearly everyone, including the wives and children of Le Truc and Lanh-binh Pham-Tuong (the enemy feared Tuong even more than Truc, for he had fought the French ferociously for two consecutive years). Truc himself escaped. Mouteaux executed Tuong at Tho Ngoa village — Tuong's native place. The remaining prisoners, including women and children, were all killed at Minh Cam. Truc continued to resist the French (1), but the strength of the resistance diminished daily, and he could no longer mount major battles. He employed guerrilla tactics, raiding numerous district and prefectural seats. Mouteaux, enraged, carried out merciless massacres on the orders of the Colonel commanding the Central Region Division:

"Consider this an order: whenever prisoners are to be sent to Hue, by the time they reach you, it will already be too late."

Later, on the strength of his record of unrestrained killing, Mouteaux was awarded the Kim Khanh decoration by Emperor Dong Khanh before departing for France.

(1) Concerning Admiral Le Truc and Commissioner Ton-that-Dam, M. Ch. Gosselin — who directed numerous mopping-up operations during the years of suppressing the Can-Vuong forces and searching for King Ham Nghi — was compelled to acknowledge:

"Despite our activities and incessant searches, despite the cruel fatigues imposed upon our soldiers, it was impossible for us to lay hands upon these two adversaries, who — we must say to their glory — while combating us without respite, despite the feeble means at their disposal, had acquired, by their noble manner of conducting themselves and by their inalterable fidelity to a cause already desperate, our esteem and forced our admiration."

CHAPTER VII

THE LAST STAGE OF THE PATRIOT KING: FROM QUANG BINH TO ALGIERS

As the foregoing pages of history have shown, from the beginning of 1886 the French army pursued King Ham Nghi with extreme severity along the routes of Quang Binh into Ha Tinh. Although Lieutenants Camus and Freystatter had been wounded by Truong-quang-Ngoc's Muong forces near the Ve River estuary and compelled to withdraw to Bai Duc and Nghe An province, King Ham Nghi could not move far, for the French had deployed throughout the region, from the coast to the very edge of the forests and mountains. The net tightened daily. After his two victories over the French, the king took up residence at Cua Khe, yet he was obliged to remain constantly in motion. The French army remained master of the battlefield, supported by well-trained local auxiliaries who had become extremely formidable.

The Muong forces, though they held strong and well-fortified positions and could kill a considerable number of French soldiers with their crossbows, could not withstand two colonial brigades and were compelled to abandon their positions and retreat into the deep forests. At this time, Ton-that-Thuyet and Tran-xuan-Soan, seeing clearly that their strength was insufficient to resist the French, took the route through Qui-Dat to Ha Tinh and thence to China, seeking aid. After the battle at Cua Khe, Ngoc carried the king to Mount Ma-Rai, some several score leagues distant. Major Pelletier, searching the camp, captured a saddled horse and several pots of freshly cooked rice, while a hundred Muong soldiers had already escorted the king out of danger.

Through years of hardship and wandering, facing death at every turn, King Ham Nghi had developed a resolute spirit. He understood that the people looked to him as their rallying point against the enemy; that if he should lose heart or fall into enemy hands, it would be disastrous for the destiny and future of his country. Ton-that-Thuyet likewise regarded the king as the soul of the resistance, the sacred symbol of the nation, and therefore entrusted the responsibility of guarding the king to his two sons (at that time Thiep was only eighteen years old, the same age as King Ham Nghi). Both were heroic and patriotic youths, like their father.

During this period, King Ham Nghi was tossed about incessantly, ascending mountains and descending waterfalls. Ton-that-Dam, together with Admiral Nguyen-pham-Tuan, was stationed at Vang Lieu (Thac Dai) in the Cooc hamlet, some six or seven dozen leagues from the king. All correspondence from the Can-Vuong organizations was sent to this location (Thac Dai belonged to Co-Liem village), reporting on activities and requesting instructions. From the beginning of 1888, Dam lost contact with the king: the French had established numerous posts in the upper reaches of the Nai River, blocking all routes used by the resistance and severing all lines of supply. Plagnol and Pelletier had evidently executed the plan that De Hoang had previously devised.

Had Truong-quang-Ngoc and Ton-that-Thiep possessed less knowledge of the terrain and natural features of this region, the French would have captured Ham Nghi much earlier. Some have posed the following questions: Why did King Ham Nghi remain so persistently in the Quang Binh area? Why did he not take the Laotian route to the North? Perhaps because the king was unfamiliar with Laotian territory, or because Quang Binh offered greater advantages than other regions? In this area, many rivers thread through the landscape: the Nai, the Phe, the Khe Ve, the Ngan Sau, the Ngan Pho. A small boat drifting on the green waters could go anywhere, today here, tomorrow there.

The task of supplying the king at this time fell to Bat-Danh of Thanh Lang village, who brought fish sauce, dried fish, and incense from Quang province for the king's use. King Ham Nghi's situation was growing ever darker. Additional loyal officers were captured: Admiral Duong Mon, Assistant Commander Thuoc, and another named Tao — whose rank remains unknown to this day.

It should be recalled that in the summer of the previous year (1887), the Co-Xa people of a Muong canton had provided the king with many expert archers skilled in poisoned arrows, for the French had pressed them so harshly that they were forced to surrender.

Meanwhile, Ngoc was encamped at Cha Mac village. When the French arrived, Ngoc fled. Only a few old women remained in the village. Captain Boulangier gave them a letter addressed to Ngoc and then withdrew to Minh Cam. A few days later, the village headman came to surrender at the Minh Cam post.

Captain Mouteaux then borrowed the canton chief to send a gift to Truong-quang-Ngoc: the opium lamp that had been seized from Ngoc's house during a previous raid, together with several ounces of opium. Mouteaux also sent King Ham Nghi a gift of one quintal of white rice and two letters — one from the Queen Mother, one from Emperor Dong Khanh — urging the king to return. But Mouteaux additionally sent a separate letter to Ngoc alone, entreating him to seize the king and promising rich rewards. (These letters were later transmitted by Ca Hinh, Ngoc's father.) It should be noted that the French were so eager to recruit Ngoc on the advice of Pham-van-My, who had previously defected to Hoang-ta-Viem.

My knew that Ngoc was a petty man who could be bribed. From that point, Boulangier sought means of communication with Truong-quang-Ngoc. Eventually, Ngoc received Boulangier's letter. On October 12, 1888, he secretly surrendered to Lieutenant Lagarrue, promising to render every assistance to the French, but requesting to be given a free hand to accomplish the task. Despite Ngoc's promises — given as early as July 1887 — the French continued their military operations, spreading their net ever tighter around the resistance king.

It was now the rainy season. King Ham Nghi was encamped at Khe Ta-Ban, near a waterfall some thirty or forty meters high. A narrow, perilous path led from Tha Mac, requiring the use of rattan ropes to climb a rocky escarpment. Beside this treacherous route ran another, broader and well-maintained path, deliberately constructed to deceive pursuers.

King Ham Nghi dwelt in a very large thatched house with walls of split bamboo, erected some six months earlier. In one chamber stood a throne upon a colored mat; adjacent to it was a room filled with rice, extending fifty or sixty meters in length. Here, a small retinue attended the king: a captain overseeing provisions (who, when later interrogated, testified that he had never seen King Ham Nghi read a book or write anything); an elderly assistant of about fifty, standing 1.70 meters tall; his secretary, called Cau Hai, 1.60 meters tall. Lanh-binh Ngoc always had two attendants — Tham Tac and Ong Lai. Three strong young men from Thanh Lang or Thanh Cuoc served as porters for the king. Two others worked in the garden and assisted with cooking. Additionally, four or five young Muong tribesmen helped with miscellaneous tasks.

At the end of the summer of 1888, despite all their efforts, the French could advance not a single step. Officers and men alike were exhausted; time seemed to stretch toward infinity for them. Approaching autumn, fearing the onset of bad weather, they were compelled to abandon some forward posts that had been established close to the mountains where they suspected King Ham Nghi was hiding. These posts, built during the year, were dismantled, and the French withdrew to their coastal stronghold at Dong Hoi.

An unexpected stroke of fortune then befell them: the platoon leader Nguyen-dinh-Tinh (misidentified by Ch. Gosselin as Nguyen-tinh-Binh), a close attendant of the king, suddenly surrendered at the Dong Ca post. Tinh, a native of Duc Vu village near Hue, had served in the royal bodyguard until September 1888. He told the French that he had followed the king since 1885 and had served as a guard until September 1888. Ton-that-Thiep, Thuyet's second son, never left the king's side and had at one time contemplated taking the king to the North. Thiep had once killed a mandarin who suggested negotiating with the French. He was determined never to surrender, and it was he who sustained the king's fighting spirit. The king was currently in a valley two hours' walk from Nga Hai, accessible only by going upstream along the Na River. No villages existed there. The people of Cha Mac commune had built the king a small house. Tinh further revealed that he and Truong-quang-Ngoc had planned since the previous month to abandon the king, surrender to the French, and hand him over. Around the king at that time, besides Ton-that-Thiep, were two high dignitaries and two attendants. The Muong people there still supplied the king, but the royal household had nothing but rice and salt, like the poorest peasants of the region.

The king wore brown clothing and was eighteen years old (1). He frequently suffered from malaria, as did his attendants. Whenever French troops drew near, a Muong would carry the king into the deep forest.

Ton-that-Dam was encamped near Vang Lieu in the mountains of Ha Tinh province. For eight or nine months he had been unable to communicate with King Ham Nghi — that is, ever since the French had appeared on the upper reaches of the Gianh River, blocking all communication between the king and the Can-Vuong forces in the Nghe-Tinh-Binh region. The king at this time had no money left, only a few gold medals, two trunks of clothing brought from Hue, and two swords — one of which was a gift from the French government to Emperor Gia Long in the early years of the century. This Vietnamese traitor further stated that Truong-quang-Ngoc had been suspected by Ton-that-Dam for some months.

The French then gave Nguyen-dinh-Tinh a letter for Ngoc, instructing him to find Ngoc and collaborate, coaching the two on how to capture King Ham Nghi. According to French orders, they were to try to take the king alive, treating him with the greatest respect; as for his attendants, if they resisted, they were to be killed without hesitation.

On November 3, 1888, the two men — Ngoc and Tinh — led two dozen able-bodied men from Thanh Lang and Thanh Cuoc, armed with spears and bows, to the place where King Ham Nghi was hiding.

At about ten in the evening, they reached the king's dwelling as described above. Hearing footsteps approaching, two figures rushed out from within and were immediately struck down by the traitors with sharpened staves and spears. (According to Ch. Gosselin, one was the aged Thong-che Nguyen-Thuy; the other, about forty-five years old, was his son, who served as a Counselor in the Imperial Secretariat — the French could not determine his name.) Ton-that-Thiep, alerted by the commotion, seized his sword and rushed outside, only to be cut down as well. The man who struck Thiep down was Cao-Viet-Luong of Thanh Cuoc village.

After eliminating the king's attendants, the traitors entered the dwelling. King Ham Nghi stood with sword in hand, realizing that he had been betrayed from within. He offered the sword to Truong-quang-Ngoc, saying:

"Kill me now, but do not deliver me to the Westerners."

A Muong who had long served the king seized him about the waist and wrested the sword from his hand. From that moment, the king displayed an extraordinary calm. One of the men knelt before His Majesty and, having lit a lamp, read Captain Boulangier's letter. King Ham Nghi sat down on his bed. In the letter, Boulangier urgently invited the king to return to the capital. The king replied:

"I do not know the person who wrote this letter. I understand nothing of it. Why should I return to Hue?"

Ngoc and his accomplices clamored for the king to depart. After pondering for a long time, King Ham Nghi said:

"So be it. You have betrayed me. It must be Heaven's will. I must follow you, then."

In that heavy hour, the traitors carried away Ton-that-Thiep's body, wiped clean the pool of blood, and gathered the king's belongings into a coffin-shaped chest bearing the character "Long Life" (thọ). The following morning (November 3, 1888), Ngoc's men bore the king on a palanquin to the Nga Hai riverbank (toward Tha Mac), placed him on a raft, and traveled for two days before reaching the Thanh Lang and Dong Ca posts. Along the river, the king remained silent, gazing at the scenery, occasionally asking the names of villages that passed before his eyes.

He was obliged to rest here for over a week, awaiting French orders. From Dong Ca, he descended to Quang Khe, then proceeded on foot to the Thua Hoa relay station (Cau Hai, Thua Thien). Captain Boulangier had just arrived. French soldiers presented arms and sounded trumpets in salute. The king neither looked nor responded. Even upon reaching the Thuan Bai fort, the king refused to acknowledge that he was King Ham Nghi. Perhaps he considered his capture a humiliation? Major Dabat read a welcoming address. The king replied:

"I dare not accept the words the Major has spoken. I am but a servant of King Ham Nghi. The king is now in the forest. Had I not been ill, I would have kept up with him."

He also asked: "What is Lang-sa [France]? I have never heard that word before."

The Governor of Thanh Thuy, Nguyen-huu-Viet, came to pay his respects. The king pretended not to know him. But when his former teacher, Nguyen-Thuan, came to visit, the king involuntarily rose to greet him. Only then did the French believe that they truly held in their hands the sovereign of the Southern Kingdom — the one for whom, over the past years, Central and Northern Vietnam had shed blood in defense, and in response to whose call all classes of the people had sacrificed themselves in battle.

On the morning of December 15, 1888, the king was carried by palanquin to Bo Trach. The local mandarins and French Lieutenant Bonnefoy escorted him to Dong Hoi. Along the way, the populace, hearing that he was passing, set up incense altars to welcome him. They brought food as offerings. Hundreds of people followed.

He arrived at Dong Hoi at nightfall. He did not enter the citadel but went instead to rest on a boat. In conversation with Bonnefoy, he inquired about steel-smelting, cannon-founding, telegraph construction, mining, and agricultural development — matters unheard of in a youth of eighteen in those days.

At Thuan An, he stopped at the fort of the commanding Lieutenant Colonel.

By this time, the Vietnamese court had already received news that King Ham Nghi had been captured. Emperor Dong Khanh immediately ordered the Thua Thien officials to bring him to the capital and instructed the Ministry of War to send a delegation to the territory of Thua Thien to welcome him, preparing a residence for his stay. However, the French Resident, having conferred with the Co Mat Vien (Privy Council), reported that the French authorities in Quang Binh had observed that King Ham Nghi's temperament was now abnormal; that his return to the capital would cause many inconveniences; and that he should be sent elsewhere for a few years until peace was restored, when he could be brought back. In truth, the French government had already issued secret orders to deport King Ham Nghi to Algiers. The colonial authorities claimed: "He needs rest to recover from the privations and fatigue of his wandering life in the mountains." (1)

Strangely enough, many people in Hue at this time observed that the king appeared quite robust. Rheinart visited him, and then a sampan conveyed him from Cau Hai to the Tu Hien estuary, with the intention that the Vietnamese court officials might identify him. Emperor Dong Khanh sent Co Mat mandarins Bon-van-Binh, Le Trinh, and Minister of Public Works Pham-Binh to Thuan An for this purpose, though the official pretext was a visit. Rheinart asked:

"The Queen Mother is gravely ill. If Your Majesty wishes to see her, I shall arrange to bring you to the capital to exchange a few words."

King Ham Nghi's face darkened as he replied:

"I am already a prisoner; the country is lost. How dare I think of parents, brothers, or sisters?"

Then he excused himself and retired to his private quarters.

During this period, a French officer secretly photographed the king many times, distributing the images to various provinces so that officials and people everywhere might know that King Ham Nghi had indeed been captured. At four in the morning of December 25, 1888, Lieutenant Bonnefoy invited Ham Nghi to board a vessel for Lang Co, where he was informed that the gunboat La Comète would carry him to Saigon. From there, aboard the Bien Hoa, he was transported to Algiers, arriving on the afternoon of Sunday, January 13, 1889.

Along the way, a French military officer questioned him about Ton-that-Thuyet. The king replied only briefly:

"I do not know Ton-that-Thuyet at all. I do not understand what you are asking about."

As they passed through the Hai Van Pass, Bonnefoy pointed to the gunboat and said:

"It is because of Ton-that-Thuyet that Your Majesty must now board this gunboat and depart from the Fatherland."

King Ham Nghi turned and said:

"I beg you not to mention this matter again."

At Linh Kien, King Ham Nghi transferred to La Comète for the voyage to Saigon. Lieutenant Bonnefoy came to bid him farewell before returning to Hue. The king looked toward the shore and, unable to contain his emotion at the thought of his own fate and that of his country, burst into tears. Fifteen minutes later, La Comète weighed anchor. At Saigon, he transferred to the Bien Hoa for the voyage to North Africa, arriving there in mid-January 1889.

King Ham Nghi's residence in Algiers was El-Biar, no more than three kilometers from the town. He lived at the Villa des Pins (Pine Villa). Upon arrival, he continued to wear Vietnamese national dress (long gown and turban). After a time, he took to cycling. But wherever he went, curious eyes would gather to stare at him, forcing him reluctantly to change his manner of dress. He began to study the French language and script. His tutor was Father Leopold (who later died at the Pellerin School in Hue in 1912), and he praised the king's intelligence and diligence, particularly in mathematics. The king gradually shed his initial taciturnity and indifference toward his captors. When first learning French, he spoke haltingly and with an imperfect accent, but after five years, he spoke with a remarkable fluency that astonished all. He eventually acquired French friends with whom he interacted intimately — men such as Tirman, Governor-General of Algiers, and Cambon. Those around him sought to ease the exiled king's nostalgia for his lost country and to soften his bitterness toward fate. Each year he met M. Sainte-Marie at Vichy, where he customarily went for a change of air. Sainte-Marie recalled that King Ham Nghi once confided to him:

"The history of France is very beautiful; so is that of my country."

King Ham Nghi possessed particular talent and aptitude for both music and painting. M. de Varigny praised him thus:

"These two arts suddenly and unexpectedly opened before his eyes a new world and awakened in his soul strange new emotions. He would spend entire days seated in his studio, absorbed in music and painting, showing that he was exceedingly curious, eager to understand, and creative."

Later, he married a Frenchwoman of noble birth. His life was not excessively wretched or tragic compared to that of Ton-that-Thuyet. He fathered two princesses: one, Nhu-Mai, later earned a degree in agricultural engineering and married a Frenchman; the other, a prince named Minh-Duc, of whom no further news is known.

Indeed, King Ham Nghi was the soul of the Can-Vuong resistance, as we have stated above. Therefore, when he was captured by the enemy, Ton-that-Dam — the most loyal element of the royalist-nationalist circle — lost heart. He instructed his officers and soldiers to surrender as they saw fit, then wrote two letters: one to King Ham Nghi, begging forgiveness for his failure as a subject to save his sovereign; the other to the commander of the Thuan Bai fort, Major Dabat, introducing his officers and men who were to surrender. After this, he declared: "If the French wish to know where I am, let them come into the forest to find me."

On November 15, 1888 — the 10th day of the Mau Ty year — he hanged himself. (According to Ch. Gosselin's Le Laos et le Protectorat français, he died by hanging; according to Baille's Souvenirs d'Annam, he took poison.) His poetry reveals a spirit of resolute defiance befitting a hero.

A few days later, acting on information provided by some resistance soldiers, the French exhumed Ton-that-Dam's body to verify his appearance and identity, then reburied him near Vang Lieu pagoda.

On November 17, Captain Boulangier arrived at Da Co to receive the surrender of twenty-four Can-Vuong officers, in accordance with Commissioner Dam's letter. Four days later, Le Truc and one hundred of his followers also surrendered at the Thanh Lang post. The soldiers were permitted by the French to return to their native villages to resume their livelihoods, after surrendering their weapons. The Can-Vuong commanders, however, refused to serve the Protectorate, pledging only henceforth not to oppose the French or the Vietnamese court. These men were dressed in the most wretched manner, bearing witness to the extraordinary hardships and privations they had endured over three long years — yet all maintained the same dignified and noble bearing as before.

When the Vietnamese court officials read Le Truc's testimony, which contained many less-than-deferential remarks, they wished to prosecute him. But the French, recognizing him as a heroic patriot, and for reasons of political expediency at that moment, released him to live in peace. He returned to his native village of Thanh Thuy in Tuyen Hoa district, Quang Binh province. French officials occasionally visited him, always showing him great respect.

Reading this, one cannot help but smile at the reactionary Vietnamese court officials who never missed an opportunity to ingratiate themselves with their French masters. With such slavish mentality, these neo-feudal elements would become the leaders of our nation for eighty years — and it is no wonder that so many tragic events ensued, giving rise to the popular saying:

"Heaven wrought a topsy-turvy change: The master became the servant, the servant became the master."

As for Truong-quang-Ngoc, this traitor was rewarded with a large sum of money and his rank of Lanh-binh was officially confirmed. Ngoc was appointed to various provinces, but wherever he went, officials and citizens alike held him in contempt. Eventually he was compelled to abandon his post and retire to his home village (Thanh Lang), where he remained until the time of Phan-dinh-Phung's uprising, when the Huong Khe resistance forces put him to death. His accomplice in this affair, Nguyen-dinh-Tinh, was also given a military post and sent to Binh Thuan, where he vanished without trace; perhaps he shared the fate of his fellow traitor. Several of their underlings received the rank of Cai-Boi and a few coins. Given the colonialists' manner of dealing with them, these wretches had sold King Ham Nghi for a paltry sum. As for how this seller of his king and country later paid for his crimes, the reader may consult the account of the Huong Khe uprising below.

CHAPTER IX

FAMOUS BATTLES OF THE TRUNG KỲ RESISTANCE AGAINST THE FRENCH

1: THE BA ĐÌNH UPRISING
(1886 — 1887)

Through the various uprisings we have already examined, we now come to that era when the French openly set about seizing the remainder of Trung Kỳ, intending to settle accounts with the whole of Việt Nam. Few Vietnamese, and indeed few French colonists, have forgotten the famous battles recounted below.

These battles revealed to all ranks of our compatriots that Việt Nam, upon its path of struggle for survival — though it had often been defeated by the enemy — had also prevailed against a wealthy and vastly better-equipped foe. It may be said that Vietnamese history is an unceasing struggle on every front, so that our people might endure forever upon this S-shaped strip of land; the national spirit, built upon the two words "freedom" and "independence," is the root from which the Vietnamese draw all their strength, and from the ancestors to the descendants they cling to that root, preserving it with every capacity and every passion.

Because of this, the Vietnamese people — no matter how dark their condition, how desperate their situation — always return at once to composure and then rise up with such power that the enemy cannot imagine it. And often we defeat them with the simplest of means, which is itself an extraordinary thing. Only by witnessing such a resurgence does one understand that no force can annihilate the Vietnamese people, yesterday or today.

But which uprisings truly embodied this terrible resilience? The answer: the Hùng Lĩnh uprising of Tống Duy Tân, the Bãi Sậy uprising of Nguyễn Thiện Thuật, the Ba Đình uprising of Phạm Bành, Hoàng Bật Đạt, and Đinh Công Tráng, the Hương Khê uprising of Phan Đình Phùng, and the Yên Thế uprising of Hoàng Hoa Thám.

The Ba Đình uprising broke out at the time when Tôn Thất Thuyết issued the Cần Vương Edict, denouncing the imperialists' ambition to swallow our entire nation. Many scholar-gentry and local notables concentrated in Thanh Hóa Province, a region bordering both Trung Kỳ and Bắc Kỳ, with many high mountains and dense forests stretching from the Đà River to the South China Sea, cutting across National Route 1 between Ninh Bình and Thanh Hóa. This area was strategically formidable due to its natural terrain. It was also economically rich, abundant in talent, and the source of many degree-holders. The central aim of the resistance leaders here was to seize the strategic throat between Trung and Bắc Kỳ, cutting off and controlling the enemy's main communication artery — National Route 1 by land. The water route from the sea could facilitate supply for the insurgent army whenever needed, using sailing boats, and also served as their retreat route if driven from the Hùng Lĩnh mountains.

Beginning in September 1886, Ba Đình inscribed its name into history. Ba Đình comprised three villages — Mỹ Khê, Thượng Thọ, and Mậu Thịnh — in Thanh Hóa Province (the birthplace of the Lê and Nguyễn dynasties), bordering Ninh Bình Province between the Hoạt Giang and Đại Lai Rivers flowing through Nga Sơn District. In the rainy season, standing water surrounded Ba Đình, deep enough to submerge a man, making it rise like an island up to two thước above the water; in the dry season, rice fields encircled these three villages like a marsh before a wall of thick green bamboo. To approach Ba Đình, there was but a single path from the dike of the canal (the river connecting the Hoạt Giang and Đại Lai).

(1) To speak of the Ba Đình uprising is to speak of the resistance of the people of Thanh Hóa Province following the Cần Vương Edict.

The three villages shared one great communal house, and the entire complex was called Ba Đình.

To block the French advance and impede their encirclement and attack, the Resistance planted numerous stakes in the marshes and dug moats and built ramparts — fortifications that, in truth, had only temporary value against Western weaponry. Those entrusted with directing the resistance here were Đốc học Phạm Bành, Đề đốc Đinh Công Tráng, Đề đốc Nguyễn Khế, and two Mường chieftains, Hà Văn Mao and Cầm Bá Thước, among others. Tuần vũ Nguyễn Bột Nô of Tĩnh Gia, Tổng đốc Lê Như Rạng, Bố chánh Ngô Xuân Quỳnh, and Tri huyện Nông Cống Tôn Thất Hàm also enthusiastically responded.

Combat responsibilities were distributed as follows: military affairs were under the command of Đinh Công Tráng, a fearless soldier who governed his troops with strict discipline. The construction of fortifications was the task of Nguyễn Khế. French officers had to acknowledge that Nguyễn's fortifications were built in accordance with proper scientific methods, revealing considerable strategic knowledge and skill in exploiting natural advantages. Beyond Ba Đình itself, an outer defense line manned by militia had the purpose of harassing the enemy and preventing them from approaching our positions. This was commanded by Đề đốc Trần Xuân Soạn. Nevertheless, as we shall see, the main force remained concentrated at Ba Đình.

After the summer rice harvest (May 1886), when the fields were temporarily dry, the Resistance began constructing the Ba Đình fortifications. They provided funds for the evacuation of the elderly, women, and children from the three villages to neighboring settlements such as Liên, Nghị, and Vinh; only able-bodied men stayed behind to fight. Each village in the two districts of Nga Sơn and Tống Sơn was required to contribute thirty pig baskets, one hundred bamboo poles, ten loads of straw, and laborers to dig trenches around the three villages. The moats were four thước wide and three thước deep; the excavated earth was piled high to form a rampart. The rampart rose eight to ten thước, wide enough for troops to move along it for patrolling around Ba Đình. On the outer face of the rampart they planted spikes; every five thước a sharpened stake lay just below the water's surface. Beyond this was a dense bamboo palisade with thorny branches spread out to a width of fifty thước. Along the top of the rampart, thousands of pig baskets filled with mud were stacked high. From inside, looking through the gaps between baskets, one could see everything outside clearly; conversely, those outside could see nothing within. After completing this rampart (which took a month), they let river water flood the fields, and Ba Đình suddenly became a vast lake.

From early October 1886, many French supply columns traveling National Route 1 were ambushed in stages by gunfire from the forest, forcing the French to launch sweep-and-encircle operations. The commanders of the Nam Định and Thanh Hóa sectors at that time were Colonel Dodds and Colonel Metzinger. The blockade by these two French commanders began in December 1886, aiming to cut off communication and supply to the insurgent army at both the inner and outer defense lines of Ba Đình.

On 18 December 1886, French forces were seen operating at the first defense line. Artillery went ahead to clear the way; infantry followed, capturing several small villages. The insurgents fought back fiercely, inflicting heavy losses on the enemy.

Dodds's Nam Định column attacked from the north, achieving some initial success. Major Nugent, commanding the second Tonkinese battalion, captured an outpost at Thượng Thọ village, but then met stiff resistance from the forts behind, a few hundred thước further on, and had to halt before the second defense line. The enemy withdrew, having suffered many dead and wounded, including Lieutenant Audibert.

Metzinger's Thanh Hóa column attacked from the south and also took losses, despite considerable bravery. Lieutenant Zanner and Lieutenant Tuffier, commanding this column, had to advance through water up to their shoulders. Volleys of gunfire came from within. Zanner was shot in the head and killed instantly, beside many of his soldiers in the trenches. Tuffier did not falter; he urged his men forward, pistol in one hand and sword in the other. But in the end, he and his comrades were also wounded, forcing Major Blaise to order the 80mm heavy artillery to open intense fire so his men could retreat, while the insurgents rushed out in a fierce pursuit.

This attack was deemed a failure. The French were forced to resume their blockade strategy, but lacking sufficient troops they had to summon forces from elsewhere, including their navy stationed off the coast of China.

By early January 1887, the French had assembled enough troops to blockade Ba Đình: two infantry companies of 260 men under Majors Rambaud, Deixonne, and Cortes; a naval infantry company of 180 men under Major Valence; a company of the second Foreign Legion regiment of 200 men under Major Préfet; a company of the third African infantry battalion of 180 men under Major Bernard; and a landing company from the warship Turenne with 150 men under a naval lieutenant.

The enemy artillery was equally formidable: ten 81mm cannon served by 120 artillerymen; four 95mm cannon under Major Girard; four 65mm naval guns served by 30 sailors; two Hotchkiss guns with 15 men; and four additional cannon with 80 men.

Combat engineers were under Major Joffre and Lieutenant Netter, with 46 soldiers. Collaborator troops (lính khố xanh) numbered 1,950 under Majors Monniot, Nugent, Bouchage, Audry, Blaise, and Lieutenants Lambelin, Drujon, and Chatier. The total French force was 3,388 men, of whom 1,580 were French soldiers.

The navy had four gunboats supporting and supplying the operation: the gunboat Estoc under Lieutenant Thounine, the Bossant under Lieutenant Le Prieur, the Avalanche under Lieutenant Dantin, and the Moulin under Lieutenant Surcouf. The enemy also brought 5,000 coolies seized from Phát Diệm (Bắc Kỳ) to Ba Đình for battlefield service and supply. All these forces were under the command of Colonel Brissaud.

The plan for this final assault on Ba Đình was decisive. It relied on the engineers building ramparts along the dike to advance step by step; they also drained the tidal water, letting it recede, then blocked it from returning. On 6 January 1887, artillery opened fire in support of the infantry advance. The French divided into three columns; by two in the afternoon, all three had reached the fortifications on the dike.

The first column, led by Lieutenant Drujon and Fauret, reached within a hundred thước of our fortifications and encountered the bamboo palisade. They had to blast and chop their way through with mines and axes, while our men, concealed within, fired fiercely. Drujon shouted for his men to advance to the village gate, but halfway there both he and Fauret were hit by gunfire and forced to withdraw.

The second column — the center — under Major Bouchage, comprised Lieutenant Lambelin's group, a second Tonkinese battalion, and the fourth Tonkinese battalion under Major Goering. This column moved to the left of the first and, approaching within twenty thước of the fortifications, was also stopped by insurgent fire. Lambelin and two non-commissioned officers, along with many soldiers, were hit and fell back. Engineers and auxiliaries also took heavy casualties.

The third column, moving to the right under Lieutenant Collot, consisted of troops from the second Tonkinese battalion leading engineers with explosives, coolies carrying brushwood and baskets of earth, with the objective of breaching our defenses. These too were hit by crossfire and could not get near.

Colonel Dodds, seeing the situation was too difficult, sounded the retreat. But the center column had advanced too far and could not withdraw immediately. Artillery had to bombard to support this wing, and then the entire infantry and Goering's right-flank force had to make a desperate charge to provide cover. Goering was struck in the shoulder, and his troops became bogged down and unable to move. Major Bouchage, commanding this assault, was also wounded in the thigh as he urged the retreat and conveyed the wounded to the rear. Thanks to the heavy artillery, the assault force gradually pulled back. Major Amar of the second Tonkinese battalion, diligently overseeing the withdrawal, was also wounded.

The result of this battle: the enemy lost four officers and a large number of soldiers of various ranks. The insurgent tactic at that time was to lure the enemy close, exploit the positional advantage, and kill them. Each time an enemy commander fell, a voice from the watchtower would call for troops to go out and retrieve the enemy's corpse for a reward. From the experience of this battle, the French abandoned frontal assaults; meanwhile, from within the fortifications, our men would hurl challenges that drove the enemy to fury.

Colonel Brissaud realized that a direct assault was ineffective and recalled the siege of Puebla in Mexico. He mobilized the main force, dividing them into two elements: one to counter internal attacks, one for external sorties, and then maintained a prolonged siege. It must be noted that this strategy could work only because the outer force under Đề Soạn had sufficient strength merely to harass; if the insurgents had attacked from within while the outer force struck from without, the French, caught in the middle, could hardly have held out.

Brissaud then had communication trenches dug, advancing step by step toward Ba Đình fortress. Meanwhile, he collected timber, bamboo, and iron wire from various places, building them into continuous palisades with a fort every interval for day-time troop shelter; where the water was deep, he built forts on ironclad boats or rafts. Then he had cannon fire poured into Ba Đình day and night. This tactic proved effective; his engineers could advance without suffering heavy casualties as before. Then both columns assaulted again — the southern under Metzinger, the northern under Dodds. By 15 February 1887, the first French units were only about a hundred thước from our positions. Yet they still did not know our defensive layout, seeing only rows of sharp stakes rising from the water and estimating our defense line to be about 1,200 thước long and 400 thước wide. On occasion, the French captured some who had fled Ba Đình because they could no longer endure the destruction wrought by French artillery; only then did they learn of our defensive arrangements, which were focused on defense, holding a defensive posture because the enemy's firepower was indeed overwhelming. In the end, the inner and outer forces could not support each other; the insurgents held out only as long as they could, though their spirit remained unbroken.

On 19 January, the enemy's engineers advanced to the very edge of the fortifications. Concentrating into three columns, they used heavy artillery to open the way, then stormed the north side of Thượng Thọ village, using flamethrowers to burn the fortifications and camps within. The insurgents, sheltered behind bamboo-and-mud ramparts, fired back; by night they used the darkness to sally forth and kill the enemy. The French, sacrificing heavily, refused to retreat and redoubled their burning efforts. Grenades and explosives rained upon our positions — up to 16,000 shells, turning Ba Đình into a sea of fire. The enemy suffered heavy losses, but before such overwhelming firepower, we had no means to stop them.

A retreat through blood and fire: A few days earlier, Đinh Công Tráng had seen that Ba Đình's situation might become critical. He wrote to Đề Soạn at Quảng Hóa, requesting a strong external attack to divide the enemy's forces. Although Soạn then had 2,000 troops stationed about half a day's march from Ba Đình, he dared not advance close to the enemy's rear, only firing artillery in support from a distance, and thus could not restrain enemy operations against Ba Đình.

Đinh Công Tráng was forced to open a bloody path for his troops to withdraw at dusk, under the glow of fire that lit the sky. The insurgents charged out in coordination with friendly units from neighboring villages attacking inward. A thick fog descended; the two sides became intermingled, recognizing each other only by the flash of gunfire. The roar of explosions large and small rumbled across the sky. The battle was savage beyond description. At dawn the next day a ghastly scene met everyone's eyes: on the French defensive line, corpses lay strewn about, blood mixing with mud in a stench that made one retch. And the French who entered Ba Đình also encountered many unburied corpses, flies and maggots swarming. In the yard of the communal house lay the body of Đề đốc Khế — this was where the most ferocious fighting had occurred.

The French occupation of Ba Đình was hailed by the colonialists as a glorious feat of arms, earning Colonel Brissaud promotion to Brigadier-General. But according to documents captured by the French — a letter from Đinh Công Tráng to the insurgent commander at Mã Cao — our troops were forced to abandon Ba Đình hastily because thousands of French soldiers (including auxiliaries) had left their corpses around the fortifications; the water was stagnant, the dead unburied, and cholera threatened to break out. The transfer of forces to a second position was unavoidable. Thus we may conclude that, had it not been for this factor, the insurgents might have prolonged the campaign, and the French would not have won.

Some observations on the Ba Đình battle: If one thinks that the resistance leaders held fast at Ba Đình simply to fight it out with the French army, this would be an oversimplification. For although we had the advantage of terrain and natural features, our troops were few, our provisions and weapons limited, and we could hold this position for only a certain period, long or short. At this time the enemy held numerous political, economic, and military advantages; naturally their resources were abundant, their recruitment easy, and a prolonged campaign would inevitably bring them victory. We lacked external reinforcement, so our capacity for holding out was merely a matter of months. Phạm Bành, Đinh Công Tráng, and their comrades were not ignorant of this. Surely they intended only to use the Ba Đình battlefield, with its formidable position, to destroy a certain portion of the enemy's forces, then exploit the renown of this resistance for propaganda abroad and throughout the country.

According to Đinh Công Tráng's report, at 4:00 AM on 21 January 1887, he and Phạm Bành broke through the enemy's lines and escaped. The enemy suffered a large number of dead, while our side had approximately 150 killed and wounded.

The enemy had suffered quite heavily during the months-long blockade and assault on Ba Đình. In France, political circles in Paris were greatly agitated, and they concealed their defeats at the Ba Đình battle zone, ordering the expeditionary forces to complete the destruction of the Resistance here at any cost, permitting them to report only victory.

We also note that the fighting spirit at Ba Đình was highly commendable: from the very start of building this battle zone to turn it into a grave for the invaders, only the elderly, women, and children were evacuated; all able-bodied men remained behind, resolved to die fighting the enemy. Ammunition was scarce because supply was heavily obstructed by the enemy's ever-tightening net, so the Resistance had to economize every single bullet. They had to wait until the enemy came close to the fortifications, within certain range, before firing to be sure of hitting; thanks to this, every round fired took an enemy life in the stinking mire. The insurgents fought with extraordinary determination and courage, causing great hardship for the enemy from officers to men. After a time, Brissaud fell ill from exhaustion and his inability to adapt to the watery terrain. Major J. Masson, in his book "Souvenir de l'Annam et du Tonkin," recorded the desperation of the expeditionary force thus:

"Our situation before Ba Đình had reached such a point that we could no more count on an enemy surrender than on their generosity should we be defeated."

After breaking this position — the great achievement of the people of Nga Sơn and Tống Sơn districts — the French and the Nam Triều feudal authorities expunged the three villages of Thượng Thọ, Mậu Thịnh, and Mỹ Khê from the map of Thanh Hóa Province and erected a plaque there, with menacing words for the people:

"These three villages — Mỹ Khê, Thượng Thọ, and Mậu Thịnh — have been condemned as bandits and rebels for having built walls and ramparts at this place without notifying the authorities. These villages shall henceforth vanish from the face of the earth. Those who transgress shall be punished..."

But these three villages, together with the people of Ba Đình, Tống Sơn, and Nga Sơn, have not "vanished" from memory or from the modern history of our country. On the contrary, there is no Vietnamese who does not remember that Ba Đình was one of the most glorious battlegrounds of the beloved sons of the Vietnamese Fatherland, and the burial ground of countless imperialist officers and soldiers sacrificed by French capitalists and financiers to their work of invasion and plunder.

The final phase of the Ba Đình insurgents — Mã Cao was a secondary position for resisting the French, supporting Ba Đình when the insurgents used Ba Đình as a site to apply the strategy of attrition. Mã Cao was built by a number of scholar-gentry from various places, and the leader of this battle zone was the Mường chieftain Hà Văn Mao. In the Mã Cao force there were also some "Black Flag" soldiers. This position was covered by dense forests and high mountains, with paths through the mountains to Laos and Yunnan. Additionally, numerous river branches flowed from the sea via National Route 1, such as the Lèn, Mã, and Chu Rivers, crossing the plains to the highlands of Thanh Hóa Province and even up to Ninh Bình, running parallel to the road to Bắc Kỳ.

The Resistance built a central artillery fort right at a bend in the river, where the water was fifteen thước deep and forty thước wide, using the river itself as a moat. In the riverbed, rocks jutted up unevenly. At the river mouth, a dike built by the locals for flood control; our troops dug a trench along the dike. To protect the central artillery fort, six smaller forts were placed within a three-square-kilometer radius, nestled in the thickets, built of earth that was tamped hard when dry so that rifle bullets could not penetrate. One of these forts housed a gunpowder workshop. The adjacent dense forest, called Cụ Bảo Forest, was crisscrossed with trenches to block enemy attacks from outside.

(1) Captain Joffre, later to become Marshal of France, renowned in the First World War, was seriously wounded during the French assault on Ba Đình.

From Ba Đình, the insurgents withdrew to Mã Cao, and the enemy pursued at once. They divided into two columns, each for pursuit: Colonel Dodds advanced into Quảng Hóa District, while Colonel Metzinger followed the right bank of the Mã River toward Thọ District. They arranged to meet at Mã Cao. Besides these two combat columns, a third under Major Joffre was responsible for commandeering all local boats for the transport of food and ammunition.

At noon on 22 February 1887, both columns arrived at their rendezvous. They had not yet learned the situation at Mã Cao when a reactionary district magistrate seized two locals to guide the enemy, suspecting they were Mã Cao's men. And indeed they were Resistance fighters, for which the collaborators could find no proof. They planned to lead the French astray into an ambush, but the wily enemy refused, demanding to go directly to the Mã Cao position. Upon reaching a deep valley, crossing a stream, a cannon shot and a volley of rifle fire came from the far bank. Brissaud immediately ordered a swift retreat to avoid encirclement, then divided his forces into three groups to attack from three sides, both to reconnoiter the routes and to check the Resistance attack. Dodds swung right, Metzinger left, Diguet held the center, while Major Nugent and two lieutenants, Faure and Betzelère, commanded mobile units.

Our guns crashed from all sides; the enemy could not tell where we were strong or weak. Brissaud ordered the center column to advance, ford the river to seize a small fort blocking the path, while the vanguard pressed into the thick forest, and the other units sought ways to avoid our encirclement.

After an hour of searching, the French vanguard stopped before a deep valley surrounded by dense trees. The French right wing came under fire at very close range — only a few thước away; from thirty thước across the valley, insurgent bullets also whistled past. The two guides bolted, calling to the insurgents, reporting that a French general and his staff had arrived. Unluckily for these two, they were pursued and shot dead by auxiliary troops.

The French assault force continued advancing, with land and water units maintaining communication, giving them strength. The insurgents had to fall back within their defense line to exploit other positions. Drums and bugles called upon the auxiliaries to turn their guns on the enemy, mingling with the sounds of small and large arms that echoed across the forested mountains. The French used mortars to fire at areas where ambushes seemed to lie and where gun smoke rose. From the forts hidden in the valley thickets, the insurgents concentrated fire on the French units before them. The enemy faltered; the bitter battle continued until evening. In the end, the insurgents, fearing the enemy's superior numbers would encircle them and cut off their retreat, withdrew into the valley. By nightfall, the enemy from the north managed to penetrate the central fort; the insurgents retreated southward. But the enemy paid a heavy price for this capture: many soldiers dead; a navy lieutenant was shot through the chest and died on the spot as he rushed into the fort; Lieutenant Causé of the second Tonkinese battalion was also wounded, along with many indigenous soldiers. The French prepared to attack the remaining forts the next day, but the insurgents had already slipped away that very night. Here too, the French acknowledged that the insurgents' defensive works were as methodically designed as at Ba Đình, and that if we had had greater offensive capability — if not for our few troops and meager weapons and ammunition — the outcome might have been different.

The uprising of the scholar-gentry and people of Thanh Hóa then gradually receded into the past. From that point onward, the insurgents no longer established themselves in any single position, for they had only enough strength for guerrilla warfare, and the resistance shifted to the highlands rather than remaining on the plains.

And the final fates of the Cần Vương leaders?

When he withdrew from Ba Đình on the night of 21 January 1887, Phạm Bành fled to Mã Cao. Some collaborators urged the French to capture his mother and children to force him to surrender. Indeed, he surrendered to save his mother, but once his mother and children were freed, he took his own life that very day. Nowadays, people consider that in times of national crisis one should place loyalty to country before filial piety. Was Phạm Bành's conception of loyalty and filial duty misplaced?

As for Đinh Công Tráng? After the Mã Cao defensive force collapsed, Tráng tried to gather the remnants to reorganize resistance in the neighboring regions, but the French were searching too intensely. He had to flee to Nghệ An.

On 5 October 1887, the village chief of Tăng Yên denounced him to Major Coste, commander of the Đô Lương garrison on the Cả River. Coste and a Lieutenant De Fitz Jammes immediately took 65 soldiers upriver by boat the very night they received this information. Tăng Yên village was about twenty kilometers from the river, the road very difficult due to prolonged rain. At three in the morning they arrived; the village was still silent as a tomb. They rushed the stilt-house where Tráng was staying; he knew only when it was too late. From inside, he fired, killing two enemy soldiers, but Coste and a soldier named Nguyễn Tuyên still burst in, firing multiple revolver shots. Tráng and his brother-in-law were killed. (Some sources record that after the Ba Đình battle, Tráng withdrew to Ngọc Lạc, and on the road to Tương Dương District in Nghệ An, he encountered a French patrol; he fought desperately, was captured, and committed suicide.)

The French praised Đinh Công Tráng: "He never avoided danger, always leading from the front on every operation. Yet he was cautious and bold, and possessed an ardent patriotism. Though a commoner, he had many noble qualities; he stood at the head of a number of officials, most of whom were proud-spirited. If he had been supported by capable men, the French would have struggled long and hard, and who knows how they might have triumphed..."

These were the words of Colonel Masson, who participated in commanding the battles at Ba Đình and later praised Đinh Công Tráng warmly, calling him "a man of order, courage, strict military discipline, keen observation, and patience. Moreover, he understood both himself and others, and never employed his troops wastefully. He was a strategist to be admired, skilled at deploying forces and luring the enemy into dangerous positions."

Ba Đình and Mã Cao were suppressed, but the colonialists could not yet set aside their arms to eat and sleep in peace. Their troops had to hastily take to the road again, to clash with other patriotic forces even more formidable — the Hùng Lĩnh and Hương Khê uprisings, and the campaign of Lê Ninh from 1885 onward.


2: THE HÙNG LĨNH UPRISING
(1886 — 1892)

Leading the uprising here (Hùng Lĩnh in Thanh Hóa) was Tống Duy Tân, a scholar-official who had once served as Sơn phòng sứ (Mountain Defense Commissioner) in this very region. His native village was Đông Biên (Bồng Trung) in Vĩnh Lộc District, Thanh Hóa Province. He earned the Cử nhân degree at thirty-six and the Tiến sĩ degree three years later, under the reign of Tự Đức. At forty-one he was appointed Đốc học (Education Commissioner) of Thanh Hóa Province, and four years later he was transferred to the post of Thương biện tỉnh vụ (Provincial Affairs Assistant) of Thanh Hóa. His final position was Chánh sứ Sơn phòng (Chief Mountain Defense Commissioner) for Vĩnh Lộc. His uprising occurred at the same time as many others — Ba Đình, Bãi Sậy, Mã Cao — during the period when the anti-French movement was spreading throughout Trung and Bắc Kỳ.

Tống Duy Tân's organization consisted of local militia: each district supplied one cơ (about 200 men), led by a Cơ trưởng. His tactics were essentially identical to those of the Ba Đình leaders: on principle, he selected a strategically formidable position, built solid fortifications, and then lured the enemy forward. When the enemy proved too strong, the insurgents would withdraw to another location. (This defensive strategy had the weakness that the enemy possessed artillery, making it impossible for the insurgents to preserve their main force.) Assisting Tống Duy Tân were the Mường chieftains Cầm Bá Thước and Cao Điền, who were then raising troops at Sầm Sơn.

During the period of fighting against French forces under Major Térillon at Đông Khố commune, Tống's insurgents once defeated the French, killing many of them. Later, Tống was subjected to a fierce French attack and had to flee to Bắc Kỳ and then to China. In November 1888, Emperor Hàm Nghi was captured and exiled. Tống received orders from Tôn Thất Thuyết to return to Thanh Hóa to resume operations. At the beginning of 1889, he established a base at Vận Động in western Thanh Hóa, about twenty kilometers northwest of Nông Cống. He dug moats and built ramparts, intending to make this a long-term base. Thanks to the Mường chieftain Cầm's support, many Mường compatriots in the region enthusiastically joined the insurgent army.

On 8 October 1889, receiving word that Tống Duy Tân's insurgents were active at Vận Động, Lieutenant Morfond, commander of the indigenous auxiliary garrison at Nông Cống, took four French soldiers and twenty Vietnamese auxiliaries to reconnoiter. Approaching Vận Động, they ran into the insurgents' fortifications. From behind the ramparts, insurgent bullets rained down like rain, killing Morfond, four French soldiers, and four auxiliaries on the spot. Most of the remaining fled, and several more died. Three days later, a force of 180 enemy soldiers marched in from Thanh Hóa town. After this second engagement, the French lost another dozen men. On 22 October, Colonel Barberet led 185 French soldiers from Thanh Hóa, only to find that the insurgents had already withdrawn to Đa Bút, an even more formidable position. To reach it, one could only take small paths. Before advancing, the enemy bombarded with artillery. The two sides fought fiercely from 2 to 3 November, after which the insurgents moved north to Phố Cát. The enemy pursued but gained nothing and had to return to Thanh Hóa on 9 November. At the end of this month, a notable event: Đề Soạn's forces came to cooperate with Tống's and Cao's troops. The Hùng Lĩnh insurgents grew stronger and expanded their operations across Yên Định District.

The colonialists began to worry and saw the need to suppress the Hùng Lĩnh movement to limit its influence on other regions. Lieutenant-Colonel Lefèvre replaced Barberet. He brought along a cavalry unit with 80mm mortars. The insurgents were then at Vạn Lại, thirty kilometers west of the provincial seat.

On 30 November 1889, the enemy reached Vạn Lại but did not open fire immediately. They investigated every aspect of the situation before striking. After the first engagement, the French were at a disadvantage: Colonel Lefèvre was severely wounded, and a number of French soldiers died. Lieutenant Colleta took command and, with Lefèvre's wounds bandaged, led the force back toward the provincial seat. Colleta withdrew to Yên Lược, south of Vạn Lại. Seizing the momentum, the insurgents divided into four groups and, armed with spears and pikes, attacked the enemy at close quarters for over two hours on the night of 1 December 1889, only withdrawing near dawn after setting Yên Lược ablaze.

The enemy suffered heavy losses and could no longer afford to underestimate us. This time they deployed 500 troops and two cannon, placing Lieutenant-Colonel Jorne de Lacale in command. Tống again changed position, for the enemy was far more numerous and powerful, and they had also discovered his whereabouts. The French pursued but without result. On 12 December, they marched back to Yên Lược to block the insurgents' route to the plains.

For five consecutive days from 20 December, the enemy conducted sweeps and searches of suspicious villages. On 30 December, they arrived at Sè village on information that insurgents were there; at noon on 1 January 1890, they encountered the insurgents at Kẽm village — an outpost with 400 soldiers and 140 riflemen sheltered behind fortifications. French cannon fired continuously for forty-five minutes, breaching the defenses, and then the French stormed in. Both sides fought fiercely; in the end, a French captain named Christophe and many soldiers perished. The insurgents withdrew at nightfall.

On the night of 5-6 March 1890, the insurgents raided Nông Cống in two columns: one attacked the district seat, while the other destroyed a bridge near the garrison to block enemy reinforcements. The insurgents also spread word that they would annihilate the Nông Cống garrison the following night. The garrison commander, Lieutenant Jolly, urgently sent word to the provincial capital. French Resident Lebrun hastily dispatched Lieutenants Savereux, Détienne, Miche, and Rémy with reinforcements, but they found nothing when they arrived. On the way back, an informant told them that 200 insurgents were present in a neighboring village; they sent a messenger back to the province for more troops. Our forces captured this messenger and, thanks to this, laid an ambush. Resident Lebrun led his troops in and was surrounded, beaten back to Nông Cống with considerable losses. Jolly's relief attempt was ineffective. When the news reached Thanh Hóa, Vice-Resident Neuville brought another 100 French troops, intending to coordinate an attack on the insurgents, but Lebrun had already been defeated and had retreated to Nông Cống. The insurgents, after their victory, moved on to Yên Lãng in Thọ Xuân District. The commander of this battle was Cao Điền.

On 29 March 1890, the French divided into two columns — one from Nông Cống, one from Thanh Hóa — totaling 200 soldiers with two artillery sections, and surrounded Yên Lãng. The insurgents were at a disadvantage in this battle, losing several men, including the Chánh tổng (cantonal chief) of Yên Lãng. On 16 April, Cao Điền's outpost at Na Lung was attacked. The insurgents lost five men and six rifles before moving to Thanh Khoải village. There Cao had about 400 men, 160 rifles, and two fortification guns. On 29 April, the two sides met at Mỹ Hòa, fought briefly, and then the insurgents withdrew to their main fort. Three enemy columns under officers Viola, Bonnet, and Marlier attacked the fort's front; two more under Le Breton and Détienne attacked the flanks; Lameray commanded the blocking force to cut off the insurgents' retreat. Despite the enemy's numbers and tight encirclement, they were decisively defeated. Several were killed (including Lieutenant Bonnet), several wounded (including Viola, shot in the right cheek), and six indigenous soldiers died. The main fort's position was a high hill surrounded by dense spikes. The enemy tried to cross and suffered heavily for their audacity. The battle lasted from 3 PM to 7 PM. After the victory, Cao Điền led his troops to Cửa Đại.

Months later (October 1890), 225 enemy troops attacked this position but failed; the insurgents moved on to An Lâm.

On 21 February 1891, Tống Duy Tân and Cao Điền both arrived at Lang Vinh, using the high mountains there for defense. Lieutenant Soler, Lieutenants Henschell, Marlier, and Normand came in search of them. Not knowing the routes, they forced the Lang Vinh village chief to guide them. At sixty thước from the position, insurgents fired from the thick underbrush. The French could not advance, and the next day, as they crossed a small stream, several were killed. But from February to April, the insurgents suffered a series of defeats, losing men and weapons. From April 1892 onward, the insurgent force numbered no more than 100 men and 50 rifles. The two leaders, Tống and Cao, fled yet again, this time to China, but they were no longer as optimistic as in previous years, because the people were too brutally terrorized to provide full support. The two leaders disbanded their battle units, sending them home to their villages to earn a living, keeping only a small number of volunteers who would follow them to the end.

From 24 September 1892, Thanh Hóa Resident Boulloche dispatched a patrol carrying Nguyệt, the son of Cai Mậu (a former insurgent commander), and his uncle Trương Lương, as guides. Boulloche arrived at the home of a local Thổ ty (indigenous chief) who had long participated in the righteous cause and had recently sheltered Cao Điền's men. Before the enemy arrived, the Thổ ty had already sent people to take Cao Điền into hiding at Đốc Tống's house in Lang Chánh.

Boulloche threatened the Thổ ty. His wife, terrified, guided Lieutenant Henschell and twenty soldiers to arrest Tống Duy Tân at 6 PM on 4 October 1892. The route was extremely perilous — climbing waterfalls and traversing ravines — and at 6 AM the next day they reached Tống's hiding place: a cave two kilometers long on a mountainside south of Niên Kỷ. Before the cave entrance stood a cannon and a burning fire. Tống was captured by surprise and taken to Thanh Hóa. (According to another source, after his defeat, Tống Duy Tân fled into the Sơn Dương forest of Thanh Hóa, where he was betrayed by a former student named Cao Ngọc Lễ in the pay of the French. This wretch, after having Tống decapitated and his head displayed, was granted the position of Tri huyện, later becoming Án sát. To curry favor, he would hunt down many more revolutionaries, like Nguyễn Thân and Hoàng Cao Khải who originated from Quảng Nam and Hà Tĩnh. The age produced such lines as:

"Vô địa khả mai Cao Ngọc Lễ,
Hữu thiên ưng thục Tống Duy Tân."
(No ground to bury Cao Ngọc Lễ,
Heaven itself should ripen Tống Duy Tân.)

Boulloche also had Cao Điền hunted on 3 October, a day earlier. On the way, his 30-man patrol was fiercely attacked; two insurgents were captured, six killed. Along with Cao Điền, only four or five men and two rifles remained. Cao escaped north and joined the Yên Thế insurgents, but was eventually caught in the enemy's net on 16 January 1896.

Now, reflecting upon this: while Ba Đình was active, the Hùng Lĩnh insurgents were also a thorn in the colonial forces' side, because each served as a diversion for the other. When Ba Đình fell, the Cần Vương army under Tống Duy Tân became isolated (1887), and its future was dark from that moment onward.

For six full years, Tống Duy Tân used guerrilla tactics to harass the enemy, raiding French outposts scattered along communication routes and in various districts and prefectures. After each battle, the insurgents would swiftly withdraw to the forested mountains to hold strong positions. However, on certain opportune occasions, the insurgents did mount some fairly large-scale mobile or encounter battles that the French still record: 8-22 October 1889, the Đa Bút battle on 2 November 1889, the Vạn Lại battle on 30 November 1889, the Yên Lược battle on 1 December 1889, the Nông Cống battle on 7 March 1890, the Mỹ Hòa battle on 29 April 1890, and so forth — these battles continued in sequence over six consecutive years. But the insurgents' strength eroded daily; replacements came slowly; the enemy, though suffering heavy losses, received steady supplies and always had formidable forces for suppression. The insurgents gradually disintegrated, and by early 1892, Tống Duy Tân saw that he could no longer preserve his forces. Forced to disband the soldiers who had followed him so long, he wept as he let them go. After this disbandment, both Tống and Cao fell into the enemy's net — truly a grievous and lamentable end.

Nevertheless, through Tống Duy Tân's activities, we see that his uprising clearly achieved a broad unity across all strata of the nation. The proclamations and leaflets calling upon the auxiliaries, drafted by Cao Điền, bore the reign-title Hàm Nghi thứ VI and raised the slogans: fight the enemy, seize the enemy's guns to arm the insurgents, Catholics and non-Catholics are one family, do not kill each other. In the early years, the insurgents fought with great spirit; many able-bodied men volunteered as local militia, so that the insurgents established a cơ in each prefecture and district, each commanded by a Cơ trưởng who was a Cần Vương adherent. During this period, the Mường compatriots also closed ranks with the Kinh people, driven and encouraged by chieftain Cầm Bá Thước.

(It should be noted that the uprising in Hà Tĩnh was first begun by Ấm Ninh, also known as Lê Ninh, styled Mạnh Khang. Ấm Ninh belonged to a line of hereditary officials in the region (Trung Lễ village, Văn Lâm canton, La Sơn District, Đức Thọ Prefecture, Hà Tĩnh Province). He contributed his own family wealth to recruit soldiers and forge weapons. His troops were drawn from the local area: from each household with three adult males, he took two; from those with two, he took one. He also recruited from his wife's home villages in Phù Long and Yên Trường communes of Nghệ An Province. His soldiers wore black uniforms with greenish-blue belts. Here he recruited five vệ (regiments) and concentrated them at Trung Lễ, combining with the insurgents of Tổng Nga canton, Kỳ Anh District, Thanh Hà Prefecture, for a total of five đạo (divisions).

Ấm Ninh was still young but possessed the blood of a hero, appearing at the very beginning of the Cần Vương movement. In Hà Tĩnh at that time, the Bố chánh (Provincial Commissioner) Lê Đại refused to obey the Cần Vương Edict, so Ấm Ninh marched his forces to the provincial seat, captured the collaborationist official, and killed him. After this event, his influence resounded everywhere. He established his headquarters at his home village, with his soldiers stationed throughout the La Giang region. He made contact with Emperor Hàm Nghi and was appointed Bang biện quân vụ (Assistant Military Commissioner). Later, Emperor Hàm Nghi ordered him to cooperate with Phan Đình Phùng in the Hương Khê Nghĩa Hội.)


3: THE HƯƠNG KHÊ UPRISING
(1885 — 1896)

If one compares all the scholar-gentry uprisings from the southern reaches of Trung Kỳ up to the northern parts of Trung Kỳ, the Hương Khê uprising was the most renowned and longest-lasting, due to its well-organized scale, thorough training, and proper equipment — earning even the enemy's profound admiration.

Joining the Hương Khê uprising were many soldiers who had been defeated elsewhere, who eagerly volunteered to place themselves under the command of the Định Nguyên Lord, Phan Đình Phùng. Though the uprising bore the name Hương Khê, its operational territory encompassed all four provinces of northern Trung Kỳ (Nghệ An, Hà Tĩnh, Quảng Bình, and Thanh Hóa). The insurgents' base was established amidst the forests and mountains of Nghệ An and Hà Tĩnh, a position both strategically formidable and secret. It should be noted that during both the Trần and Lê dynasties, when resisting the Yuan and Ming invasions, this region was used as a base for advance, retreat, and defense (Lê Lợi established his anti-Ming base here for six consecutive years). Indeed, this region's eastern side includes the Thiên Nhãn mountain; to the north are the mountains and rivers of Thanh Hóa; to the south stretches the Trường Vật range down to the Hoành Sơn range; to the west lies the Trường Sơn range (also called the Giăng Màn mountains). The Lam River basin includes the tributaries Ngàn Phố (Hương Sơn) and Ngàn Sâu (Hương Khê). Advancing south leads to Tuyên Hóa (Quảng Bình), from which one can reach Quảng Trị and Thừa Thiên; advancing north leads directly to Thanh Hóa and Hòa Bình.

The insurgents established their headquarters in the Ngàn Trươi region, at a mountainous area called Vũ Quang in Hương Sơn, with three single paths leading to Laos, Siam, and to Hà Tĩnh. Anyone straying into this region would sink in mud up to their waist. The insurgent posts were scattered from the foot of Vũ Quang mountain to Trung Khê and Tri Khê, stretching nearly one hundred dặm, all relying upon forests, mountains, and rivers for both combat advantage and supply convenience.

Phan Đình Phùng was the soul of the Resistance here. We still recall that a few months earlier, he had disagreed with the two Phụ chánh đại thần (Regents), Tôn Thất Thuyết and Nguyễn Văn Tường, over the deposition of Emperor Dục Đức, and was imprisoned for a few days then dismissed and sent home. The Huế court at this time was like a pot of water boiling over. A political crisis weighed on everyone's mind: Emperors Dục Đức and Hiệp Hòa were forced to die, Phụ chánh Trần Tiến Thành was assassinated, Án sát Tôn Thất Bá was imprisoned. Although Phan Đình Phùng had cursed Thuyết, he was only expelled from court because Thuyết still respected him as sharing the same anti-French position. It is unclear whether this was by design — the discord between Thuyết and Phan — but Phan's return to his home village of Đông Thái in Hà Tĩnh Province to organize the uprising was secretly facilitated by Tôn Thất Thuyết, who appointed him Tham biện sơn phòng (Assistant Mountain Defense Commissioner) of Hà Tĩnh.

At the end of 1885, when Emperor Hàm Nghi and Tôn Thất Thuyết fled to Hà Tĩnh, Phan Đình Phùng brought a number of comrades-in-arms who had considerable prestige among the scholar-gentry — Phan Quang Cự, Phan Khắc Hòa, Hoàng Xuân Phong, Ngụy Khắc Kiều, Phan Trọng Mưu — to introduce himself and request official instructions for action. Thereafter, Thám hoa Nguyễn Đức Đạt, Hoàng giáp Nguyễn Quý, Tiến sĩ Nguyễn Xuân Ôn, Phó bảng Nguyễn Văn Chất, Cử nhân Nguyễn Văn Hạnh, Nguyễn Hành, Thái Vĩnh Chính, the Ấm Ninh brothers, Cao Đạt, and many others enthusiastically joined the "Hương Sơn Nghĩa Hội." But among these Cần Vương soldiers, the most exceptional was Cao Thắng — a young commander of talent and great bravery. It may be said that he was the pillar of the Hương Khê uprising, just as Đinh Công Tráng was the pillar of the Ba Đình uprising in Thanh Hóa. He joined the insurgent army from its very first days.

In 1874, there was an uprising in Hà Tĩnh led by Đội Lựu, which the Nam Triều called the "Cờ Vàng" rebels. Cao Thắng was in the ranks of Đội Lựu even then, though still in his youth. When Đội Lựu failed, Cao was pursued by the court but was saved from capture by Phan Đình Thuật, the elder brother of Phan Đình Phùng. Growing up, Cao increasingly possessed the spirit of the knight-errant: he recruited a few comrades and took to the mountains and forests, specializing in robbing the wealthy. When Phan Đình Phùng raised the banner of revolt in 1885, Cao Thắng and a comrade named Nguyễn Kiều brought sixty brothers to join the righteous cause. At that time Cao Thắng was just over twenty, but his martial arts were already renowned, and Phan Đình Phùng accepted him immediately, appointing him as Quân Cơ (Military Advisor). In every engagement, this commander always led from the front; during retreats, he was always the last to withdraw.

In the initial phase, 1886, the Hương Khê insurgents could not withstand the enemy's sweeping campaigns and had to withdraw to Phùng Công village in Hương Sơn District, then retreated into the forests and mountains to fight guerrilla-style, nearly disintegrating. In 1887, Phan Đình Phùng had to go north to seek out the scholar-gentry of Bắc Kỳ. Before departing, he instructed his generals to remain patient, await the right moment, avoid rash actions, and await his return to plan the restoration. All military affairs during this period he entrusted entirely to Cao Thắng. Cao labored day and night, fanning the dying flame of national salvation in the army's heart. By Cao Thắng's side at this time were Cao Nữu, Cao Đạt, and Nguyễn Niên. They all had to withdraw to Lê Động to reorganize their forces.

The greatest difficulty facing the insurgents at this time was the problem of weapons. Years of experience in the Việt-French clashes had shown that swords, lances, and clubs could not prevail against bronze cannon and warships. Cao Thắng devised a way to manufacture Western-style guns and ammunition. He thus became Vietnam's first mechanical engineer. Initially he cast 200 firelock muskets and recruited 400 comrades. He employed these slow-firing weapons cleverly, using them in two stages: one squad fired while another reloaded. By the time one squad finished firing, the other had reloaded and could continue. Through this alternating and rapid fire, the enemy was completely baffled, unable to understand where the insurgents' weapons came from.

Then, in a hand-to-hand saber battle on the Nghệ An-Hương Sơn road, he captured 17 rapid-fire rifles from the enemy, after killing two French officers and fifteen auxiliaries. He immediately took these rifles as models to the blacksmiths of Vân Tràng and Trung Lương villages (Can Lộc District, Hà Tĩnh Province), had them disassembled piece by piece, and copied them exactly. The work encountered many difficulties due to lack of materials and machinery. After purchasing some materials from Siam and finding some locally, after months of labor he cast 350 rifles identical to the enemy's Model 1874.

(1) Lieutenant Ch. Gosselin, author of "L'Empire d'Annam," criticized the Hương Sơn guns as follows: "I brought one of these rifles back to France; everyone recognized that it was an exact copy of our own manufactured weapon, astonishing the artillery officers. It was only inferior in the weakness of its spring and the lack of a rifled bore, making it less accurate at long range."

When Phan Đình Phùng returned from the north, the insurgent base was already fairly solid in both organization and numbers: the insurgents had 1,000 soldiers and 500 good rifles. His left-hand generals were Đề Niên, Đồ Vinh, Đề Đạt, Tuấn, Nguyễn Chánh, Lê Văn Tạc, Phan Đình Phong, Phan Đình Cần, and others.

The number of troops in each region varied according to the area's importance, averaging from about 100 soldiers to 500 per region, maintaining regular contact with General Headquarters to receive orders and battle plans. The insurgents' uniforms were uniform everywhere and notably well-regulated. Beside Commander Phan, there was a bodyguard unit commanded by Nguyễn Mục. Examining the insurgents' deployment, we see that from Quảng Bình to Thanh Hóa stretched a military system designed to disperse the colonial forces' strength, while individual units could support each other when encountering enemy sweeps. Beyond this military system, the insurgents also had mobile units dispatched to various places to interdict enemy communications and supply, and to raid enemy posts. Moreover, on many occasions, the insurgents lured the enemy out of their positions for ambushes, destroyed fortifications, and even once attacked Hà Tĩnh provincial city directly. When the enemy launched major campaigns against the insurgent base, the insurgents would slip behind the enemy's rear (in the plains) to attack strategic points, forcing the enemy to hastily withdraw.

Notably, the problem of food supply was solved quite adequately thanks to the active assistance of the people. The inhabitants of each locality were responsible for providing rice and grain to their local military region, as if paying taxes in a formal manner. From Vũ Quang up to Tri Khê, every few dặm there was a pit up to thirty trượng in circumference, dug deep into the ground. To prevent dampness, they burned firewood inside to dry and harden the pit before pouring in the grain; then they covered it with earth, concealing it like ordinary ground. Nearby, they kept a few implements for husking and pounding rice. Besides the grain stored in these underground granaries, the people also carried supplies directly to the main camp at Vũ Quang.

(2) Phan traveled north disguised as a Tú tài (bachelor) teaching in Sơn Tây. During this journey, he gathered political intelligence and public sentiment in Bắc Kỳ; afterward, he made contact with Cần Vương leaders in Hải Dương and Yên Thế. But he saw little hope of building an effective collaboration to resist the invaders with certain leaders there, due to numerous differences in political views; just as he was preparing to return to Hương Khê, General Cao Thắng sent men to welcome him back. By then the Hương Sơn forces were already well-prepared (1,000 men), but they needed Phan's presence — as a figure of great prestige and a national exile — to stir popular sentiment powerfully. Indeed, when the people learned that Phan was at Vũ Quang, able-bodied men flocked to join the insurgent army from all directions. They went up to the highlands of Quảng Bình, entered Thanh Lạng village to take the head of the traitor Trương Quang Ngọc. This was also the time when Messrs. Vũ Phát, Cầm Bá Thước, Nguyễn Cấp, and Ngô Quảng brought their forces to join Phan. According to T.V.G.'s historical materials, Phan had previously been in contact with Tôn Thất Thuyết and was appointed Bình Trung tướng (General of the Pacified Center) — suggesting that Thuyết either had not yet left the country or was still in contact from abroad with the Cần Vương leaders.

THE DECISIVE PHASE BEGINS — A PRELUDE TO TEN YEARS OF WARFARE

By 1889, the Hương Khê organization could be said to be thorough. Morale was optimistic, as the hearts of the people and soldiers in the four provinces of northern Trung Kỳ were greatly uplifted. Seeing the insurgents expanding visibly with proper plans, the French deployed numerous posts everywhere to blockade the area of insurgent activity and influence, paying particular attention to cutting off Hương Khê's supply routes and preventing the insurgents from descending to the plains. In Hương Khê alone, the enemy established up to 20 posts, each with 30 soldiers garrisoned. A portion of the auxiliary troops was kept mobile for reconnaissance, after which a force concentration would be made for attack. In this phase, the enemy applied their most cruel and insidious tactic: using Vietnamese to kill Vietnamese, through General Warnet's decree of 11 February 1886 and Governor-General Paul Bert's decree of 6 August 1886. After these two decrees were issued, the first organizations of auxiliary troops (lính khố xanh) sprouted like mushrooms to suppress the Văn Thân movement in Trung Kỳ. (In November 1889, the Indochina Supreme Council handed over the suppression of the insurgents entirely to the auxiliary troops.) (1)

(1) The French acknowledged: "Conquering the South was the achievement of the expeditionary force, but pacifying the Văn Thân uprisings was thanks to the indigenous troops — both red-band, blue-band, and yellow-band." (lính tập, khố đỏ, khố xanh, khố vàng)

The first battles of Hương Khê:

1) On 1 September 1889, Đề Niên established two small forts at Mỹ Hòa, at Cồn Chùa and Khe Ben, defeating the forces of auxiliary Lieutenant De Ninort. A few days earlier, the insurgents had killed one auxiliary sergeant and 16 auxiliary soldiers. The Tuần phủ (Provincial Governor) of Hà Tĩnh and Inspector Jacquet had to bring many troops to sweep the area.

2) On 15-16 December 1889, the insurgents raided the Dương Liễu post.

3) At the end of December 1889, the insurgents attacked Hương Sơn District, where Lieutenants Lambert and Roty were stationed.

4) In March 1890, the French post there was attacked again.

5) In March 1890, enemy troops came to sweep through La Sơn and Thường Sơn districts, where Đề Thăng and Phan Trọng Mưu resisted. In Hương Khê, Bang Trúc and Hiệp quản Diệp were also active.

6) On 13 April 1890, Đốc Chanh and Đốc Trạch ambushed the enemy at Hốt village. Many auxiliary and indigenous soldiers were eliminated.

7) On the night of 26-27 May 1890, an auxiliary sergeant named Hẹp at the Trường Lưu post defected to the insurgents, showing them the way to attack this post. The post commanders were Lieutenants Devoogt and Trouvé. On the night of 28 May, the attack took place; the enemy fled to Trường Lưu. On the night of the 31st, this post was attacked again, and then the insurgents attacked the Hương Sơn post.

8) On the 26th, at Hà Trai, an auxiliary unit under Sergeant Hai coming from Napé was ambushed by Đề Đạt.

9) On the night of the 3rd, the enemy post at Hương Sơn was breached by the insurgents, and the Linh Cẩm post was harassed.

10) On 4 June, Inspector Lambert was attacked above Hương Sơn.

11) On 26 June, Lambert and two auxiliary lieutenants were attacked at Gia Định, Đông Tai, and Hốt village. They fled, and the insurgents attacked Trường Lưu for the fourth time.

12) On the night of 28-29 June, there was a clash between the insurgents and the enemy at Trung Lễ and Thượng Ích villages. The enemy burned these two villages.

13) On 20 July, the insurgents ambushed a collaborator colonel coming to reinforce Linh Cảm. Two insurgent commanders, Đội Trạc and Sergeant Hẹp, were wounded.

On the night of 24 July, the insurgents attacked the Quý Châu post but failed; Lãnh Thiệt died in battle. The enemy discovered the situation of the insurgents under Đề Mậu in the area between Cây Chanh, Đồn Cốc, and Trấn Lương. Nghệ An Resident Luce organized an attack on Đề Mậu's post according to an encirclement plan. Lieutenant Haguet from Cây Chanh post surrounded the rear of Đề Mậu's camp; troops under Đinh Huân Dương and Lãnh Hy at Diễn Prefecture, and Cửu Cát at Đồng Cốc, surrounded the other sides. On 28 August, intense firing broke out; the enemy was too strong, and Đề Mậu had to temporarily withdraw. On the night of 2 November, the insurgents attacked Cửu Cát's force at Mạc Hạ post.

14) On the 29th, Đốc Tranh fought an enemy unit at Cầm Xuyên as they came from Hà Tĩnh and Kỳ Anh District.

15) On 14 September, the insurgents again attacked Lambert at Liễu village, 14 kilometers from Hà Tĩnh provincial seat. The enemy had Tuần phủ Đinh Nho Quang go to Trường Lưu and Nam Huân to bribe the people not to follow the insurgents.

16) In October 1890, the insurgents of Hương Khê and Hương Sơn districts combined forces with Đốc Chanh's troops to attack the Nam Huân post. The fighting lasted from 20 to 25 October at Gia Hanh and Hốt villages, and their surrounding areas. In December 1890, the enemy brought more troops to attack the Tháp camp area, about 6 kilometers from Nam Huân. The insurgents under Mai Văn Vinh still had enough strength to attack the Linh Cảm post (near Đông Thái) besides confronting the enemy's sweeping force. In this period, the insurgents held the initiative on battlefields in Thanh Chương, Hương Sơn, La Sơn, and Hương Khê districts.

17) On 2 February 1891, Bang biện Vinh ambushed an auxiliary unit moving from Nam Huân post to Nghệ.

18) From 2 to 9 March, Inspector Boudon swept from Tri Ban to the Quy Hợp pass and into Hương Sơn without meeting resistance; the insurgents had secretly concentrated at Thiên Thôn to intercept and free a number of our prisoners whom Lieutenant Hugnit was escorting from Nam Huân to Linh Cảm. The battle lasted four hours; on the insurgent side, Hiệp quản Diệp died in battle; on the enemy side, Hugnit escaped the encirclement. Meanwhile, Boudon marched through Trường Vật and Tháp camp back to Linh Cảm, where he was attacked.

19) From 2 to 5 March, Đốc Đóp and Bá hộ Thuận attacked Inspector Lambert right near the Hà Tĩnh provincial seat.

20) On 28 March, Đề Thành attacked Lieutenant Robert at Thượng Bôn.

21) At the beginning and end of April, the insurgents eliminated a number of auxiliary troops when they marched to attack a post, and the insurgents surrounded Đức Thọ on 24 April.

22) In June 1891, Boudon's troops were ambushed by the insurgents; in July, there was a clash at the Commercial Office in Can Lộc.

23) On 15 July, Lãnh binh Dương Lê fought with Lieutenant Duravisseau. Facing the insurgents' scattered but continuous activities, the enemy had Inspector Boudon, Hugnit, Bourgeois, Focher, Magistris, Marquet, and Robert — officers stationed in the Linh Cảm, Chợ Bông, and Tri Ban (Hà Tĩnh) areas — launch a large-scale sweep from 1-15 September everywhere, but only a few minor battles occurred between the two sides.

24) On 19 November, Duravisseau, Magistrit, and Montigneau led troops to Trường Xim, where they were fiercely attacked by the insurgents; the fighting lasted 1.5 hours, after which the insurgents withdrew to Vũ Quang.

25) On the night of 19 December, Đề Thắng led troops to attack the enemy at Lương Điền.

26) On 7 March 1892, the insurgents attacked the Trung Lễ post because a group of enemy informers there had pointed out insurgents for the enemy to capture. The insurgents also captured two reactionary Bá hộ (hundred-man chiefs) and imprisoned them at Lạc Hà, then ambushed Thạch Hà District, capturing the Tri huyện (District Magistrate). This was the work of Bá hộ Thuận. Meanwhile, Cao Thắng had his troops disguised as auxiliaries and captured Tuần phủ Đinh Nho Quang, who then held the position of Tiễu phủ quân vụ (Pacification Commissioner) for Hương Sơn District. This collaborationist official had once written a letter insulting Phan Đình Phùng, but when captured, Phan did not kill him, merely forcing him to pay a ransom. In summary, from the execution of the traitor Trương Quang Ngọc to the capture alive of Đinh Nho Quang, the insurgents made the collaborators tremble in fear and dare not act boldly.

In the early months of 1892, a notable event was Vice-Resident Damade's order for a major sweep into the Trường Xim area. Đề Thăng's insurgents were present there. All auxiliary troops in Hà Tĩnh were mobilized to pour into the area between the Ngàn Phố and Cả Rivers, and into the Trường Xim forest.

The insurgents divided their forces into two parts: one part stayed to resist the sweep; the other slipped away to operate near the Hà Tĩnh provincial seat and the various prefectures and districts.

On 2 March 1892, a few small battles occurred at Trường Xim; the enemy managed to rescue Đinh Nho Quang and Cử Nhan.

In Kỳ Anh and Nam Huân, the insurgents planted false information about Bá hộ Thuận's whereabouts for Hugnit. On 20 June, Hugnit took a guide, but upon arrival found only a small, long-abandoned camp. At this moment, the insurgents surrounded the enemy in a dangerous trap. Lieutenant Briscout hastily brought troops from Kỳ Anh to reinforce but was ambushed en route. In two hours of fighting, the enemy suffered heavy losses before fighting their way back to Kỳ Anh. Meanwhile, Hugnit, wandering around the abandoned camp, heard that Briscout was being badly defeated and that the Voi post was under attack; he hastily divided his troops into two columns and rushed to Voi, but the insurgents had already left. In the end, at the end of April, Damade's sweep forces had to withdraw after two months of hardship and suffering.

28) From April to August 1892, the following events occurred: Auxiliary and indigenous troops foraging near Hương Bộc, one kilometer from the Ngụy Giang post, were ambushed on 8 June. A week later, the insurgents swept through Trung Lễ, Đông Khê, and Văn Lâm villages, punishing a number of collaborators and killing a collaborator Hiệp quản at Đông Khê. The Linh Cảm post commander came to the rescue but was ambushed at Văn Lâm. Troops from Nam Huân and Tri Ban came to reinforce Linh Cảm and were also attacked. On the evening of 18 June, an insurgent supply convoy also clashed with the enemy at Hà Tĩnh, causing post commander Robert to come to support at Trường Vật; the insurgents fought against De Soulages (1) and Robert. On 7 July, Hugnit and Le Parc sought out Bá hộ Thuận's camp; the two sides fought briefly, then the insurgents moved on.

(1) De Soulages was the post commander at Nam Huân.

THE INSURGENTS STRIKE INTO HÀ TĨNH

Many sweeping campaigns occurred, yet the insurgents' activities never ceased, infuriating the enemy. In the face of these victories, the people grew ever more enthusiastic in supporting the insurgents with food. The enemy saw they had to make a strong effort and launch battles before the rainy season to end the presence of the Hương Khê forces, which had become an abscess in their eyes.

According to their intelligence, Phan Đình Phùng and many of his generals, along with 150 riflemen, were stationed at Hội Trung. Cao Đạn and a few insurgents were at La Sơn; Bá hộ Thuận at Trại Chè; Đốc Chanh at Thiền Thôn with about 100 comrades.

They devised a plan to attack Trại Chè first, to draw the main Hương Khê force off-course; then, catching them by surprise, they would strike directly at Hội Trung with a large force, after which they would finish off the insurgents on the right bank of the Ngàn Sâu.

Hugnit, Bourgeois, Le Parc, and Lãnh binh Trọng were ordered to sweep from Hà Tĩnh to Trại Chè. Their march began from 5 to 9 August 1892, with the following division of tasks:

— Lieutenant Haguet and Focher led 80 auxiliaries to Hương Khê.
— Robert and Bourgeois led 60 soldiers to Trạc Cuội (Hương Sơn).
— Lambert with Lieutenants Crémon and Mariani led 50 soldiers to Hương Khê.

These three columns were ordered to attack the insurgent camp simultaneously on the night of 12-13 August. The enemy's plan was poorly researched and based on inaccurate intelligence; consequently, they did not deploy a force proportionate to the situation. In fact, at this time the insurgents were present throughout the prefectures and districts, communication between units was tight, and the insurgents, besides resisting the sweep, could also turn from defense to offense. Commander Phan fully understood all of the enemy's calculations.

At 9 PM on 12 August, Robert attacked the insurgents' outpost; the two sides fought briefly, then the insurgents withdrew. The next morning, Robert advanced into the forest to search but found nothing. On the night of 13 August at 10 PM, the insurgents counterattacked quite strongly. The enemy had 16 killed, 5 seriously wounded, and several more pierced by spikes and unable to stand. They panicked and fled. Yet at this time, only a few kilometers from Robert, Lambert was marching on the other side of the mountain, knowing nothing of this; thus he could not come to reinforce. As for Lambert, on the morning of 14 August he reached an abandoned camp, so he came and went empty-handed. By the end of that month, all auxiliary units were forced to withdraw to their bases.

While the enemy was wandering in the areas of Khê Thượng, Hương Sơn, and Hương Khê, searching deep forests and thickets, on 23 August, Bá hộ Thuận, under secret orders from Phan, brought his troops close to Hà Tĩnh town. He feigned an attack on the market quarter to create a disturbance, while the main body split into two columns and launched a fierce assault on the auxiliary barracks and the Hà Tĩnh prison, which then held 70 captured insurgents. (In the prison attack, Bá hộ Thuận also aimed to rescue Duy Thịnh, his elder brother.)

The insurgents opened fire everywhere, catching the enemy and the population completely by surprise. Đội Sinh, in charge of the auxiliary barracks, shouted for his men to resist desperately. The collaborationist Chánh and Phó Lãnh binh brought many troops to confront Bá hộ Thuận outside the town. By this time, the two main assault columns had already entered the auxiliary barracks and the prison. Thanks to this attack, the imprisoned soldiers broke free from their chains, as the external assault and internal prison break overwhelmed the guards, who fled and were killed in small numbers. However, Duy Thịnh was held in a separate blockhouse within the barracks, on the Tuần phủ's special orders, and the insurgents could not find him. Nevertheless, this battle was a clear victory; the insurgents suffered no losses.

On the evening of 28 August, Nguyễn Cấp fought against De Soulages to pin down the enemy while a supply unit transported food from Đông Khê (La Sơn District). Because of the raid on Hà Tĩnh town, the enemy concentrated all their forces in the plains for searches, sweeps, and terrorizing the population from early September through early October. During this time, the insurgents had all vanished into the high mountains and deep forests.

In the midst of this victorious period, a collaborator led the French to Đốc Chanh's headquarters, and Đốc Chanh was caught in an enemy encirclement and died in battle. Enemy sweeps resumed. Not wanting to fight at a disadvantage, the insurgents withdrew from the Phương Nga post on 16 November and the Trường Vật post on 30 November. On 15 December, there was a clash between the insurgents and Bourgeois and Robert at Quạt Mountain.

At the beginning of February 1893, the French brought auxiliary and indigenous troops to sweep the Trường Vật and Ngàn Trươi mountain ranges. They brought 120 riflemen across the Trường Vật peak, advancing from south to north, and stationed some troops to block other routes for a pincer attack. The commanders of these forces were still Lambert, Mariani, Haguet, and others. In executing the encirclement plan, De Soulages on 7 January 1893 had brought his troops northward, departing from Khiêm Ích, but encountered only an abandoned camp. Haguet was blocked at Khê Thượng after pulling back from Trường Xim to the south; unable to move beyond this point, he had to turn back to Hòa Duyệt. Lambert entered Ngọn stream on 23 January and was also stopped. Bourgeois on 21 January could not advance either and hastily contacted Haguet; on 6 February, they were fiercely attacked by the insurgents, with 20 wounded. They had to withdraw completely. A few days later, the insurgents returned. Through the end of March, the two sides continued pursuing each other. On 29 March, Đề Thăng encountered Bourgeois at Mỹ Duệ, then withdrew to Trại Chè. At the beginning of April, Đề Nam clashed with Bourgeois, and Robert was also present at the above position.

At the end of April, Hugnit, now promoted to Inspector and holding the position of Regional Commander for Hương Sơn and Hương Khê, set out to attack Trại Chè. But many of Phan's columns slipped back to the plains, attacking the Trung Lương, Kim Chúc (in Can Lộc), Hương Khê, and many other enemy bases in Nghi Xuân District. Hugnit had to withdraw again to protect areas under threat. From May to July, Đề Nam and Đốc Trạch harassed the enemy fiercely, employing the tactic: "When the enemy comes, we go; when the enemy goes, we come" — that is, avoiding enemy contact to strike them when they exposed themselves or were caught off guard.


THE PLAINS CAMPAIGN FAILS — CAO THẮNG FALLS IN BATTLE

The Hương Sơn uprising began in late Ất Dậu (1885) and by 1893 had lasted exactly eight years. It had experienced all vicissitudes — highs and lows — and had gathered many glorious victories. The insurgent commanders always grasped the factors of popular support and geographical advantage to resist enemy sweeps and to attack the enemy at favorable opportunities. But in the final years of the 19th century, the French colonialists had established nearly complete administrative machinery throughout the plains, and their system of forts and ramparts daily pushed deeper into the forest and mountain regions, tightening the noose around the insurgents. The people's ability to supply provisions correspondingly became more difficult, making the enemy's suppression ever more effective. Constricted within an ever-shrinking territory, the insurgents saw their future growing darker — especially as the military regions from Quảng Bình to Thanh Hóa lost contact with each other and could no longer support one another as before. General Cao Thắng saw that great battles must be fought to break this deadlock; otherwise, the insurgents would simply wither away before their eyes.

Phan Đình Phùng hesitated, fearing that if major battles did not succeed, the insurgent army could collapse very quickly, for the enemy's forces were always fully supplied in every respect. He also perceived that simply lingering among the mountains of the four provinces of Thanh-Nghệ-Tĩnh-Bình without breaking out onto the plains would mean defeat anyway. In the end, he accepted his brave general's plan: bring the main force to attack the city of Nghệ An, while the military regions in other provinces would act simultaneously to support the general offensive and divide the enemy's forces to ensure victory. If the insurgents could seize the provincial capital of Nghệ An, the other military regions would also attack their respective local administrative seats. Phan was to hold the enemy at the inland front, while Cao Thắng blocked the enemy from the north and also interdicted the sea approaches.

We may consider this battle (in the tenth lunar month of 1893) to be Hương Khê's general counteroffensive against the enemy — a final convulsion to live or die, to open a bright future or to perish gloriously. Then Cao, from his Ngàn Trươi base, led 1,000 troops directly toward Nghệ An, dividing his forces into two columns: he and Nguyễn Niên commanded 500 troops for the assault; Cao Nữu commanded 500 as reserves.

In the initial phase, the insurgents advanced to the Nỏ post. The commander there, a collaborator Lieutenant Phiến with 100 soldiers, realized his force was too weak to withstand the famous general Cao Thắng. He divided his troops in two: half held the position, firing intensely outward; the other half slipped outside to set up an ambush and then strike the insurgents from behind. His tactic worked, and when the insurgents attacked the post that night, they were met with enemy fire from both front and rear. Cao Thắng was unfortunately hit by a bullet. He died on the battlefield, his heart still burning with the desire to kill the foe and save the country, at the age of twenty-nine — having given himself wholly to the national cause. Thus the plan to break out onto the plains collapsed with the sudden death of the great general. The remains of this national hero were brought back to Ngàn Trươi for burial. The officers and soldiers of Hương Khê were heartbroken, their very bowels torn asunder.

When Phan Đình Phùng received this news, he was struck silent for a long time. Later, he wrote two couplets to honor and mourn his comrade, who had shared ten years of hardship with him:

"Công cầu tất thành, kích tiếp thế tảo thanh quốc tặc."
(The work was destined to succeed — to strike and sweep the nation's foes.)
"Sự nan dự liệu, cử yên tích di thiều tư nhân y."
(Events are hard to foresee — raising a hand, what remains? We miss you.)

And:

"Vi tiệp tiên tử, thiên ý vị hà?"
(To die before victory — what is heaven's intent?)
"Hữu chí phất thành, anh hùng di hĩ."
(To have ambition unfulfilled — the hero's eternal regret.)

Then Phan read a funeral oration composed in Nôm by Vũ Phát of Kỳ Anh District:

[Alas!]

For ten years the precious sword was ready, the heroic spirit ever honed;
In one moment upon the battlefield — why did the jealous fates begrudge you so?

Remembering your spirit:

A hero of rare talent, the nation's restoration your purpose;
Shouldering half the burden of the land, your wings spread to the four winds;
Meeting the nation in the turmoil of war, you answered the call to share the sovereign's sorrow.

Leaving your family for the soldier's trade, your heart in fighting the foe, your toil was great;
You aspired to follow Nhạc and Mục, your war cry striking fear on the field.

A born genius, you sought to emulate Võ Hầu, devising guns and ammunition with what skill!
The mandarins' favor gave you the seal of supreme command;
Your noble titles from the court, the banner of the unicorn, the sword polished on stone — determined not to fail your sovereign.

Who could foresee — in one morning, without warning, the stray bullet's course, on the battlefield — who knows fate's decree?

In your three decades of spring and autumn, though not yet many, on horseback many a time you wielded the whip, upholding the bonds of loyalty to reveal the face of a true man.

Beyond ten years of frost, your achievement — who knows how great? — before the palace gate, a star suddenly fell, the celestial spirits hastening to carry off the warrior's work.

Thiên Nhãn mountain lies exposed to the cold wind's breath — how piteous this man of loyalty!

The three-forked river shimmers under the moon's reflection, recalling the man of constancy — what heartbreak!

Better to die, my hand against the foe, following our ancestors — all the clearer!

Those who live, how many have surrendered — in the eyes of heaven, all the more shameful!

Now I proclaim:

The seventh-day rite has come,
The offerings are laid before you.

A cup of wine, wafting the scent of righteous spirit — before the tent, all the officials assemble;
A stick of incense, the smoke of white silk rising — beneath the altar, the gifts vie with each other.

Bitter sorrow — your two aged parents, leaning on staff, weeping for their son — the deep love cannot leave the underworld.

Pitiful sight — your young children, trusting in their uncle's care — may fortune bring them the cassia branch.

Your spirit, if it perceives this offering, protect us that our troops be strong and steadfast.

Your soul, if it has power and awareness, in days to come you will receive the royal canonization.

Enough! Enough!

The crimson gate and golden tower must hold the departed; your hundred battles won, you leave a fragrant name to show the hero's face.

The bronze cannon and silver sword are left to others; but issue your command to the three armies, with strong breath build the foundation of peace.

Alas, alas!
How can I recount it all!

Upon hearing of Cao Thắng's death in battle, the enemy seized the opportunity of the insurgents' grief over their commander's loss and urgently concentrated forces for a general offensive. But they were mistaken — the insurgents still preserved their fighting spirit. Nevertheless, we cannot forget that our material strength had been considerably eroded, which inevitably affected the resistance. Now that surrender seemed more opportune, the enemy deployed Hoàng Cao Khải, a great traitor whom anyone reading the history of the anti-French struggle remembers, in his capacity as Khâm sai đại thần (Imperial Commissioner). In the fourth lunar month (1894), Hoàng began the task of persuading the Hương Khê insurgents to surrender. The French knew that Hoàng Cao Khải and Phan Đình Phùng were fellow villagers (both from Đông Thái) and also shared a literary background, so no one could negotiate with Phan more easily. But the hero Phan resolutely refused the arguments — outwardly seemingly earnest and well-reasoned, emotionally stirring — of the collaborator Hoàng. (1)

(1) See the letter of Hoàng Thái Xuyên to Phan Đình Phùng and the response of the warrior of Quạt Mountain in the appendix section.

On 1 March 1894, the insurgents attacked the Chợ Phố post, and two days later lured the enemy to a nearby village to divide their forces. On 4 March, the insurgents disguised themselves as ordinary folk and attacked the enemy at 9 AM. That same day, Bricout and his auxiliary unit were attacked at a different location.

On 29 March 1894, Đốc Binh Nguyễn Bảo killed the collaborator Lieutenant Phiến — the traitor who had shot General Cao Thắng — in an ambush at Vạn Sơn (Nam Đồng). Lãnh Lợi commanded the ambush that day.

On 31 March, Bá hộ Thuận launched another raid on Hà Tĩnh town, attempting to carry out Cao Thắng's old plan, but the result did not meet expectations. For three consecutive months (April, May, June 1894), numerous clashes occurred between the insurgent commanders Phan Duy Cần, Đốc Cơ, Đốc Trạch and the French De Soulages and Philippe.

On 25 June and the night of 12 July, the French post at Quy Hợp was raided by insurgents.

On 11 July, Rallier du Baty's men sought out Bá hộ Thuận. The next morning, both sides fired and cursed at each other from within the forest. Many enemy soldiers fell into spike pits and were raided.

On 14-27 July, Samaran led a sweep of Diễn Châu Prefecture and Anh Sơn District. Nearing the provincial capital (Vinh), they were intercepted by insurgents.

On 27 July, De Soulages was attacked again at Đại Hàm and had to flee. Throughout August, many engagements occurred at Ngàn Cổ and Mô Hổ mountain between Samaran, De Soulages, Gruault, Rallier du Baty, Focher, and the insurgents.

THE FINAL BATTLES OF THE HƯƠNG KHÊ INSURGENTS — THE ĐẠI HÀM BATTLE

At the end of 1894, Phan Đình Phùng had to move his general headquarters to Đại Hàm mountain. By then his forces were already weakened. Not long before, the enemy had struck directly at the main camp and the insurgents could not stop them. Here, both sides struggled bitterly. Phan was stationed on the mountain, with the enemy encircling the foot. This mountain belonged to Tĩnh Điệm village, in the Điệm thứ region. The regional commander was Cao Đạn, with 400 men and about 200 rifles. The enemy, by contrast, had up to 1,000 men, both soldiers and civilian laborers. By this time, the insurgents faced a severe food shortage. The enemy besieged them tightly and attacked fiercely. Both advance and retreat were difficult. Phan hesitated, then consulted everyone. Cao Đạn and Nguyễn Mục volunteered to attack first. Seeing that everyone was determined to break the encirclement or die, Phan ordered the insurgents to open fire on the enemy's vanguard. The two sides fought for fifteen consecutive days. As many auxiliaries as climbed the mountain fell. The insurgents calmly waited until they were close before firing. Enemy losses were considerable, and they had to call for 150 more reinforcements from Nghệ An. This force divided into two columns and charged up the mountain. When they reached the summit, the insurgents had already left the position the night before and had returned to Quạt Mountain (Hương Khê).

In July 1895, the insurgents withdrew to Vũ Quang, intending to advance to the most formidable mountain peak to establish a base. But they arrived too late — the enemy had already seized this peak. The insurgents had to choose another site in the Vũ Quang valley. Phan was present there. Before the insurgents' camp, the Vũ Quang River rushed violently from the heights. The insurgents anticipated that the enemy would bring a large force to attack, so they cut many large trees into logs, tied them into rafts, and placed them upstream, waiting for the enemy to cross. And indeed, the enemy fell into the trap. A few days later, as they were wading into the water, the insurgents released the rafts. Hundreds of logs from the head of the rapids hurtled down like arrows; the enemy was knocked over, wounded, and killed, unable to scramble ashore. Additionally, they were caught in crossfire from the insurgents on both banks. Three French officers and over 100 soldiers were eliminated. The enemy lost 50 rifles and much equipment and ammunition. The Đại Hàm battle is recorded as a brilliant victory in the midst of the insurgents' most desperate circumstances.

THE CAO KHÊ BATTLE

On 17 January 1895, the enemy sought revenge for the Đại Hàm defeat. Samaran led his troops to sweep the Trường Vật mountain range, and on 4 February he and Đốc Trạch clashed in the afternoon. Đốc Trạch had fully prepared his positions, waiting for the enemy to arrive. This reckless, strategy-lacking commander charged straight into the insurgents' position, dismissing the insurgents as insignificant — only to be hit by a pincer attack from Đốc Trạch's two wings. Samaran was shot in the neck and fell before his troops; the auxiliaries immediately fled. Our troops pursued. On 5 February, Đề Đạt's forces also routed an auxiliary unit and then withdrew. Although the Hương Khê forces won several consecutive battles, the enemy still commanded the battlefield and still held the insurgents within their grip.

On 20 February, Huguet sent a collaborator official with an auxiliary unit to attack the insurgents at Lang Trại in Nghi Xuân District — and the fighting ceased until May, when it resumed. From this stage onward, the insurgents focused on evasion to preserve their forces, as they had encountered enough obstacles.

On 10 May, Marquet attacked Phan Đình Nghinh's forces at Trường Vi. Two days later, Lý Võ raided Lieutenant Bại's unit, which was wiped out. At the end of May, Lãnh Dao was also captured by the enemy at Diễn Châu Prefecture. In June, there were more battles between Phan Đình Nghinh and Đề... with Huguet, Fonne, De Soulages, Gruault, Hugnit at Trường Vật and Công Rhè. Each time, the insurgents sank deeper into a defensive posture, though still active; the enemy, meanwhile, intensified their terrorization of surrounding villages to cut off all sources of supply from the people.

In July 1895, Nguyễn Thân was appointed Khâm mạng đại thần (Imperial Commissioner), representing the new feudal Nam Triều to suppress the insurgents in the three provinces of Nghệ-Tĩnh-Bình. Duvillier, the French Resident of Nghệ An, served as commander-in-chief of the collaborationist forces in these three provinces. Under his command were 15 newly appointed and veteran inspectors, supervising 3,000 auxiliary troops, launching yet another general offensive against the Hương Khê forces — the final blockade.

In this phase, the Hương Khê main force, along with the military regions' forces, were scattered due to lost communication and supply routes. Food, weapons, and troop numbers were critically depleted, with no solution in sight. The people's support was further restricted, dangerously so. Each time the enemy attacked strongly, the insurgents could only flee from Giăng Màn mountain to Quạt mountain and then back to Vũ Quang mountain. In this darkest hour, Phan Đình Phùng, exhausted by the ceaseless hardship of combat, fell gravely ill with dysentery. He could not stay in any location for more than three days; the army's spirit perceptibly declined by the day. On 12 July 1895, De Soulages captured rice, salt, and dried fish from a supply convoy meant for the insurgents. On 19 July 1895, Phó lãnh binh Hồ Lai was captured. Đề Mậu and Đề Vinh, stationed at Quy Châu, Diễn Châu, and Anh Sơn, tried to slip down to the plains but failed; soon after, they were surrounded and fiercely attacked by Stenger and Allain's forces. The enemy concentrated their troops at Thổ Hoàng, expecting to capture Phan, believing he was then at Vũ Quang, but when they arrived, Phan had already left.

On 21 January 1896 (lunar calendar), Phan could struggle no longer. He passed away on Quạt Mountain, aged forty-nine.

Before drawing his last breath, he left a deeply moving poem:

Nhung trường phụng mạng thập canh đông,
Võ lược y nhiên vị tấu công,
Cùng hộ ngao thiên nan trạch nhạn,
Phi đồ biến địa thượng đồn phong,
Cửu trùng xa giá quan san ngoại,
Tứ hải nhân dân thủy hỏa trung.
Trách vọng du long ưu du đại,
Tướng môn thâm tự quý anh hùng.

[In free translation:]

Ten winters I have borne the charge upon the field,
My martial plans — still no achievement sealed.
The starving cry to heaven, filling the land,
Rebels swarm like bees upon the sand.
The royal carriage beyond far mountain pass,
The people suffer as in fire and flood, alas!
The greater the charge, the heavier the toil,
A general's house — a hero's worth must foil.

Like the final phase of many uprisings before it, Hương Khê's last days were truly sorrowful and tragic.

Many commanders who had long evaded the enemy in the treacherous mountains and forests fell ill, nearly all of them. A small number — such as Đốc Phương, Đốc Trạch, Nguyễn Duật — no longer had the will to continue fighting and surrendered. Another group that surrendered later included Đề Mậu, Tạc Ngan, Phan Đình Nghinh, Phan Đình Phong, Phan Đình Cần, and Phan Đình Trinh. The enemy executed 23 members of the Hương Khê command structure. Tôn Thất Hoàng, Hiệp Tuân, Phan Đình Thoại, and Tôn Thất Định fought until captured; Đề Đạt and some others withdrew to Siam.

(1) The biography "Phan Đình Phùng" by Đào Trinh Nhất, published by Tân Việt (p. 231).

One noteworthy detail: even at the final moment, with the enemy surrounding them like an iron net, Phan's generals still buried him with care. A log of golden-cassia wood was hollowed into a coffin, sealed very tightly, and lowered into the earth of Quạt Mountain. After the burial, the insurgents flattened the grave mound and disguised the area so the enemy could not find it. Unfortunately, ten days later, someone driven by greed for fame and reward showed Nguyễn Thân where to dig. This great traitor had Phan's remains burned to ash, ground fine as powder, mixed with gunpowder, and fired into the La River. He also had a stone stele erected on Tùng Sơn peak in central Vietnam recording his traitorous achievements, but a few years later lightning struck and shattered it, and it disappeared — so that people might forget the Khâm sai Nguyễn's slaughter of his own countrymen.

But Phan's legacy from that day onward was recorded in history; his name lives forever in the hearts of the people. His indomitable spirit illuminated the nation, and the Fatherland will never cease to be grateful to him. The scholars of Nghệ-Tĩnh offered this funeral couplet:

"Thành bại anh hùng mạc luận, thử cô trung, thử đại nghĩa, thế dữ chư quân tử thủy chung. Châu chỉ anh, Mạc chỉ linh, độc thư mỗi niệm cương thường trọng. Khả hận giả thày điền đại hạ, nhất mộc nan chi! Cung lãnh yên tiêu, thùy nhân bất tác thâm sơn oản. Huống đương nhật long phi vân ám cộng. Ta nhân sự vô thường khả liên La Việt giang san bách niên văn hiến phiên cung mã."

"Of heroes, speak not of success or failure — this lone loyalty, this great righteousness, bound with all noble men from first to last. The pearl of Châu, the spirit of Mạc — each reading recalls the weight of duty. What bitter regret: the great house totters, a single beam cannot hold! The palace cold, the smoke dispelled — who does not sigh in the deep mountains? And in those days when the dragon soared and clouds darkened — alas, the fickleness of human affairs! Pity the La Việt mountains and rivers — a hundred years of civilization now turned to battlefields."

"Cổ kim thiên địa vô cùng, nhi lưu thủy, nhi cao phong, đồng thử đại trượng phu vũ trụ. Lam chỉ phong, Hồng chỉ tuyết, xung hàn vô nại bách tùng điêu. Vị hà tai: hội quyết đồi ba, trung lưu để trụ! Tính di vật hoán, hà nhân bất khởi cố viên tình? Cập thử thời nhạn tán phong xuy, kham thán thiên tâm mạc trợ. Độc thử tùng, mai khí tiết, nhất tử tinh thần quán đầu ngưu."

"Heaven and earth, ancient and modern, know no end — the flowing water, the high peak — all share with this great man the universe. The wind of Lam, the snow of Hồng — the hundred pines endure the cold. Why? The waves break upon the shore, the pillar stands midstream! Stars shift and things change — who does not feel for the garden of old? At this hour the wild geese scatter, the wind blows — one sighs that Heaven's heart lends no aid. Only this pine, this plum's spirit and integrity — one death, and his spirit pierces the constellation."

Summing up all events, the Cần Vương movement in Trung Kỳ was completely extinguished with the death of Phan Đình Phùng (1895). This movement can be divided into two phases. The first phase, while Emperor Hàm Nghi was still at large, lasted from 1885 to 1888. After the Edict, uprisings erupted in scattered fashion everywhere from southern Trung Kỳ to northern Bắc Kỳ, but they were disorganized, fragmented, and could not last long (only two or three years), because the forces lacked organization, training, weapons, and ammunition. Though the people participated or supported them, they still perished and were easily suppressed by the enemy. The Cần Vương leaders in this phase could only stir patriotic feeling and the people's love of freedom, and preserve the nation's heroic spirit while awaiting a brighter future.

With Phan Đình Phùng, the Cần Vương movement entered a second phase, from the time Emperor Hàm Nghi was captured and exiled to North Africa. The insurgents had gained considerable experience in both organization and combat. The national salvation movement rose very high even without an emperor. (At this time, Đồng Khánh was emperor, but the people regarded him and his court as mere puppets.) Yet Phan could not expand the scope of the anti-French struggle beyond the four provinces of northern Trung Kỳ. And even within this scope, command was not as tight as desired, and the results of battles could not open the way for the uprising to break out to the plains.

Meanwhile, the colonial collaborators advanced far more through diplomacy than through military means. France had gained control over Cambodia, Laos, Siam, and China, thus sealing Vietnam's borders. With the border gates blocked, our revolutionaries lost all external aid and supply routes — which was utterly disastrous. Even on our own soil, the enemy confined the insurgents within ever-narrower territories, plucking their feathers and trimming their wings until the Roc birds of the Vietnamese Revolution withered and died. Moreover, the enemy gradually established the Governor-General's office, the Superior Resident's office, the Imperial Commissioner's offices, the various Residencies, and the large and small auxiliary posts — from the central lowlands up to the mountains — driving the insurgent forces deep into the forests to die from bullets, poisoned water, hunger, and misery.

The Hương Sơn uprising, after ten years, came to an end. It was the most brilliantly notable among the national salvation movements of its time, achieving many victories from 1885 to 1895 if one considers the balance of forces between us and the enemy: a few troops, scarce provisions and weapons, yet they resisted several foreign armies that had already established their domination over our country. The Cần Vương movement in Trung Kỳ ended here, but it remains a point of pride for our nation in the history of the anti-French struggle.

Some observations: Comparing the anti-French movement in southern Trung Kỳ (from Quảng Nam southward) with the northern Trung Kỳ resistance (Thanh-Nghệ-Tĩnh), we see that the latter was markedly stronger, more sustained, and more consistent. The French struggled far more against the resistance here and had to sacrifice no small amount of manpower and material. The reason is that northern Trung Kỳ had a dense population, abundant resources, and a long heroic tradition. The people's endurance here was also greater, having faced centuries of Trịnh-Nguyễn division — a trial and forging far more severe than in other regions. Thanh-Nghệ-Tĩnh also had a larger territory for combat, which was an additional advantage. In suppressing the Cần Vương movements in this region, the colonialists also aimed to crush the resistance spirit of the Vietnamese patriotic feudal class, because this group had great prestige among the Vietnamese people. They thought that if the patriotic feudalists were eliminated, the resistance to foreign domination would end. But they were mistaken. Vietnamese history proves that the fundamental driving force of the Resistance is the masses — yesterday as today — and the feudalists or scholar-gentry only served as leaders. Even if the leadership class is eliminated, the Vietnamese masses will always produce other leaders from their midst to assume this role, no less ardent. The Vietnamese Resistance across many generations is a many-headed snake: cut off one head, another grows; suppressed in one place, it appears elsewhere; it is everywhere, sometimes falling silent but never truly dying. Thus no imperialist invader can ever hold this country under their domination for long; and if they win, the victory is only temporary.

Readers will surely not forget that under the Ming dynasty, in a council discussing intervention in Vietnam during the time when Mạc Đăng Dung usurped the throne from Lê Thái Tổ's descendants, the incompetent scholar-officials observed: "Never has China been able to maintain long-term control over the southern regions since the Hán, Đường, Tống, Nguyên, and Minh..." Now, when we examine the anti-French resistance of the Cần Vương movement and the people in Bắc Kỳ, we understand further how enduring and powerful the Vietnamese national spirit is — so that today we may firmly grasp the concept of struggle in this era of the strong prevailing over the weak, the clever surviving the foolish.

THREE NOTEWORTHY LETTERS

Letter from Hoàng Cao Khải to Phan Đình Phùng:

"To the esteemed elder Phan Đình Nguyên,

We have been separated, distant from each other, through many summers and winters, and already seventeen years have passed. With the affairs of the nation and the world, the roads of North and South covered in dust, each of us has gone our separate way, yet in dreams our spirits often meet — distance is not the issue. Sitting and reflecting on days past, when we were still in our homeland, our friendship was so deep — how can words express it?

"Since you raised the banner of rebellion, your righteous spirit and loyal heart have been evident to all who see and hear. I often hear officials speak of you, and every one of them sighs in admiration and holds you in great esteem. Truly, the heart of righteousness moves even foreigners; they share in the same sentiment — it is not only those of the same spirit who feel thus.

"On the day the capital fell and the royal carriage fled, you stepped forward with righteous indignation. Considering the circumstances of that time, what you did was entirely right — everyone says so. However, the situation has now changed. Ask yourself whether anything more can be done. Even the poorly educated and slow-witted would answer no. How much more so you, a man of outstanding talent — surely you have thought of this? I venture to consider your intention: you must think, 'I will do according to my strength, exhaust my abilities; doing what should be done is my part; whether it succeeds or not is Heaven's affair. I only know to pledge myself to my country until death.' Therefore you persist to the end, and no one can change that resolve.

"There is one thing: I see our homeland's condition these days and my heart aches. I have wanted to present my humble views for your enlightened consideration. But many times I have opened the paper, ground the ink, and set down my brush — only to put it aside with a sigh. Why? Because I know your heart is firm as rock and cannot be moved by words. Moreover, our circumstances differ, our positions differ; my words may not reach you — and even if they did, they might not enter your ears, serving only to make our old friend laugh at me for a fool.

"Now, upon the Governor-General's (1) return, he discussed the affairs of our province with me and urged me to send someone to inform you that you are a man who understands great principles. Even if you care nothing for yourself or your family, you should still think of saving the people in your locality. The Governor-General did not say this to just anyone; he said it to me because he believes I have old ties with you and that you might listen. If I truly could not speak, would I have remained silent?

(1) The Governor-General of Indochina at that time was De Lanessan, who had retired to France and recently returned.

"Consider: the Governor-General is a man from another country, ten thousand miles away, yet he concerns himself with and worries about our people in this way. How much more should we, who were born and raised in this land — the land of our parents' inheritance — have any reason to remain indifferent? What will future generations say of us?

"Those in high positions must take compassion for the people as their foundation. No one who does not love the people can truly be loyal to the sovereign. What you have done these many years — call it loyalty, it is indeed loyalty — but what benefit have the people gained that they must suffer such misery? Whose fault is that? If you say, 'I have acted for the world and care nothing for my family,' that is all very well. But sacrificing your own family may be permissible — but to sacrifice every family in the entire region — how can your conscience bear it? To let our homeland be devastated and ruined, with officials leading nowhere, and still act thus? I believe that if you persist in this unyielding course, I fear that the entire Lam River and Hồng Mountain region will become a lake of fish — not merely a worry for our single village of Đông Thái.

"I have pondered long and finally say to myself with relief: 'Good! This I can present for your guidance.' Do you think it is proper?

"Yet your situation is like riding a tiger — you have already mounted and now wish to dismount; how difficult!

"If I had no confidence in myself, I would never dare speak so recklessly, lest I bring trouble upon an old friend. But fortunately, I have known the Governor-General for many years, and I am also well-acquainted with the Imperial Commissioner in the Capital and the Resident of Nghệ-Tĩnh. Previously, Trần Phiên Sứ and Phan Thị Lang (2) were also in my circle; when they, within the province and village, were sentenced to heavy punishment or exile, I was able to protect them and see them settled. And recently, when Phan Trọng Mưu surrendered, I took him before the high authorities, and they received him with all due honor like a welcome guest, immediately ordering his return to his home province, the restoration of his ancestral graves, and the pardon of all his family. Thus we see how magnanimous the protectorate government is! From this you may know that, although we are a thousand miles apart, human hearts are the same everywhere.

"Now, if you do not consider my words too lengthy, please do not hesitate. I will never allow an old friend to bear the reputation of one who abandoned his duty.

(Signed) HOÀNG CAO KHẢI"

(2) Trần Phiên Sứ was Trần Khánh Tiến, a Tuần phủ. Phan Thị Lang was Phan Huy Nhuận, a Thị lang.

Phan Đình Phùng's Reply:

"To the noble Hoàng,

Of late, I have been engaged in military affairs, living constantly in mountain ravines, and now the winter cold has set in — my circumstances are truly dreary! Suddenly I received word that a letter from an old friend had arrived. Hearing this, all the cold and gloom vanished without a trace. I opened the letter at once and read it. In it, my old friend advised me of every fortune and misfortune, set forth every advantage and harm. I understand your heart: you seek not only my safety but the peace of the entire region. Your heartfelt words I comprehend fully. Though a thousand miles separate us, it is as if we sat together in the same house, speaking face to face.

"Yet my thoughts and circumstances contain much that is exceedingly difficult to express. Looking at the world as it is today, and my own strength as it is — as you have said, 'What is a mantis that it dares to stop a chariot?' Yet what I do now is even more than that — not merely a mantis stretching out its arm to bite a wheel.

"But on reflection: our country for several thousand years has preserved its civilization through successive generations. The land is not vast, the troops are not strong, the wealth is not abundant; the only thing to rely upon for building the nation is the fundamental bond of sovereign and subject, father and son, following the five constant virtues. In ancient times, the Hán, Đường, Tống, Nguyên, and Ming dynasties of China — how many times did they covet our land, wishing to turn it into their own prefectures and districts? And still they could not seize it. China and our country share a border; their strength is ten thousand times greater than ours; yet from ancient times they could not use their might to swallow our country. Why? Simply because the mountains and rivers of the South have been assigned their own separate destiny by Heaven, and the civilizing virtue of ritual and righteousness has its own foundation upon which we can rely. Now the French, facing us from a distance of tens of thousands of miles, cross the seas and arrive like the wind wherever they go — to the point where the king has fled and the entire nation is in turmoil. In an instant, our mountains and rivers have become someone else's territory. The whole country and its people have been submerged — it is not a single person or a single family that suffers this misery alone.

"In the year Ất Dậu (1885), the royal carriage arrived at Sơn Phòng in Hà Tĩnh. At that time, I was still in mourning, wishing only to close my door and observe proper filial rites. How could I have dreamed of anything else? But because I am the son of a hereditary official, and His Majesty repeatedly issued edicts summoning me forth, I could not refuse. Thus I had to step forward in response to the Edict — it could not be helped. Recently, His Majesty again considered me worthy to entrust with great responsibility and gave me full authority. Such is the sovereign's trust. If you were in my position, could you bring yourself to refuse and flee?

"From the time I raised the banner of revolt until now, ten years have passed. Those who pledged their lives to the cause have been punished, some beheaded or killed. Yet their hearts have never wavered; on the contrary, they have contributed their talents and strength to help me, and the number of bold men rallying to me only grows. Do you think men take up danger and hardship for pleasure, leave their families to follow me thus? No — they trust my heart and my judgment — that is all. Such is their loyalty. If you were in my position, could you bring yourself to abandon them?

"Because the people treat me thus, I have let my own home fall into desolation — incense and smoke gone cold, relatives scattered — and I dare not look back. Consider: those closest to oneself — if one cannot even care for them, what of strangers? Those near — if one cannot protect them, what of those far away? And that our country has fallen into such misery is not merely the calamity of war. You must know that wherever the French go, their minions swarm forth, seeking to establish their own merit, even at the expense of their fathers; they incriminate the innocent. One day, this punishment; the next day, that penalty — whatever method can extort the people's wealth, they employ to the fullest. Hence a hundred evils and a thousand corrupt practices pour forth — how can the French know the full extent of the sufferings of the people in our rustic villages and hamlets? How can the people not be scattered and displaced?

"You and I were both born in the province of Hoan, though we are now separated by thousands of miles. You still care for our homeland — how much more must I, who have personally endured hardship and witnessed it with my own eyes? The misfortune is that circumstances constrain me, and my strength cannot match my desire — thus I must accept it, not knowing what else to do. Since you care for the people, take my heart and my situation and place yourself in them to reflect. You will see clearly — no lengthy explanation from me is needed.

(Signed) PHAN ĐÌNH PHÙNG"

As for Nguyễn Thân, we have his letter below, recounting his services to the Protectorate. In truth, in dealing with the Hương Khê insurgents, he never directly engaged Phan in battle. He only deployed troops to block the insurgents' communication and supply routes. He brought 3,000 troops to Nghệ-Tĩnh in the fifth month of Ất Mùi (1895) and withdrew in the eleventh month, after Phan fell ill and died.

This letter today stands as a verdict of treason that the descendants of the Khâm sai Nguyễn cannot deny, and an eternal shame for them. The recipient was Governor-General Paul Doumer, and the letter was sent in 1912 — Thành Thái 18 — when Doumer had completed his term as Governor-General of Indochina and was en route back to France.

"At the time of the upheaval in the capital (1885), when Tôn Thất Thuyết took Emperor Hàm Nghi and fled, inciting the people to rise up against France, all of Trung Kỳ was in upheaval. The Văn Thân (scholar-gentry) summoned the people, simultaneously rising in resistance — on the one hand massacring Catholics, on the other opposing the French.

"In the midst of this, my father passed away. I succeeded him and was then serving as Tiễu phủ sử (Pacification Commissioner) for the mountain regions of the three provinces of Quảng Ngãi, Quảng Nam, and Bình Định. Although there was an edict from the capital summoning me back, I only concerned myself with keeping the regions under my pacification authority calm, awaiting the right moment.

"The two Văn Thân rebel leaders Cử Định and Tú Tảm at that time occupied the provincial capital of Quảng Ngãi, with a large following. I led two thousand troops from the highlands down to attack the Văn Thân rebels. I recovered the provincial capital, captured fourteen rebel commanders, and had them beheaded on the spot to intimidate the remaining Văn Thân. After Quảng Ngãi was pacified, I obeyed the orders of His Majesty Đồng Khánh and led the entire army into Bình Định to suppress the rebels there. I eliminated the rebel elements, restored order, and reorganized the administration of the prefectures and districts. The last remaining rebel commander, Mai Xuân Thưởng, fled to An Khê, and I sent troops in hot pursuit. (1) At the same time, Mr. Phó Phá Lộc brought his forces from Nam Kỳ to suppress the Văn Thân rebels led by Mai Xuân Thưởng. At that time, I was ordered by His Majesty to lead troops to Quảng Nam to attack the Văn Thân rebels, whose notorious leader was Hường Hiệu, who had long resisted the Protectorate government — to the point where the French commanders had to establish up to 36 military posts in the region for patrolling, yet still could not pacify it. I led my troops forward, sending trusted men to reconnoiter, and discovered the hiding place of this rebel leader in the An Tâm area. Twenty-five of his deputy commanders were captured by me; the remainder surrendered to me. Hường Hiệu escaped, fleeing to Ngô Hành mountain, but I eventually captured him alive, placed him in a cage, and sent him to Huế. (2)

(1) In truth, Phó bảng Nguyễn Duy Hiệp, after capturing Lê Thành Phương to avoid disaster for the people, saved the people but was considered a coward... [illegible]
(2) In truth, if it had not been for the assistance of the collaborator Phan Bá Phiến's betrayal, Nguyễn Thân could not have arrested Nguyễn Hiệu so easily.

"For my loyal services to the French cause, the Republican Government at that time awarded me the fifth-class Bắc Đầu Bội Tinh (Legion of Honor).

"Some time later, Bình Định Province again had disturbances. The government sent me to pacify it a second time; I suppressed the rebels, and the region has been quiet ever since. For my success in fighting, Governor-General Picquet and Imperial Commissioner Hector requested the Republican Government to award me the fourth-class Bắc Đầu Bội Tinh.

"When I returned to my former post in the highlands of Quảng Ngãi, I continued as Tiễu phủ sử of the mountain regions as before.

"A few months later, I was summoned to Huế to receive the position of Binh bộ Thượng thư (Minister of War). Governor-General De Lanessan, after negotiating with the court, appointed me Tổng thống (Governor) of Bình Định Province — a vast territory — and I resolved many important problems that had been left pending for years. Thereafter, I was appointed Khâm sai đại thần (Imperial Commissioner) and sent troops to suppress the Văn Thân of Nghệ-Tĩnh. Because Governor-General Rousseau and Imperial Commissioner Brière interceded with the court, I was entrusted with this important responsibility.

"The reason I was ordered to lead troops to Nghệ-Tĩnh was to cut off the head of the Văn Thân rebel Phan Đình Phùng, who had been causing trouble in Nghệ An and Hà Tĩnh for ten years, despite the government's ceaseless efforts to suppress him.

"Though I had only 1,400 men, I pursued the rebels from one mountain to the next, and I was able to surround them thanks to the blocking posts established at intervals along all communication routes.

"Our strength exhausted, their power spent, and wounded in a great battle, Phan Đình Phùng was forced to take poison and kill himself. All of his subordinate officers were captured; several thousand rebels surrendered. Thus the rebel provinces of northern Trung Kỳ were completely pacified.

"For my meritorious services recorded above, the Republican Government awarded me the third-class Bắc Đầu Bội Tinh, and His Majesty the Emperor recalled me to the Capital to serve as Phụ chính đại thần (Grand Regent), etc."

BIOGRAPHY OF NGUYỄN THÂN

As we know, the colonialists always relied on the native feudal class as their allies, hoping thus to establish their domination easily. In Vietnam, when our nation was in decline, a number of officials of all ranks emerged who, seeing the country weak and inferior to the West, allied themselves with the enemy, worshipped them as masters to preserve their positions and privileges — knowing no shame. Then, in this chaotic situation, a new breed of brazen-faced men also sprouted forth, rushing to compete for position with those already entrenched in the machinery of that dark regime. Naturally, this lot had to serve our enemies more actively to gain more favor — such men as Trần Bá Lộc, Đỗ Hữu Phương, Lê Phát Đạt in Nam Kỳ, and Hoàng Cao Khải and Nguyễn Thân in central Vietnam. These were new faces that the times had produced — never before seen on the Vietnamese political stage.

Đào Trinh Nhất, when writing about Nguyễn Thân (p. 185) — his emergence in the role of Khâm sai Tiết chế quân vụ in the fifth month of Ất Mùi (1895), bringing 3,000 troops to Nghệ-Tĩnh to suppress the Hương Khê forces — wrote the following:

"Nguyễn Thân, like Hoàng Cao Khải, was one of the two great mandarins raised up by the times."

(NOTE: Hoàng Cao Khải's letter to Phan was delivered at the end of the tenth month of Nhâm Ngọ (1895) by Phan Văn Mẫn, a paternal cousin of Phan, who carried it to Vũ Quang mountain. At this time Hoàng Cao Khải held the post of Bắc Kỳ Kinh lược sứ. He began as a Cử nhân but dissipated his youth and drifted north. The French employed him in their administrative machinery to replace the old-style mandarins who refused to cooperate with the enemy. The three letters above are all excerpted from Đào Trinh Nhất's "Phan Đình Phùng" (pp. 168-172).)

"He was originally an Ấm sinh from Quảng Nam Province, son of Nguyễn Tấn, who formerly had merit in suppressing the Mọi rebels in that province. The Mọi had revered Nguyễn Tấn as a divine general because of his clever stratagems. He once scattered rock-sugar lumps mixed with pebbles under the eaves of his thatched house, then had several Mọi brought in under pretext of negotiation; meanwhile, he ordered his soldiers to fetch some pebbles for him to eat. The soldiers chose sugar lumps and presented them; the Mọi, thinking the great mandarin truly ate pebbles, believed him a celestial being, and went back to persuade their people to submit. During his lifetime, the Mọi never dared rebel against the court. But after his death, they rose up again. The court repeatedly sent troops to suppress them without success. Eventually they had to send Nguyễn Thân. The Mọi, seeing he was the son of their former divine general, all fled in panic. Nguyễn Thân gained fame from that day.

"He was born in a time of great national calamity. At first, he regarded the times with a pessimistic eye and was filled with indignation. But because his desire for fame and achievement outweighed his patriotism, he soon changed his views, adapting the times to his own advantage. It is not known in which year — nor what office he held — only that during the period when our country was at war with France and the court had been forced to sign a peace treaty, a group of soldiers in Bình Định rose up in opposition. They did not care that the court had made peace with the French — they refused to accept it, acting as if Bình Định Province had declared independence. Nguyễn Thân had originally intended to do the same — to seize Bình Định Province and oppose the French — but he was a step too late; others had done it first. He thought that if he now did the same, he would only be following in their footsteps. He did not like that. He wanted to do something no one else had yet done. He therefore turned to the French side, offering to lead troops to suppress the others. He succeeded, and the French held him in high regard. Thus, before long, he became Tổng đốc (Governor) of Bình Định, precisely at the time he was ordered to lead the campaign against Phan. Later he rose to Phụ chính (Regent), was highly honored at court, and was granted the title of Quận công (Duke) before his retirement.

"He was a cruel and vicious man, fond of killing. During his military campaigns, he killed countless people. After retiring to his native village of Thu Xà, he went mad and died. People said he was haunted by the ghosts of those he had wrongfully killed; when he was ill, every time he blinked, he would see hordes of vengeful spirits with disheveled hair and blood-soaked bodies appearing to demand his life. Whenever he saw them, he would scream in terror and remorse. His magnificent mansion, built on a small hill in Thu Xà, faced the market behind and the river in front, overlooking the sea — all proclaiming the grandeur of a martial man. They say the house was haunted; after his death, it was abandoned and no one would live there. Beside it stood a pagoda he had built, as if to secure a peaceful afterlife for those he had wrongfully killed. The author of this book once visited Thu Xà to see his former mansion, but felt nothing whatsoever.

SEVERAL MARTYR FIGURES OF CENTRAL VIETNAM

NGUYỄN HIỆU
(1885 — 1886)

A martyr of the Cần Vương movement who once fiercely stirred the hatred of the invaders among our people in Quảng Nam, Quảng Ngãi, and Bình Định provinces was Nguyễn Hiệu. He was born in 1847 in Thanh Hà commune, Duyên Phúc District (Quảng Nam Province), commonly called Hường Hiệu. He earned the Phó bảng (Junior Doctorate) degree and was appointed Phụ đạo (Assistant Preceptor) under Emperors Kiến Phúc and Hàm Nghi, due to his virtue and profound learning. In this capacity, he held the honorary rank of Hồng lô tự Khanh.

After the Ất Dậu (1885) incident, when Emperor Hàm Nghi and Tôn Thất Thuyết fled to Tân Sở (Quảng Trị) to plan a protracted anti-French resistance and mobilize the entire nation for war, he encouraged the scholar-gentry and notables of the three southern provinces of Thừa Thiên to organize righteous militia bands. This movement began in 1885. His comrade-in-arms was Phan Bá Phiến, a fellow villager who devoted himself wholeheartedly to assisting in all matters.

He established military and administrative organs, constructed forts and ramparts, forged weapons, and trained troops at Trung Lộc village in Quế Sơn District. The people regarded this place as a new province, completely independent from the puppet government across the Hương River and Ngự Mountain of Emperor Đồng Khánh. Among his comrades was Nguyễn Thân of Quảng Ngãi, who joined the righteous society at the very beginning but later turned traitor, revealing everything he knew about the organization and strength of the insurgent forces to the enemy for his own advancement. Nguyễn Thân had previously been appointed Sơn phòng sứ but, seeing French power, went over to them to destroy the Cần Vương movement. However, the colonial army was still occupied with suppressing other regions in more serious situations and had not yet launched a campaign against Nguyễn Hiệu's militia. Hiệu remained active for three years until Nguyễn Thân devised numerous effective plans for the French, and Hiệu's militia was broken.

Before reaching defeat — his forces few, his position weak — he conferred with Phan Bá Phiến:

"Our militia bands in the three provinces of Nam-Ngãi and Bình Định are under our leadership. Now that the cause has failed, death is our only option. But if we both die at the same time, it will be difficult to spare our compatriots from harm, for the enemy will arrest and interrogate them, terrorizing them brutally. Better that one of us should remain to bear all responsibility, to save the people, and then die when all is done. You should die first, to avoid capture; I will disband the members and then surrender myself to the enemy. If in future someone among our comrades can continue our cause and accomplish great things, then we shall live on..."

Phan Bá Phiến accepted with righteous dignity. He dressed formally, made five prostrations to the spirits, then bid farewell to Nguyễn Hiệu with two bows, saying:

"Old friend, please stay and strive to save the people. I go ahead!"

Then he took a packet of poison he had prepared in his sleeve, drank it in one draught, and expired. After Phiến's death, the militia was disbanded. When all was done, Hiệu surrendered himself to the French. The enemy placed him in a cage and sent him to Huế, interrogating and torturing him severely to extract the whereabouts of the other members and the militia's organization. He steadfastly maintained that he alone had instigated and led the rebellion in the three provinces of Quảng Nam, Quảng Ngãi, and Bình Định; the people had been coerced by the insurgents into following out of fear for their homes and families, and deserved no punishment — it was enough to execute him alone.

From prison, he sent a few words to his son:

"If by good fortune the court sentences our entire family to death, we shall all meet again in the underworld. But if the court is lenient and punishes only me, then you must take care of your mother and teach your siblings as when I was alive. Do not pursue the path of examination and officialdom — it only harms oneself, one's family, and the world."

The enemy sentenced him to death, and he uttered no word of complaint. On the full-moon day of the eighth month of Bính Tuất (1886), before his execution, he left behind a few deeply moving poems:

"Cần vương thế dữ Bắc Nam đồng,
Vô nại khuông tương lệ vị thông.
Vạn cổ cương thường vô Ngụy Tháo,
Bách niên tâm sự hữu Quan Công.
Thiên thu phận dĩ sơn hà định,
Địa thế sâu khan thảo mộc cùng.
Ký ngữ phù trầm tư thế giả,
Hữu tương thành bại luận anh hùng."

[Translation:]
The Cần Vương cause unites North and South,
But the way to help — alas, still blocked.
Through ten thousand ages, none like Ngụy Tháo betrayed the bonds of duty;
A hundred years of heart's desire — there is Quan Công.
Heaven's thousand autumns have fixed the mountains and rivers,
Earth's contours — sadly — grass and trees reach their end.
A message to those who rise and sink, pondering this age:
Do not judge heroes by success or failure alone.

"Tây Nam vô địch xích đồng tri,
Tảo cập kim thời thế khả vi.
Nhược sử gian phong vô ảo viện,
Hà nan chung đỉnh thát cường di.
Hàn sơn kỷ đắc cô tùng bạn,
Đại hạ yên năng nhất mộc chi.
Hảo bả đan tâm triều liệt thánh,
Trung thu minh nguyệt bạn ngô quy."

[Translation:]
Even children know the West and South are invincible,
But if we seize the moment, much can be done.
If the traitors had no secret allies,
How hard would it be to cast out the strong invaders?
The cold mountain has but a lone pine for company,
A great house cannot be propped by a single beam.
Let me offer my red heart to the host of saints,
The mid-autumn bright moon will accompany me home.

(Translation of poems by Sử Binh Tử — the pen-name of Huỳnh Thúc Kháng.)

Through these verses, we see the heart of Phó bảng Nguyễn Hiệu — the same heart shared by many scholar-gentry of that time: when faced with a duty, they did it, knowing full well that their efforts might be like an ant trying to move a mountain — but content to show their unyielding loyalty to the nation.

On the heroic death of Nguyễn Hiệu, Phan Sào Nam expressed his profound admiration in "Việt Nam Vong Quốc Sử" in these words:

"Alas! Those two men (Hiệu and Học) — heedless of the ruin of their homes, undaunted by death — thought only of preserving righteous ideals for the future. Before their eyes, in their hearts, there was only the Fatherland, only their compatriots. Such courage — even Heaven and Earth must admire it, even the spirits must honor it."

LÊ THÀNH PHƯƠNG — A CẦN VƯƠNG FIGURE IN PHÚ YÊN

Besides Mai Xuân Thưởng, who raised his name in the anti-French struggle in southern Trung Kỳ (Bình Định and southward), we must also mention Lê Thành Phương, known as Tú Phương. It was Lê Thành Phương who actually commanded the movement on the ground — a fact not yet widely known and recorded in history. (2)

(2) According to Ngô Đình Tư's materials entitled "Lê Thành Phương với phong trào Cần Vương tại Phú Yên," published in Bách Khoa magazine, issue 43, year 7, 4 January 1968, Saigon.

Tú Phương was born in the year Ất Dậu (1825) in Mỹ Phú commune, An Vinh canton (now An Hiệp village), Tuy An District, Phú Yên Province, into a middle-peasant family. He earned the Tú tài (Bachelor's) degree at age thirty (1855) under the reign of Tự Đức, after studying under Điền học Đinh Xuân Trạc. After obtaining his degree, he returned home to live a reclusive life, but his heart was inevitably stirred, like many intellectuals of the time, by what they heard and saw of the bronze cannon of French Captain Rigault de Genouilly's fleet entering the Hàn estuary in the autumn of Đinh Mùi (1847). The feeble resistance of Vietnamese troops, the collapse of the Đà Nẵng coastal fortifications — all foretold the very dark days ahead for the nation. From that day, his heart overflowed with indignation. Then the enemy poured into Gia Định, and the three eastern and three western provinces fell one after another. From the 1862 treaty to the 1884 treaty, the country changed masters; Emperor Hàm Nghi fled; the puppet court of Emperor Đồng Khánh was installed. The reality of national loss was undeniable. The intellectuals and leaders of Vietnam could now only live or die with the enemy.

The Cần Vương Edict resounded throughout the land; scholar-gentry from all four directions responded, except for a number of traitors and reactionaries. In Quảng Nam, Phó bảng Nguyễn Hiệu established a righteous society, eagerly joined by local scholars. In Bình Định, Mai Xuân Thưởng and Bùi Điền raised the call. In Phú Yên, Bùi Giảng, Lê Thành Phương, Nguyễn Hảo Sự, and others also raised the banner and beat the drums.

Bùi Giảng styled himself Tổng Trấn (Governor-General), raising troops and occupying the provincial capital at Xuân Thọ commune, Tân Thịnh hamlet, Sông Cầu District. Tú Phương styled himself Tổng thống quân vụ đại thần (Supreme Commander of Military Affairs), establishing his headquarters at Đèo Quản Cau in An Hiệp commune, Tuy An District (present-day), appointing his son Lê Thành Bính as Hữu tham quân (Right Counselor). After conducting a flag ceremony at Phù Điềm mountain (on whose flat summit thousands could gather), he called upon all his compatriots in the province to join him in resisting the invaders. His appeal was answered by many wealthy families in the region, who raised and supported their own insurgent troops stationed at various locations. The insurgents' weapons at this time were insignificant — nothing to compare with their patriotic spirit.

At this time, the French were still occupied with northern Trung Kỳ and Bắc Kỳ, and had not yet extended pacification to southern Trung Kỳ. But they had sent their navy along the coast to reconnoiter. Wherever they saw insurgent activity, they opened fire with cannon; our side also had sacred cannon emplaced on shore to bombard enemy ships. We inflicted some damage upon them.

Later, the French landed troops. The enemy had many advantages in weapons and tactics over these rustic scholars and peasant compatriots, whose only notable asset was their love of country and concern for their race. Bùi Giảng had a fairly strong force, thanks to his earlier capture of the provincial capital and the seizure of a number of enemy weapons, and thus resisted for a time. The final battle of Bùi Giảng took place at Hảo Danh field, after which he retreated to La Hai. The forces of Tú Phương fought their last battle at Cầu Đá in Quản Cau.

The simple weapons of the Cần Vương forces nevertheless caused the enemy some loss of men and materials. But in the end, swords and spears could not long sustain a struggle against cannon, warships, and the sophisticated military science of the invaders. After several major defeats, Tú Phương led his remnant forces to Củng Sơn to organize guerrilla warfare. From this phase onward, he had to face the notorious Nam Kỳ collaborator Trần Bá Lộc, who was sent by the French as Bình-Phú-Khánh-Thuận Tổng đốc (Governor-General of four provinces). Lộc had been in French service for over twenty years, was skilled in treachery and murder, and thoroughly understood mass psychology. He commanded over a thousand auxiliary troops (with French reinforcements when needed). He established posts everywhere for both suppression and patrolling of areas where revolutionary activity occurred. Simultaneously, he issued proclamations everywhere, offering amnesty and promising positions and privileges to the Văn Thân who would surrender to "the Great French State." His headquarters was set up at Ngân Sơn, a strategic point to block the insurgents' supply routes. The position of Ngân Sơn at that time was not unlike Vũ Quang, the strategic base of Phan Đình Phùng in Hà Tĩnh that the collaborator Nguyễn Thân had besieged and destroyed.

Seeing that the struggle was nearly hopeless — the enemy too strong, Trần Bá Lộc's methods of cruelty unmatched in history (disemboweling and quartering regardless of age, sex, or status, grinding bodies in rice mortars like preparing nem...) — Bùi Giảng surrendered himself and his subordinates, offering Lộc all the money and goods he had captured when taking Phú Yên. He even asked to become Lộc's adoptive son for promotion by the French colonialists, and was appointed Ký lục at Sông Cầu; after six months, he became Tri huyện, then gradually rose to Tổng đốc of Bình Định a few years later. From that point on, he took up the profession of harming his own countrymen, changing his name to Bùi Tử Quảng, becoming an effective lackey of the Imperialists. Who can forget that in the failed tax resistance of Mậu Thân (1908) in Bình Định, Bùi Tử Quảng, then serving as Tri phủ at Tuy Phước, brutally tortured the compatriots involved in that movement?

But Tú Lê Thành Phương never wavered. Trần Bá Lộc's seductive words had no effect, and Lộc turned to a policy of terror. Phương's wife and children were imprisoned, yet he remained resolute, refusing to surrender. Finally, Lộc placed a bounty on his head. The Chánh tổng of Hòa Bình, Đặng Trạch, deceived him, captured him, and delivered him to Lộc. Lộc did not kill him immediately but used sweet words to persuade him to surrender, treating him well to use him as bait to lure other Văn Thân still active in the region. Phương spoke a final defiant line to silence the brainless, ear-deafening rhetoric of the great traitor Trần: "Ninh thọ tử, bất ninh thọ nhục" — "Better to suffer death than to suffer humiliation." After a week of coaxing and cajoling to no avail, Trần Bá Lộc had him executed. He stretched out his neck to meet the enemy's sword at the Cây Dừa ferry landing, in Phường Lụa hamlet, Ngân Sơn commune, Tuy An Prefecture, on 28 January of the Đinh Hợi year (1887), keeping his oath to the people and the country. He was sixty-two. Before dying, he said to the enemy: "You kill me, but if you do not also kill my fourth son, he will cut off your heads." After killing him, they displayed his head at the banyan tree of Chợ Chiều, Xóm Gỗ, in his home village. A month later, his relatives managed to retrieve the head for a joint burial on a hill in Quản Cau pass, about one kilometer from National Route 1.

His son, Lê Thành Bính, after this affair, planned to flee with a few close subordinates to Lower Laos to organize a restoration. Unfortunately, while passing the Vân Hòa post in Sơn Hòa District, they were intercepted by the French and severely wounded. Thanks to the rescue efforts of his soldiers, he was carried home but died five days later. Lê Thành Bính died on 3 February of the Đinh Hợi year (1887), at age thirty-two, and was buried beside his father's grave.

With Lê Thành Phương's death, the anti-French movement in Phú Yên died with him. A few officials of the Thứ xá rank died for the cause — such as Nguyễn Hảo Sự, Võ Thiệp — while many others surrendered, were captured, or imprisoned.

(1) In 1966, on the occasion of National Day and the promulgation of the Constitution of the Republic of Vietnam, the authorities of Phú Yên and the people of the province allocated funds to repair the graves and commemorate the achievements of father and son Tú Phương with great solemnity.

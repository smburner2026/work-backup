MODERN REVOLUTIONARY HISTORY OF VIETNAM

CHAPTER II
FROM KING KIẾN PHÚC TO KING HÀM NGHI

The word was "Vietnam." The congratulatory address was contained in a small chest. It was Guerrier himself who bore it before the Dragon Throne, opening it to read aloud. The court of Rites had rehearsed with Guerrier the precise syllables of the text, but he did not follow them with perfect fidelity. Having heard the address through, King Hàm Nghi dispatched a senior mandarin to question Guerrier about news of the President of France, of various generals, and of the civil officials who had passed through Vietnam on previous occasions. When the French delegation withdrew, they found the Ngọ Môn gate shut fast and were obliged to exit through a side gate. The colonial party was beside itself with fury; yet all Vietnam applauded this gesture of the sovereign as much as that of Tôn Thất Thuyết. In his volume Le roi proscrit, pages 40-41, Marcel Gauthier writes:

"King Hàm Nghi preserved the sacred character of his authority in the eyes of his subjects. Unwittingly, the young sovereign had done something whose resonance struck to the depths of the entire country: with a will resolved upon independence, even though France maintained her officials in Huế, the court of Vietnam still proclaimed an attitude that was anything but servile. That attitude originated with the Regency Council (Hội-đồng Phụ-chánh), and the Council had every reason to believe that the common people looked to the bearing of the king as a model to follow, reading in that bearing an unspoken command to resist the new arrivals..."

Naturally, during this phase the French understood that their position at the imperial capital of the Nguyễn as everywhere else was profoundly precarious. Khâm-sứ Rheinart, chastened, joined with Lieutenant-Colonel Pernot to establish in haste a series of defensive installations at Mang Cá and around the French Residency. The gentlemen Tường and Thuyết took this very ill, for the presence of French troops within the Citadel constituted a formidable obstacle to their various anti-French designs. They argued that the treaty, though signed, had not yet been ratified or exchanged by France, and that Clause 5—which granted the French Resident-General the right to station troops at Mang Cá for self-defence—could therefore not yet be invoked. (The treaty was subsequently ratified by both chambers of the French parliament in early June 1885.) They further contended that the establishment of a military base at Mang Cá would diminish the prestige of the sovereign and agitate the popular mind. Despite these objections, Guerrier and Rheinart proceeded on the very same day (17 August 1884) to Mang Cá, where they raised the Tricolour at Trấn Binh Đài—French soldiers had in fact been present there for two days already. At the very moment when the atmosphere between Vietnam and France was suffocating, and when Rheinart had become more useful to France than ever before, he was recalled to Paris by telegram from Prime Minister Jules Ferry, dispatched on 15 May 1884—several weeks before the Patenôtre Convention. The reason for this lay in the fact that the Resident-General, or Khâm-sứ, in Huế—that is to say, the Minister of Foreign Affairs of the Southern Kingdom—held authority over even the Commander-in-Chief of French forces; and since Rheinart was merely a lieutenant-colonel, it was convenient to replace him with a civilian. Rheinart departed. His successor, Lemaire, was appointed by telegram of 30 August 1884. He arrived in Vietnam on 10 October 1884 and remained until 5 June 1885. As we have noted, Lemaire had previously served as French Consul in Shanghai.

The first act of King Hàm Nghi upon mounting the throne was to punish Hường Đỉnh, the twenty-third son of King Thiệu Trị—known as the Prince Kỳ-Phong—on the pretext that this prince was an opium addict, a man of dissolute habits, and had dared to insult the newly enthroned sovereign by calling him a bastard. Kỳ-Phong suffered the same penalty as Dục Đức and perished of starvation in prison on 19 September 1884. On this occasion, one morning there appeared pasted upon the Ministry of Personnel a couplet:

    One river, two nations—hard to divide—THUYẾT
    Four months, three kings—omen ill—TƯỜNG

Another affair of note occurred at this time (mid-April 1885): the case of the Prince of Gia Hưng, formerly a Regent under King Kiến Phúc and head of the Clan Court (Tôn-Nhân-Phủ), who harboured designs against the autocratic policy of Nguyễn Văn Tường. The Prince intended to bring him down by opening an inquiry into the mysterious death of King Kiến Phúc, but before the plan could be executed it was betrayed. Nguyễn Văn Tường struck first. He had the Prince of Gia Hưng arrested at the beginning of May 1885, on the charge of incest and of treasonable communication with the French (the crime of illicit liaison with the enemy), the evidence being his intimate contact with Rheinart—whom the Khâm-sứ had reportedly proposed to enthrone in place of Hàm Nghi. Lemaire intervened at once, and the Prince was released from custody. But Nguyễn Văn Tường found another pretext; the Prince was subsequently stripped of all his ranks and titles and exiled to Cam Lộ (Quảng Trị), while his children were reduced to the surname Tôn Thất and scattered to various places. On 9 May 1885, as the Prince and his household were leaving his private residence, they were all put to the sword. Various pro-French elements suspected of reactionary sympathies, as well as those who had opposed the xenophobic policy, were likewise eliminated.

Considering these events, the French bore a burning hatred toward Nguyễn Văn Tường and Tôn Thất Thuyết—men whom they could neither bribe nor intimidate. These two were the most resolute leaders of the anti-French resistance, and they held every advantage in the court. They had bluntly destroyed all French schemes for establishing a protectorate over Vietnam in recent months. The populace, for its part, harboured resentment toward Tường as well, owing to a monetary affair in which Tường was accused of having diverted more funds into his own pocket than he had devoted to the anti-French cause. At the end of 1884, Tường had ordered the recall of all copper coins in circulation throughout the markets and streets, replacing them with a new currency so thin and flimsy it resembled paper—indeed, copper coins that floated on water. The missionary Max de Pirey conducted a public demonstration of floating coins before large crowds, the better to propagandize against Tường. Be that as it may, the issuance of this new currency proved a grave loss of popular confidence and a blow to his prestige. Its unhappy consequence was that many wealthy families hoarded the old coinage; the markets grew deserted, for no one wished to spend the new money. Seeing public opinion in ferment and the situation untenable, Tường ordered the beheading of one of the Chinese contractors responsible for minting coins for the court, in a transparent attempt to shift the blame for the fraud upon the shoulders of the avaricious Hoa Kiều. Whether the collection of copper coins was intended for the casting of bullets, no source tells us.

What of Khâm-sứ Lemaire's relations with the Southern court?

In his daily dealings with the court, Lemaire concerned himself principally with the political dimension. He naturally disapproved of the harsh and terroristic methods of the men who bore swords and commanded troops. He endeavoured to put an end to the friction existing between the court and the Protectorate, and on one occasion proudly declared to an editor of the journal Gil Blas: "I can obtain from the Southern court whatever I wish, and never need resort to threats of force; a little mutual accommodation is all that is required." What Lemaire did not want was the interference of the military men: on the Vietnamese side, Hoàng Tá Viêm and Lưu Vĩnh Phúc; on the French side, Brière de l'Isle, the French Commander-in-Chief in Tonkin, and Pernot, the lieutenant-colonel commanding the Mang Cá garrison. These he accused of perpetually running counter to the civilian government and of distorting the meaning of the Protectorate. Having arrived in Huế only days before, he set about resolving several matters left unsettled since General Millot's departure for France—among them the assassination of seven French missionaries and of indigenous Christians in certain districts along the Ai-Lao frontier and in Thanh Hóa, responsibility for which had to be laid at the door of local officials and ultimately at that of the two Regents. A mixed commission was established to hear the case, but the court lobbied for its dissolution. (From this episode alone one may observe that France had begun to act the overlord with the court; henceforth she could punish whomever she pleased.) In February and March 1885, the Governor of Cochinchina, Thompson, produced evidence that Tường and Thuyết had sent agents into Cambodia to foment disorder. Relations between the Vietnamese and French sides grew acrimonious once more. Thuyết issued secret orders to position artillery around the Palace, within the Citadel and without, the barrels trained even upon the French Residency. Pernot reported this to Lemaire, but seeing no reaction of the kind he desired, he sent a dispatch north to Brière de l'Isle. The Commander-in-Chief at once ordered Pernot to send patrols into the Citadel, to conduct mock military exercises, and to stage nocturnal surprise mobilizations at suspect locations. The court protested to the Residency; Lemaire intervened at once with the Commander-in-Chief, demanding that the matter be settled by peaceful means. But on this same occasion Lemaire reproached Brière de l'Isle for having, on his own authority, sent back to France certain Residents without consulting him. As for the artillery positioned around the Citadel, Lemaire secured from Tường and Thuyết the concession of removing twenty pieces per day. But the two anti-French leaders were secretly transferring arms, ammunition, and troops to Cam Lộ (Tân Sở) and constructing numerous other fortified camps throughout Quảng Trị—laying the groundwork for a future war zone.

Several further incidents of friction arose between Vietnam and France. The court sent instructions to the northern provinces to collect money and rice in exchange for weapons as a war reserve. After Lemaire opened an investigation into the Cambodian disturbances, he protested to the court, which was obliged to discontinue the practice—at least on paper. At the end of March 1885, a French gunboat intercepted a shipment of 6,000 large-calibre fathoms of chain cable from Hong Kong destined for Huế aboard the German-flagged vessel Triumph. Lemaire signed the import permit, judging that the royal navy might have need of the material. According to the French, the chains were intended to obstruct the passage of French vessels. A certain prince, while out riding, was slapped by a French officer for having mocked him. Lemaire demanded that the French commander in Huế punish the officer forthwith. On another occasion, several sailors from the vessel Lionne, stationed at the Thuận An estuary, were pickpocketed by villagers from nearby Lại Ân. The ship's commander, Captain Hennique of the navy, promptly led a detachment into the village, seized the headman, and had him flogged with a ray-tail whip. Tường reported the matter to Lemaire, who sent a letter of censure to Hennique. Hennique refused to accept it, appealed to Hà Nội, and received the support of General Brière de l'Isle. Thereafter the Minister of the Navy intervened on Hennique's behalf, and this captain was immediately promoted to the rank of capitaine de frégate—lieutenant-commander.

Perceiving the dissensions among the French, the court exploited this contradiction to the fullest and submitted a memorandum of five points:

1. The treaty had not yet been ratified, yet 200 or 300 French soldiers were already stationed at Mang Cá; the court requested a revision of Clause 5 so that the Resident-General might reside outside the Citadel, the better to avoid offence to the dignity of the imperial house and agitation of the public mind.

2. Various French Residents in Tonkin had passed death sentences on Vietnamese mandarins, or had flogged and fined them.

3. French vessels exercised such strict surveillance over the ships and boats of the Southern king that commerce in Thừa Thiên suffered grievously, goods being detained for inspection interminably.

4. The Southern court was compelled to permit the French authorities to destroy the royal seal of investiture, which ought rather to have been returned to China by the court itself than left to French officials to smash and pocket the proceeds—a trifle worth a mere 240 Mexican dollars.

5. The Privy Council (Cơ-Mật-Viện) demanded that the French government restore the revenues collected from the opium monopoly, from mining taxes, and from other levies.

Nguyễn Văn Tường was no stranger to these matters, for the investigation was already complete; he continued to order mandarins in the northern provinces not to assist the French forces. France was consumed with indignation. Freycinet, the Minister of Foreign Affairs, telegraphed Lemaire demanding that Tôn Thất Thuyết be punished at all costs (he was now considered the most dangerous of the two)—that he be removed from his post as Minister of War and exiled to the most distant possible location.

Faced with the court's protests, Lemaire had not yet formulated a reply when, on 5 June 1885, he was obliged to vacate his post. In Paris, his conciliatory temperament had been condemned—just as Philastre's had been before him. In short, not to be tough with the Huế court was to be impotent and faithless to the interests of France. Such was the consensus among the colonialist mandarinate of the period.

Paul Bonnetain, a Frenchman of pronounced imperialist convictions during this troubled time, wrote: "At Giai Nhân, I saw many missionaries eager to descend because the Residency in Huế lacked the firmness they feared would plunge them into many embarrassments. In Paris, people must understand: for the Huế court, to be Catholic is to be French. Should we remain weak in Tonkin, only to open the way for further massacres? Thousands of native converts, children of ten among them, had to die because they had accepted baptism. In these regions, baptism is a political act; call it what you will, that is how matters stand.

As things now are, we appear to stand on the side of the mandarins against the people—when in truth we are playing a role exactly contrary to what one may and should expect of the citizens of a Republic. The common people, crushed by taxes, are dying of hunger and crying out to us; yet we remain forever on the side of the executioners and the thieves. Under our very supervision, the Vietnamese government debases its coinage (copper money), forcing the people to spend it, so that merchants are obliged to close their shops and to spend what savings they have hoarded over the years; while the mass of the populace, destitute and without a morsel for their mouths, can only cry to heaven—for they no longer possess the strength to rebel.

One feels the most profound pity for the lot of the Vietnamese people, for they dwell forever between the anvil and the hammer: the anvil is ourselves, the hammer is the Southern court—and it is wished that this wretched predicament should encompass the whole protectorate.

The Southern court appears formidable; it beheads men as one trims bamboo-shoots; but its authority exists solely through terror. We, the French, are nothing in their eyes; the people trust only in the rice-fields. As Orientals, they respect only force—force alone. We have been too weak, abjectly weak, becoming a laughing-stock in the eyes of a Regent of boundless ambition and cunning. Had we struck at the outset, two soldiers and a corporal would have sufficed to topple the throne. Had we corrected matters last year, all would have been well; but now it is too late—we have allowed them to organize their resistance. That force will rise against us on a day not far distant."

Paul Bonnetain must have lived in Huế in those days, possessing a keen understanding of the Vietnamese situation. His reasoning was perfectly consonant with that of many other French military men who were shedding the blood of the common people without remorse in order to advance the interests of French capitalists and financiers. Their own people they did not spare; the people of another land—would they be more solicitous? Hence they constantly goaded Paris to seize the entire Indochinese peninsula by any means whatsoever, for they feared that France was moving too slowly along the path of colonizing the backward nations of Asia. This conviction had become a deep-rooted tradition, embedded in the minds of the senior military men who filled the Ministry of the Navy and Colonies at that time, and the Ministry of Overseas France thereafter.

On the 12th day of the 4th month of the year Ất Dậu (1885), the French Ministry of War appointed Lieutenant-General Roussel de Courcy to proceed to Vietnam with the most sweeping powers: all French civilian officials, beginning with the Khâm-sứ in Huế, were to be placed under his command. He held supreme civil and political authority with the title of Governor-General, and in military matters served as Military Governor with jurisdiction over all officers and men throughout Annam, Cochinchina, and Tonkin. Lemaire, having lost all authority, could not tolerate this situation. After the gunboat Pluvier, dispatched by de Courcy, arrived at Huế to convey him north for a conference (reaching Tonkin on 31 May 1885), Lemaire presented his accounts, handed over his duties, and tendered his resignation, having no intention of returning to Huế. De Champeaux replaced Lemaire as de Courcy's representative in Huế. Henceforth, with this new appointment, the Khâm-sứ in Huế was no longer the direct representative of the French government.

New mandarin, new regime: de Courcy was instructed to inaugurate a fresh phase in Vietnam—to impose upon that country a rule of unprecedented severity, to reduce the Southern court to a puppet government, to extirpate all opposition root and branch, and to strike hard and fast in Tonkin to bring the war to an end. At this juncture the second round of negotiations between France and the Chinese government had concluded (4 April 1885). Taking advantage of this favourable circumstance, de Courcy's first task was to annihilate the two leaders of the resistance who held all authority on the banks of the Perfume River. He dispatched the following telegram to France upon completing his survey of the situation in Vietnam: "I am profoundly angered by the two Regents of the Huế court and shall proceed with caution but with resolve. Please telegraph to Huế if the Ministry does not approve my intention to employ force."

To his surprise, the Ministry of War did not in fact approve this proposal. De Courcy therefore set about deliberately manufacturing a pretext for the use of force while avoiding responsibility should adverse consequences ensue—in short, he provoked the Southern court and systematically drove it to the wall.


CHAPTER III
DE COURCY PROVOKES THE SOUTHERN COURT INTO RESISTANCE
THE FALL OF THE CITADEL (ẤT DẬU, 1885)

As we have seen, the Patenôtre Convention took effect from the 27th day of the 4th month of the year Ất Dậu (1885), yet France continued to dispatch troops to the Far East for two reasons:

First, a substantial force was required to intimidate Peking into honouring the treaty in good faith—particularly as the Patenôtre Convention contained the lacuna of failing to address the lifting of the blockade of the Yangtze. This fell within the province of Admiral Courbet.

Second, it was necessary to crush the Vietnamese resistance, which was flaring up everywhere in the wake of news that China and France had concluded a separate peace, and that the Qing court had resolved to abandon the Vietnamese cause. This had to be done with urgency, for France recognized—drawing upon her experience in Cochinchina—that while seizing fortresses, defeating regular armies, and compelling the submission of the Southern court was easy, suppressing a nation possessed of a thousand-year history of struggle for freedom and independence was difficult. Moreover, the further the anti-French movement spread, the greater would be France's exertions and expenditures.

On 2 April, General Brière de l'Isle telegraphed to France: "Numerous small rebel bands have formed in every region, including the plains... We have been obliged to organize mobile columns to fight many small engagements, but with great hardship and embarrassment while awaiting the autumn, when we may mobilize in greater strength."

Nevertheless, during this phase France still entertained some hope that the abandonment of Vietnam by the Peking government would dishearten the Vietnamese; but they were mistaken. Vietnamese patriots, though they had lost their ally, did not lay down their arms. The Brisson government (which had replaced the cabinet of J. Ferry) at once appointed General de Courcy to proceed to Vietnam with the title of Governor-General with supreme political and military authority. This commander arrived at Hạ Long Bay on 1 June 1885 (Courbet died a few days before de Courcy reached Vietnam). With the mandate described above, de Courcy began to ride roughshod over the country. But this arch-reactionary and blood-thirsty general was at the same time a shrewd politician and diplomat; accordingly, upon entering Huế he re-enacted the stratagem of seizing the citadel and capturing the commander—the method of F. Garnier and H. Rivière in former days. This act of extreme audacity drew reprimands from the French government, and the principal figure was recalled not long afterward.

De Courcy's first task was to deploy the forces at his disposal to occupy the key positions along the frontier and in the upper regions of northern Vietnam, following the withdrawal of Chinese troops (the evacuation was completed only at the end of May 1885). But the season was the monsoon rains of Tonkin, and French forces could not advance far into the highlands; moreover, from the midlands down to the delta of Tonkin, insurgent bands were springing up around every important French strategic position from the basin of the Red River to Tam Đảo (Vĩnh Yên). But what preoccupied de Courcy's mind above all else was the question of Tôn Thất Thuyết, the leader who held in his hands the soul of the Vietnamese resistance. He also wished simultaneously to avenge the humiliation inflicted upon Guerrier during the enthronement of Hàm Nghi the previous year—an insult which every Frenchman in Vietnam had been obliged to swallow in silence and had not yet forgotten.

As de Courcy set forth with his expeditionary force in full fury, General Brière de l'Isle in Hà Nội remarked: "I have always held that the only solution to the present situation is to abduct the two Regents..." De Courcy himself declared with studied ambiguity: "This question must be resolved in Huế." Commissioner Gosselin, author of L'Empire d'Annam, page 197, quotes from a letter written by the colonial general on 1 July 1885: "Better late than never: we shall arrest Tường and Thuyết, or render them incapable of harming us." What vexed de Courcy most was that these two gentlemen controlled the two most powerful ministries—those of War and of Personnel. Moreover, both possessed a spirit of courage, and did whatever they pleased without consulting Lemaire, the former Khâm-sứ. De Courcy's objective was now to remove Tường and Thuyết from the Regency Council, to strip them of their authority, and to disband the Phấn Nghĩa militia; only then would the Southern court lie entirely passive in French hands.

On the 19th day of the 5th month of the year Ất Dậu (1 July 1885), de Courcy, accompanied by Battalion Commander Metzinger, entered the imperial capital with 800 Arab troops and 150 light infantry, having first imposed a curfew. After arranging all affairs in Tonkin, Champeaux informed the court that the French Military Governor would shortly present his credentials to the King of Vietnam. Tường and Thuyết discussed with Champeaux the temporary arrangements for de Courcy's reception and promised that two senior mandarins with an honour guard would await him at the Thuận An gate. De Champeaux declined.

Aboard the vessels H. Rivière and Brandon, de Courcy arrived at Huế on 1 July 1885 (the 19th of the 5th month, Ất Dậu), with the officer Crétin and two companies of infantry transported by two warships. On the French side stood de Champeaux and several civil and military officials; on the Vietnamese side, two senior mandarins and a guard of soldiers. The city gates were hung with bunting, and as the ships docked, the Vietnamese fired a salute of nineteen guns.

After the French had rested for several days, on the 3rd of July de Champeaux held a secret conference with French civil and military officials to plan the arrest of Tôn Thất Thuyết. The plan was simplicity itself—and singularly devoid of honour. They proposed to stage a Hong Môn banquet, after the fashion of Ngô Tôn Quyền's invitation to Quan Văn Trường, seizing their victim at the appointed hour.

It would appear that this treacherous scheme was betrayed through the indiscretion of one of de Courcy's aides-de-camp, and word reached Thuyết's ears.

Bishop Gaspar, like Khâm-sứ Champeaux, was an old colonial hand who counselled de Courcy against rashness; but he would not be deterred. On the afternoon of the 4th of July—the day before the shooting began—the general spoke with characteristic self-satisfaction:

"On the road of fortune through rank, one always succeeds; wherever rank arrives at the throne, the star of rank is never dimmed. At present, rank has the impression that his star is about to shine even brighter."

We shall see presently whether the star of de Courcy grew bright or dim, and whether he was permitted to remain in this country long enough to swagger about as he so ardently desired.

Tôn Thất Thuyết hastened to confer with Nguyễn Văn Tường on how to deal with the French. Tường spoke:

"Your Excellency is a branch of gold, a leaf of jade; it is only natural that you should devote yourself entirely to the preservation of the dynasty. I, for my part, am a man who would give his life for his country. But consider: if we provoke hostilities, are we certain of avoiding defeat? The citadel of Gia Định was as strong as any, and it could not withstand the enemy's artillery. The affair at Thuận An of late has already disturbed the public mind and shaken the jade foundations. Should fighting break out, His Majesty could not escape the shock... To surrender our treasures and territories to the enemy now, so as to avert greater calamity, is one way to resolve the matter. If Your Excellency is confident of defeating the enemy and restoring peace, then I defer entirely to Your Excellency; I dare not presume to advise..."

Thuyết recognized that Tường's arguments contained more than a grain of truth; but he was burning with the spirit of resistance, and being pressed into a corner at that very moment, he felt that in any case the question must be settled with sword and musket. To sit with folded arms and submit to being bound was unthinkable. He saw himself standing in exactly the same predicament as Nguyễn Tri Phương and Hoàng Diệu in former days—not a jot different. Moreover, the intention of France was to seize the whole of Vietnam and strip the Vietnamese of every vestige of authority by the insidious method of the protectorate; this was clear enough, and any accommodation would not last long. Then he uttered his final words:

"One who eats the King's rice must repay the nation's debt. Military authority rests in my hands; to let the enemy seize the citadel would be dishonour. Whether the struggle against France brings victory or defeat, it must be undertaken. I have already ordered all officers and men to prepare for battle."

De Courcy then sent invitations to the princes and the members of the Privy Council to cross the river and attend a banquet at the Residency (the Residency stood on the opposite bank, facing the Citadel), there to discuss the ceremony of audience with King Hàm Nghi and the presentation of credentials. Everyone attended except the Second Regent. De Courcy was furious. At the banquet, Courcy raised his glass to toast the prosperity of both Vietnam and France, then delivered several remarks of stunning bluntness:

"If your kingdom wishes to remain tranquil, within three days you must deliver to us the cost of the war: 200,000 bars of gold, 200,000 bars of silver, and 200,000 strings of cash..."

"We are pleased to see so many of you present; but where is the Second Regent, Tôn Thất Thuyết? Perhaps the Regent is at home preparing to receive us?"

The company blanched, knowing that de Courcy was showing his hand and seeking a quarrel. One mandarin ventured an excuse:

"The Regent is indisposed and cannot come."

De Courcy smiled thinly and bellowed in reply:

"If he is ill, then he should come by palanquin. I shall have him fetched."

With this, the discussions regarding the ceremony of audience with King Hàm Nghi all but collapsed. The Privy Council proposed that the Commander-in-Chief enter the court by the central gate while his officers and soldiers used the two side gates and bore no arms; that upon reaching the second row of columns in the Thái Hòa Palace, a senior mandarin would receive the credentials and present them to the king. De Courcy refused. He insisted that the king descend from the Dragon Throne to receive in person both the credentials and the text of the treaty recently ratified by the French parliament; and he demanded that his officers and soldiers accompany him through the central gate. The negotiations dragged on. At length de Courcy postponed the matter, awaiting Thuyết's recovery. As for the gifts sent by the Queen Mother Từ Dũ and by King Hàm Nghi, de Courcy ordered them returned.

On the 3rd of July, Thuyết busied himself in frantic preparations for military action, concentrating the troops of the various guard units and dividing them into two columns, summoning Colonel Tôn Thất Lệ back from Sơn Phòng. Tường, for his part, protested to de Courcy about the French soldiers who terrorized the common people and went about in slovenly dress, and requested that they be barred from the Citadel. Despite assurances from Lieutenant-Colonel Pernot and Bishop Caspar, de Courcy refused to believe that the Southern court would dare resist France. On the morning of 4 July 1885, de Champeaux received a secret report from a French agent that Thuyết was deliberately avoiding de Courcy and that Tường was following him only under duress. The Queen Mother Từ Dũ (and Prince Hoài Đức, brother of King Thiệu Trị) had not approved the violent designs of the resistance faction.

When the banquet ended, Nguyễn Văn Tường went straight to the Ministry of War to find Thuyết and declared: "The moment has come—there is no more room for delay. We must strike at once, come what may."

Tôn Thất Thuyết, beside himself with anger, conferred with Nguyễn Văn Tường on launching an immediate attack before the enemy could react. In his view, France was already seizing all of Tonkin: the provinces of Thái Nguyên, Tuyên Quang, Hưng Hóa, and others had all been occupied; now French troops were posted at the Thuận An gate as well—to do nothing was to lose the country regardless. Better to risk one decisive engagement with the enemy and let the consequences fall as they might. It was, in truth, a gamble with heaven at that moment. He then made the following dispositions: the front would be entrusted to the Dragon-Boat and Võ Sinh units, crack troops versed in the arts of assault, who would attack the French position directly. Within the Citadel, the Cầm Y and Kim Ngô guards would hold the inner defences. Newly recruited soldiers would be responsible for the rear. At the Đông Ba gate, five companies would be posted stretching to Trường Đình; at the An Hòa and Rear gates, five companies each; at the Left gate, the Hồ Oai regiment, composed of veteran fighters; at the main gate and the Tây gate, the Hùng Duệ corps. Outside the Citadel, war elephants would be deployed, each with two mahouts. Artillery would be emplaced at all necessary and commanding points. On the far bank of the river, the Phấn Nghĩa militia and the Dragon-Boat forces would constitute the vanguard—suicide troops awaiting the signal to storm the French barracks. Prisoners in the two jails of Thừa Thiên and Trấn Phủ would be released, given food, wine, and ample money, to add their strength to the fight.

In sum, the column under Tôn Thất Lệ's command would cross the river at midnight to join forces with the Admiral's troops and the riverine navy, their mission to attack the Residency. Thuyết himself would lead the "Phấn Nghĩa" column together with Admiral Trần Xuân Soan to strike the southern face of Trấn Binh Đài (Mang Cá).

At sunset, Admiral-Governor Trần Soan led a detachment to secure the city gates and to emplace additional cannon. The Vietnamese forces numbered 12,000 in all, and received orders to cook rice in advance of possible emergency. At the Privy Council, the conference planned to open fire at ten o'clock in the evening, since at that hour the French officers would still be at the Residency, leaving Mang Cá without a commander. But the disadvantage was that at that hour it was still too dark to direct artillery fire; moreover, the deployment of troops had not yet been completed, and it was therefore necessary to postpone the commencement of hostilities to the hour of Sửu (one o'clock in the morning).

All arrangements being complete, Tôn Thất Thuyết and his son Tôn Thất Đạm ascended the watchtower to await the end of the hour of Tý before giving the signal.

Though the preparations had been made with the utmost secrecy, they did not escape the notice of Bishop Caspar. He informed de Courcy at once; yet de Courcy still did not believe it. Meanwhile, a number of mandarins of the Southern court, upon seeing the war preparations, turned pale with dread. Some murmured: "Press too hard and you provoke a catastrophe." Others said: "This defensive posture is more than sufficient; resistance to France can bring nothing but harm." Before the spirit of retreat and defeatism that pervaded their ranks, the party of Thuyết had indeed embarked upon an adventure.

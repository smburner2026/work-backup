CHAPTER III
THE FRENCH INSTALL EMPEROR ĐỒNG KHÁNH
[Original: PHÁP TÔN-LẬP VUA ĐỒNG-KHÁNH]

THE FRENCH CONFUSION IN CENTRAL VIETNAM

A question that might astonish those following the current events up to this period is that after Mr. Thuyet's surprise attack on the French Residency Office and the Mang Ca garrison, the French did not dare to immediately pursue the defeated royal court as it fled urgently to Quang Tri. According to documents from the archives of the Resident's Office in Hue (F.V. page 67, appendix 1), after Mr. Tuong had returned to the French side and re-assumed the post of Minister of the Personnel (and Finance) Ministry, he assigned Truong Quang Dan to Quang Tri affairs, reporting that since 9 July 1885 he had requested General de Courcy for troops to go to Quang Tri to retrieve the emperor, but Mr. Truong requested that this be postponed until the three Empress Dowagers had returned to Hue, then asked for an additional 300 native auxiliaries. During this same period, de Courcy received orders from the French Ministry of War not to take any strong action in the Land of Nam (1). The French general was also hesitant before the problem that installing a new king would hardly win popular support; he knew that even if Ham Nghi was in a weak position, he enjoyed popular support because the king was patriotic and dared to resist the enemy. The new king about to be installed would in any case be merely a puppet king. The installation of a new king at this time showed that the French colonials did not believe they could wrest Ham Nghi from the hands of the Resistance. By then, the scholar-officials and people of Vietnam were rising in rebellion everywhere, further adding to French confusion. Although the French controlled the Imperial Citadel and the city of Hue, their influence barely extended beyond the Perfume River and Ngu Mountain area. After the events of 5 July 1885, the situation —

Vietnam was completely transformed. In Hue, people acted as if there was no government or authority whatsoever. The French saw the need to find a political solution to support their military one. At one point the French considered ignoring the need for a king and the Vietnamese court altogether, simply turning Vietnam directly into a colony like Cochinchina. But upon reflection, they saw the following difficulty: This would certainly bring about the definite consequence that the remaining scholar-gentry, feudalists, and monarchists would abandon their hesitation and resolutely rally behind the banner of the Resistance. The strength and prestige of the Resistance would multiply greatly. Pacification would never be completed. Furthermore, the colonial principle in conquering a country had always been that one must ally with the ruling class of that country to achieve success. Indeed, this principle originates from the key doctrine of imperial political science...

A second complication was: abolishing the Vietnamese court like that — the French would be unilaterally abrogating the Treaty of Tientsin. The French asked themselves: Could they avoid complications with China? These were the ideas that strongly agitated the minds of the colonialists in Saigon, Hue, via Paris, and back again. Then they had to withdraw the above intention and saw no better solution than to choose another king to serve as a puppet, although they knew that the Vietnamese people would always only recognize Ham Nghi as the legitimate king, the sole representative with value for the nation. Faced with this difficulty, de Courcy and Nguyen Van Tuong conferred together and issued a declaration stating that the two countries of Vietnam and France still maintained friendly relations as before, so that people at home and abroad would not be bewildered. On 15 July, he convened the Nguyen royal family at the Legation to temporarily install Prince Tho Xuan as Regent while awaiting King Ham Nghi's return. Prince Tho Xuan, i.e. Tho Xuan Vuong Mien Dinh, was the uncle of King Tu Duc, then aged 75. He was also the clan chief of the Nguyen line. Another noteworthy matter was that de Courcy appointed de Champeaux as Minister of War in the provisional and powerless court, and the Vietnamese army was completely disbanded. He also summoned Nguyen Huu Do, who was serving as Governor of Ha-Ninh in Bac Ky, and

Phan Dinh Binh, former Governor of Nam Dinh, to Hue together with Nguyen Van Tuong to manage the Privy Council (1). These two men were already experienced in serving the Protectorate and had long had pro-French spirit. But the two gentlemen Tuong and Do could not reach agreement with each other, so Mr. Do returned to Bac Ky a few days later.

On the 28th of the lunar month, the Three Dowager Empresses received word from Nguyen Van Tuong saying everything had been arranged and inviting the Ladies to return. The Ladies sent people to fetch the king but only received a letter from Mr. Thuyet denouncing the betrayal of Nguyen Van Tuong and urging the Ladies not to listen. Everyone was indecisive for two days, then on the 30th the Ladies received another dispatch from Tuong. Lady Tu Du then decided to depart on the 4th of the following month. On the evening of the 5th day of the 6th month, At Dau year (17 July 1885), the Empress Dowagers returned to Khiem Cung. Accompanying the procession were Secretary Truong Nhu Cuong and Ton That Thiem. Truong Quang Dan also brought troops for escort. On 17 June, Nguyen Van Tuong and a Frenchman (rank unknown) named Ranchot came to pay respects to the Ladies at their temporary residence. On this occasion, as mentioned above, the French and their traitorous Vietnamese collaborators exploited the Empress Dowagers by issuing a directive calling upon the notables, the population, and the Catholics to remain calm and continue their livelihoods, and for the princes and officials to return to the capital and work as before.

As we know, de Courcy's proposal had no effect; he was somewhat disappointed, so he summoned Silvestre, the Director of Administration and Politics in Bac Ky, and Nguyen Huu Do from Hanoi to Hue to resolve the Central region problem. This group arrived in Hue on 28 July. After meeting with de Courcy, Silvestre visited Nguyen Van Tuong at the Thuong Bac (Commercial Council) and together they decided to hold a Grand Council the following day. On the morning of 29 July, Tuong, Do, and their associates studied for a very long time the plan to stabilize the general situation and to choose a political regime for Central and Northern Vietnam.

After these deliberations, de Courcy cabled France requesting that an Additional Protocol be appended to the Treaty of Giap Than (6 August 1884) to be implemented in order to stabilize the situation as soon as possible. However, on 13 August 1885, the French Ministry of War rejected most of the Protocol's provisions, especially because parliamentary elections were approaching in October, and the Cabinet did not want to provoke major military operations in Tonkin (1). Some believe that due to the contradictory policies of those in charge, the Vietnamese Resistance in Central and Northern Vietnam had free rein to operate and expand; whereas if Paris had heeded de Courcy's faction and struck hard and fast, the Resistance would not have had time to react, and the Protectorate could have been established sooner. Not only were they unable to act, but due to epidemics and unsanitary camps, French military morale visibly declined — also a disaster. In Bac Ky as well as in Central Vietnam at this time, by 31 August of that year, the accounts of French military hospitals recorded up to 4,000 deaths, i.e., one-sixth of the French forces in Vietnam at the time (2). This situation was further aggravated by the massacre not long before of 24,298 Catholics of the Qui Nhon diocese in July 1885. Three French missionaries were killed, churches and seminaries were completely destroyed, and additionally, 8,620 Catholics were martyred in Quang Tri province from 8 to 20 September.

The Nam Trieu (Imperial Court) was reorganized but without any fundamental change. Some pro-French officials were placed in positions formerly held by civil and military personnel who had joined the Resistance. At the end of August that year, in the midst of the intense Vietnam-France conflict, news came that King Ham Nghi had fallen severely ill with malaria and dropsy in Laos. The newspaper Avenir du Tonkin of 10 September 1885 reported:

"... People rumored that the king was gravely ill; some confirmed that he had been poisoned. Many Catholics spread the news outside Hanoi — in reality this was just a cunning scheme of the French and their lackeys to test public opinion and confuse the spirit of the Vietnamese masses." Bishop Puginier of Ke So went up to meet De Courcy to exchange views on this matter. Two high mandarins — Nguyen Trong Hop, acting Viceroy (Kinh-lược) of Bac Ky, and Phan Dinh Binh, former Governor of Nam Dinh now serving as Minister of Finance — took this opportunity to raise the issue that Ham Nghi's ascension to the throne was illegitimate. In fact, these two high officials were merely political bosses, either of their own accord or under orders to clear the way for the French colonists to depose King Ham Nghi because the king refused to return to them and play the role of a puppet and figurehead. To this day, we find no historical record of what arguments Hop and Binh actually put forward when they claimed that Prince Ung Lich's occupying the supreme position was irrational or illegal on one ground or another. What is clear is that de Courcy could not let Prince Tho Xuan prolong his role as Regent because he saw that the prince had not created any favorable political influence among the masses. Earlier, Tho Xuan had issued a proclamation to the nation accusing Thuyet of establishing a rebellious party, but who paid any attention to that proclamation? Criticizing Regent Tho Xuan, the author of L'Empire d'Annam said: "The only ability of this Regent was to sign his name." This measure having proved ineffective, they turned to other schemes. Thus, there had to be high-ranking

mandarins who would speak out to both destroy the prestige of King Ham Nghi and legitimize the installation of the new king, Mr. Chanh Mong — later King Dong Khanh. In summary, one can conclude either that Hop and Binh attacked Ham Nghi and then showed the deer the way to run (i.e., deliberately misled) to earn merit with the French, or that the French prompted these time-serving feudal elements to legitimize the installation of King Dong Khanh according to a new plan so that the scholar-officials and people of Vietnam would forget their exiled king. The French were accustomed to playing the game of staying behind the scenes and pulling strings, as they would many times later throw stones and hide their hands, use scapegoats when dealing with our revolutionaries.

When Empress Dowager Tu Du received the proposal to install a new king — "since Ham Nghi does not know when he will return, since Ham Nghi does not deserve to hold the supreme position" — she was initially hesitant, but later yielded to the treacherous faction and issued a declaration of deposition. Nevertheless, she was somewhat consoled because in deposing Ham Nghi the French were installing Mr. Chanh Mong Ung Ky (i.e., Kien Giang Quan Cong), the older brother of the two deceased kings, which she considered acceptable. In reality, the resistance forces had shed blood and bones to block the invading enemy and were defeated everywhere — what reason was there to encourage the Empress Dowager to oppose the French? Moreover, the Empress Dowager had a strongly comfort-seeking mentality even from the time when King Duc Tong was still alive, which had already caused much harm to national affairs...

The big roles in the Hue Court fabricated by de Courcy for convenience in directing matters at that time included Nguyen Huu Do, who was officially appointed by the French to replace Nguyen Van Tuong as Chairman of the Privy Council, and Phan Dinh Binh, former Governor of Nam Dinh, appointed Minister of Finance to jointly manage national affairs from Central to Northern Vietnam. Nguyen Van Tuong was permanently expelled from politics because de Courcy considered him a dangerous man, unfathomably cunning, and that if Tuong remained in the Court, he might seize control of the new inexperienced king and cause trouble for the French in ways and at times unknown (1).

On 17 October 1885, Major-General Prudhomme deemed it possible to lead troops to capture King Ham Nghi and suppress the resistance; a base was being established in the highlands of Quang Binh and Ha Tinh. This base belonged to Qui Hop canton, Tuyen Hoa district, situated between the two provinces of Quang Binh and Ha Tinh, against the high mountain range starting from the Truong Son and extending straight to the sea. Traditionally, to enter Ha Tinh, people used National Highway No. 1, the thousand-li road hugging the coast, or the Tan Ap Pass (also called Bai Duc Pass). But for several years now, the Resistance had anticipated enemy occupation of the coast and therefore opened a new road running parallel to the thousand-li road. This road passes through Tan Ap Pass, where the Ngan Sau River (flowing to Ben Thuy) and the Gianh River (flowing to Quang Khe) are located. These two rivers are 15 kilometers apart. King Ham Nghi was based in the upper reaches of these two rivers because it was a fertile and densely populated area. Moreover, from a strategic perspective, the Resistance could from here advance northward or, if necessary, retreat through this point into Laos. High mountains and deep forests concealed the Resistance so well that the enemy could hardly find them. Ch. Gosselin had to say: "Truly the region King Ham Nghi chose for his base was very clever.

It facilitated communication between both Quang Binh and Ha Tinh provinces. Here was also the territory of the Van Than (literati), of the hereditary clans ready to resist our authority..." (L'Empire d'Annam, p. 249). Moreover, if the French used ships to advance from the coast, they would encounter sandbanks. From Thanh Thuy upward, the Gianh River basin is often cut into sections by rocky mountains extending into the plains. The Righteous Army on the high ground could easily monitor all enemy movements. Below lived the Kinh people; in the forests lived the Muong compatriots; the total population reached 40,000, with Catholics making up about a quarter. Local people gathered at Cho Don market three times a month for trade. Each gathering attracted up to ten thousand people, making this place a commercial center. King Ham Nghi established his headquarters here, with the added advantage of communication with scholars and Righteous Army groups in the South and North.

Prudhomme had Priest Hoang employ someone familiar with Ha Tinh's geography to study the terrain before launching the campaign. All three recognized that they would have to exploit all 11 passes through the mountains (Qui Hop Pass) to reach King Ham Nghi's hiding place, which at this time was Ham Thao in Tuyen Hoa district. (The war map drawn by Hoang was later filed in the archives of the French Resident's office, according to missionary A. Delvaux.) De Courcy was in the process of moving French troops from Bac Ky to join with the French force at Nghe An under General Prudhomme when he was recalled to France on 28 January 1886. Commandant Carnot, commanding the lower Gianh River unit, also left Hue on the 16th of that month, while Lieutenant-Colonel Metzinger departed on 30 January 1886 to take command in Quang Binh. Metzinger was tasked with providing rear support for the above forces, establishing headquarters at Cho Don and Minh Nong. He brought Father Tortuyaux along as a guide to manage the Vé outpost. At this time, King Ham Nghi, though in exile and on the run, had managed to make contact with several Can Vuong units.

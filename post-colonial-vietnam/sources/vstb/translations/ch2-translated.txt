CHAPTER I

DE COURCY PROVOKES THE SOUTHERN COURT'S RESISTANCE — THE FALL OF THE IMPERIAL CITADEL (ẤT-DẬU-1885)

As we have seen, the Patenôtre Treaty[1] had been in effect since the 27th day of the 4th month of the year Ất-Dậu (1885), yet France continued to dispatch troops to the Far East for two reasons.

First, a substantial force was needed to intimidate Beijing and compel the Qing court to execute faithfully the treaty already signed — especially the Patenôtre Treaty, which contained the loophole of failing to mention the lifting of the naval blockade of the Yangtze River, a matter that fell within the operational domain of Admiral Courbet.

Second, it was necessary to crush the Vietnamese resistance, which was boiling over everywhere after word arrived that China and France had made a separate peace and that the Qing court had resolved to abandon the Vietnamese struggle. This task required urgency, for France recognized from its experience in Cochinchina (Nam Kỳ) that seizing citadels, defeating regular armies, and pressuring the royal court of the Southern Kingdom was easy enough — but suppressing a people who had a thousand-year history of fighting for freedom and independence was another matter entirely. Moreover, the more the anti-French movement expanded, the greater the cost and difficulty France would incur.

On the 24th[2], General Brière de l'Isle telegraphed Paris reporting that "many small bands of insurgents have been formed everywhere, including in the delta regions... we have been forced to organize numerous mobile columns to fight many small engagements, but they have been extremely arduous and costly while we await the rainy season before we can operate with larger troop numbers."

Nevertheless, during this period France still cherished some hope that Beijing's abandonment of Vietnam would cause the Vietnamese to lose heart — but they were mistaken. Though the Vietnamese scholar-gentry and people had lost their ally, they did not lay down their swords or lower their guns. The government of Prime Minister Brisson (which had replaced that of Jules Ferry) immediately appointed General de Courcy to Vietnam with the title of Political and Military Governor-General (Toàn-quyền chính-trị và quân-sự). This commanding general arrived at Hạ Long Bay on 1 June 1885 (Courbet had died a few days before de Courcy reached Vietnam). With this mandate, De Courcy set about creating storms in our country.

But this heavy-handed royalist reactionary and utterly bloodthirsty general was also profoundly ignorant of politics and diplomacy. Consequently, once in Huế he re-enacted the old farce of luring commanders into a citadel and seizing them, in the style of Francis Garnier and Henri Rivière before him. For this reckless act, the French government would soon have to reprimand and recall the principal culprit not long afterward.

De Courcy's first priority, with the troops at his disposal, was to deploy them to take over the key positions along the border and in the upper regions of Northern Vietnam (Bắc Việt) after the Chinese forces had withdrawn (the evacuation of the Qing army was completed only at the end of the 5th month of 1885)[3]. But at this time it was the rainy season in Tonkin (Bắc Kỳ); the French could not advance far into the highlands. Moreover, from the Midlands (Trung du) down to the Delta (Trung châu) of Tonkin, insurgent forces were actively competing with each other around France's important strategic positions, from the Red River basin up to Tam Đảo (Vĩnh Yên). Yet what burned most fiercely in de Courcy's mind was the problem of Tôn Thất Thuyết — the leader who held in his hands the very soul of the Vietnamese resistance. The general also wanted simultaneously to wash away the humiliation that all Frenchmen in Vietnam had been forced to swallow during the Guerrier[4] affair the year before, when the enthronement of Emperor Hàm Nghi had gone so badly; they had not forgotten it.

[Footnote 3: After J. Duncan Campbell, a Chinese Customs Service officer representing the Qing government, and Billot, a political inspector from the French Foreign Ministry, had concluded the ceasefire agreement, Admiral Lưu Vĩnh Phúc was compelled to fold his sleeves and withdraw. He left behind much regret among his comrades-in-arms in Vietnam, for he had devoted himself wholeheartedly to cooperating with our people in resisting Western invasion. He had achieved many great feats, killed many enemy troops, and could not suppress his indignation.]

As de Courcy set off fiercely with his troops, General Brière de l'Isle in Hanoi reminded him: "I have always maintained that the surest way to resolve the present situation is to kidnap the two regents." And de Courcy himself had already declared, in half-veiled terms: "This problem must be resolved in Huế." Captain Gosselin, author of *L'Empire d'Annam*, quotes on page 197 a passage from a letter written by this colonial general on 1 July 1885: "Better late than never — we shall arrest Tường and Thuyết, or else we shall put them beyond the means of harming us."

What de Courcy found most galling was that these two men controlled the two most important and powerful organs of state: the Ministry of War (Bộ Binh) and the Ministry of Personnel (Bộ Lại). Moreover, the two men possessed such courageous spirit that they acted entirely on their own judgment, never deigning to consult with Lemaire, the earlier French Resident (Khâm-sứ) in Huế. De Courcy's objective now was to eliminate Tường and Thuyết from the Regency Council (Hội-đồng Phụ-chính), strip them of their authority, and disband the Phản-nghĩa[5] troops. Only thus could the Southern Court (Nam-triều) lie entirely passive in French hands.

On the 19th day of the 4th month of Ất-Dậu (1 July 1885), De Courcy, together with Chef de Bataillon Metzinger, brought 800 Arab soldiers and 150 light infantry into the Nguyễn capital after imposing martial law and making all arrangements outside Tonkin. Champeaux immediately informed the Southern Court that the French military governor-general was about to present his credentials to the Vietnamese monarch. The two regents, Tường and Thuyết, discussed with Champeaux every detail of the reception for de Courcy and promised to dispatch two high dignitaries with an honor guard to await him at Thuận An. De Champeaux refused.

[The footnote about the commanders who stayed to fight alongside Vietnam, including: Lục Đông Hoàn, Vương Ngọc Chấu, Đàm Vinh Đình, Hoàng Tuấn Phương, Diệp Thành Lâm, Chu Bằng Thanh, Lưu Văn Khiêm, Tham Thông Chính, Lương Mậu Lâm, Tạ Bình An — who for a time dominated the upper regions of Tonkin among the ethnic minority peoples.]

Aboard the ships *H. Rivière* and *Brandon*, de Courcy arrived at Huế on 1 July 1885 (the 19th day of the 5th month of Ất-Dậu), accompanied by an officer named Crétin and two companies of infantry transported by two warships. On the French side, de Champeaux and several civil and military officials attended. On our side, two grand mandarins and soldiers stood in formation. All city gates were hung with banners, and as the ships came into dock, our side fired nineteen cannon salutes in welcome.

After the French party had rested there for several days, on 3 July de Champeaux secretly discussed with French civil and military officials the plan to arrest Tôn Thất Thuyết. The scheme was exceedingly simple and also exceedingly dishonorable: they would hold a "Hongmen banquet"[6] in the manner of Ngô Quyền inviting the Governor of Văn Trường to a feast in order to seize him in Nghệ An. It appears that this treacherous plot reached Thuyết's ears through the indiscretion of one of de Courcy's own staff officers[7].

Bishop Gaspar (Giám-mục Gaspar), like Resident Champeaux, was a seasoned colonial hand who tried to dissuade de Courcy from acting rashly, but he would not listen. On the afternoon of 4 July, the day before the shooting began, this conquering general declared with self-satisfaction:

"On the path of honor and achievement, I have always succeeded; wherever I have gone, my star has never dimmed. I feel now that my star is about to shine even brighter..."

Soon enough we shall see whether De Courcy's star brightened or dimmed, and whether he was permitted to remain long in this land to strut and rage as he had wished.

Tôn Thất Thuyết hurried to consult with Nguyễn Văn Tường on how to respond to the French.

[Footnote 7: According to Bửu Kế, author of the article "TÒA KHẤM SỨ" in *Bách Khoa* magazine, issue no. 433, pp. 26–27, the plot to arrest Thuyết was leaked by several kitchen hands at the French Residency.]

Concerning de Courcy's arrogant and self-satisfied attitude, the author of *L'Empire d'Annam*, Ch. Gosselin, writes on page 203: "The supreme and boundless error of General De Courcy was that he knew nothing whatsoever about Annam. He thoroughly despised the Annamese people. He believed that the Annamese mandarins would not dare to take any action at all, and he thought that a battalion of Zouaves and a company of infantry would be enough to terrify the Regency Council, the court, and the entire Annamese people."

Regarding the strategy for dealing with the French, Tường said: "Your Excellency is a jewel of the realm, and it is natural that you should devote yourself utterly to the support of the altars of the state. As for me, I am but one of the hundred surnames who knows also how to sacrifice self for country. But ought we not consider that if war breaks out, might we not be defeated? The Gia Định Citadel was as solid as a rock, yet it could not withstand the enemy's cannon. The recent affair at Thuận An has already shaken the people's hearts and dissipated our treasure. And if we fight, His Majesty cannot escape terror and distress... Now, to offer up our wealth and land to them in order to end the affair is also one way of resolving the situation. If there is another way — if Your Excellency believes you can surely defeat the enemy and restore peace — then do as you see fit; I dare not discuss it further..."

Though Thuyết recognized that Nguyễn Văn Tường's reasoning had some truth to it, he was at this point already inflamed with the spirit of resistance and cornered by circumstances. He believed that matters must inevitably be settled with the sword and the gun against France — he could hardly fold his arms and submit to capture. He saw himself standing in precisely the same situation as Nguyễn Tri Phương and Hoàng Diệu before him, without the slightest difference. Besides, France's intention was plainly to seize all of Vietnam and strip the Vietnamese of every vestige of authority through the policy of "incremental encroachment" (tầm thực)[8]; even a truce would not last long. Then he declared his final word: "He who eats the King's rice must repay the nation's debt. As long as military power remains in our hands, to let the enemy seize the capital is shameful. Whether we win or lose in resisting the French, we must do it. I have already ordered the commanders to prepare for battle..."

Then de Courcy issued invitations to the Imperial Princes (Hoàng-thân) and the members of the Privy Council (Cơ-mật-viện) to cross the river and attend a banquet at the French Residency (Tòa Khâm lay on the opposite bank, facing the Imperial Citadel) to discuss the protocol for an audience with Emperor Hàm Nghi and the presentation of the credentials. Everyone attended except the Second Regent (Phụ-chính). De Courcy was furious. At the banquet, Courcy raised a toast to the prosperity of the two nations, Vietnam and France, and then suddenly and brazenly produced a number of remarks:

"If your illustrious country wishes to live in peace, within three days you must pay us a war indemnity of 200,000 bars of gold, 200,000 bars of silver, and 200,000 strings of cash..."

"We are pleased to see you all present here in such numbers, but why is the Regent Tôn Thất Thuyets absent? Could it be that the Regent is at home preparing to fight us?"

Everyone's face turned pale, recognizing that de Courcy was showing his true colors and picking a quarrel. One grand mandarin tried to mediate: "Our Regent is ill and unable to attend."

De Courcy gave a contemptuous laugh and declared emphatically: "If he is ill, he must come by sedan-chair. I order him brought here to be seen!"

[Footnote 1 — actually the second footnote in the original: The delegation that crossed to the banquet at the Residency was led by Nguyễn Văn Tường and Phạm Thận Duật. According to *Việt-Nam Pháp-thuộc-sử*, de Courcy, not seeing Thuyết arrive, showed his anger openly. Our officials had to go back to urge him; still he refused. After various urgent messages failed, de Courcy then sent the doctor Mangin to offer treatment, so as to investigate the true situation. But Thuyết did not receive him, sending word in refusal that he was not accustomed to Western medicine. The situation between the two sides had clearly become far too tense.]

"Tầm thực" (incremental encroachment) is like the silkworm eating the mulberry leaf or an oil stain spreading — once it takes hold, it takes everything.

At this, the discussion of the audience ceremony with Emperor Hàm Nghi fell apart. The Privy Council requested that the Governor-General enter the court through the central gate, while the attendant mandarins and soldiers would use the two side gates and would not bear arms; when the Governor-General entered the Thái Hòa Palace, he would proceed as far as the second row of pillars, where a grand mandarin would hold up the state document to present to the monarch. De Courcy refused. He insisted that the Emperor must descend from the Dragon Throne to receive the state letter and the treaty document, which had been ratified by the French Parliament a few weeks earlier. He further demanded that his entire staff of adjutants and accompanying soldiers also enter through the central gate. The argument dragged on; finally de Courcy ordered a postponement, pending Tôn Thất Thuyết's recovery. As for the gifts sent by the Dowager Empress Từ Dũ and by Emperor Hàm Nghi, de Courcy had them returned[9].

[Footnote 9: At noon on the 22nd day of the 5th month of Ất-Dậu, the Southern Court still entrusted several grand mandarins to go to the Residency to negotiate about the side-gate protocol for the mandarins and accompanying troops, but de Courcy would not receive them. In diplomatic terms, this was decidedly a provocative act.]

During the day of the 3rd of July[10], Thuyết hastily prepared to move his troops, concentrating the men from various garrisons and dividing them into two corps. He summoned his younger brother, Tham-biện Tôn Thất Lễ, back from the Mountain Defense Command (Sơn-phòng). Tường, for his part, protested to de Courcy about French soldiers harassing the populace and dressing slovenly, requesting that they be forbidden to enter the inner citadel. Although Lieutenant-Colonel Pernot and Bishop Caspar brought him intelligence, Courcy still did not believe that the Southern Court would dare to resist the French.

On the morning of 4 July 1885, de Champeaux received a secret report from a French spy that Thuyết was deliberately avoiding de Courcy and that Tường was being reluctantly compelled to follow him; that Dowager Từ Dũ (along with the Imperial Prince Hoài Đức, younger brother of Emperor Thiệu Trị) had not approved the violent plan of the resistance faction[11].

[Footnote 11: According to A. Delvaux, this secret report may have been put forward by Tường or one of his subordinates (*Guerre du Tonkin*, p. 885). But in our opinion this is not correct, for if Tường had truly been in collusion with the French, de Courcy's attitude toward him would not have been so extremely hostile when Tường met him later in Huế.]

After the banquet, Nguyễn Văn Tường went straight to the Ministry of War to find Thuyết and told him: "The time has come when there can be no further delay. We must take the risk now, whatever comes."

Tôn Thất Thuyết, also at the peak of his fury, discussed with Nguyễn Văn Tường the need to strike hard before the enemy could react. In his view, France was already occupying all of Tonkin — the provinces of Thái Nguyên, Tuyên Quang, Hưng Hóa, etc., had all been taken. Now the French had come to station themselves even at Thuận An. Without making a move, the country was already lost; better to fight one decisive battle against the enemy, win or lose, and then see what happens. It was truly a gamble with heaven at this point. Then he laid out his dispositions as follows: the front line would be entrusted to the Long-thuyền and Võ-sinh companies — elite units expert in martial arts — who would attack straight into the French positions. Inside the Imperial Citadel, the Cấm-vệ (Imperial Guard) and Kim-ngô troops would hold the defenses. Newly recruited soldiers would cover the rear. At Đông Ba Gate, five regiments (vệ) would be stationed, stretching as far as Trường Đình. At An Hòa Gate and the Rear Gate (Cửa Hậu), five regiments each would be placed. At the Left Gate (Cửa Tả), the Hùng-Oai regiment of battle-hardened veterans was positioned; at the main gate and the Western Gate, the Hùng-Duệ company was assigned. Outside the citadel, elephant troops were readied, each elephant assigned to two mahouts (nài). Artillery was placed at all necessary and strategic points.

Across the river, the Phản-nghĩa and Long-thuyền forces — the shock troops — were to be held in readiness, awaiting the order to charge straight into the French camp. Prisoners in the two jails of Thừa Thiên and Trấn Phủ were to be released, given rice, wine, and ample money to contribute their strength to the fighting. In sum, the force commanded by Tôn Thất Lễ would cross the river at midnight to join the troops of the Đề-đốc (Admiral) and the naval Hiệp-lý, tasked with attacking the French Residency. Meanwhile, Thuyết himself would lead the "Phản-nghĩa" army with Đề-đốc Trần Xuân Soạn to launch a surprise attack on the southern side of Trấn-Bình-Đài (Mang Cá).

As the sun was setting, Đề-đốc Trần Soạn led a detachment to occupy the city gates and placed a few more cannon in position. Our troops numbered 12,000 in all, and were ordered to cook rice in advance in case of emergency. At the Privy Council (Cơ-mật-viện), the council resolved to open fire at 10 p.m., since at that hour the French officers would still be at the Residency, meaning Mang Cá would have no commander. But the disadvantage was that at this hour it was still too dark to aim the heavy artillery; moreover, the disposition of troops was not yet complete, so the hour of attack had to be postponed to the hour of Sửu (1 a.m.).

Once everything was prepared, Tôn Thất Thuyết and his son Tôn Thất Đạm ascended the watchtower to await the end of the hour of Tý (the midnight hour), when the signal would be given.

Although the preparations had been made with utmost secrecy, they could not escape the notice of Bishop Caspar, who immediately informed de Courcy — but de Courcy still would not believe it[12]. During this time, many Southern Court officials, seeing the war preparations, turned pale. Some argued that tension would inevitably lead to disaster; others said that such defensive preparations were already excessive and that fighting the French could only bring harm. Faced with their defeatist and capitulationist spirit, Thuyết and his circle were indeed embarking upon a desperate gamble.

[Footnote 12: It is rightly said that many French missionaries served as agents and spies for the colonial army — this accusation is not false. They acted as spies throughout the period of French conquest and domination of our country.]

On the evening of the 22nd day of the 5th month (4 July 1885), the French Governor-General hosted a banquet for French civil and military officials on the upper floor of the Legation building, in very high spirits. The moon had already risen high. In the midst of the feast, de Courcy discussed plans for a grand military show of force on the day he would go to court to pay homage to Emperor Hàm Nghi. Towards the first watch, on the river near the Legation, the movement of our boats suddenly became unusually busy, prompting the duty officer, Second Lieutenant Bouché, to report to the Governor-General. In the midst of the revelry, de Courcy, annoyed, threatened to punish him for raising a false alarm. At 11:30 p.m., the dinner guests rose. Our cannons, already positioned on the ramparts and trained on Thanh Long Bridge, were waiting to fire as the French officials departed, but because the attack was delayed, the French party got back to Mang Cá safe and sound[13].

[Footnote 13: Later the French named Thanh Long Bridge "Pont de l'attentat" — the Bridge of the Assassination Attempt.]

The French forces in Huế at this time consisted of: at Mang Cá, two companies of black African infantry, three platoons of marine infantry in the concession area near the Mang Cá fort, two companies of African riflemen, and one platoon of marine infantry. North of Mang Cá were two gunboats, the *Javeline* and the *Brionval*, plus one small vessel. In total: 81 officers and 1,387 soldiers, with 37 cannon.

In the pitch-black night, while everyone was sleeping, at about 1 a.m. the roar of cannon erupted, shouts thundered to the heavens, and red flames blazed furiously at Mang Cá and in the Legation quarter. At Mang Cá, several squads of our soldiers charged in to set fire to the French barracks; other squads used their guns to break through the West Gate and push inside. Inside, the French troops, caught in a surprise attack, scrambled to their feet; many could not even manage to put on their trousers or shirts, fleeing in all directions; some had no time to seize their weapons, half-awake and utterly disorganized. Captain Bruneau was struck through the chest by a bullet and died without even a gasp. Captain Drouin had both legs shattered. After a few minutes of panic, the French received orders to organize an urgent defense: some fetched flour sacks and empty barrels to fill with earth to block the fort's gates; others fought fires; others guarded the powder magazines, ammunition, and telegraph office; still others held back the waves of assault troops. The Vietnamese attack was launched simultaneously all around the Legation and against the Mang Cá fort. At the fort, the French were ordered to remain motionless, listening and waiting for dawn. Đề-đốc Trần Xuân Soạn thought they had all been killed. Thuyết thereupon shifted the cannon barrels to fire across the river at the French Residency, while the force under Tôn Thất Lễ pressed the attack fiercely, capturing a section of that compound. Most ferocious of all were the Phản-nghĩa soldiers — Thuyết's personal bodyguard — together with the newly released prisoners hoping to redeem their crimes through merit. They carried guns, cannon, or broadswords, their bare torsos glistening, their hair streaming down their backs, fighting hand-to-hand with the enemy with extraordinary courage. The roof and the undercroft of the Legation building, struck by cannon fire, collapsed in many places. From the windows of the Legation, the French fired down into the burning grass where the Vietnamese were swarming. Their fire was so intense that Tôn Thất Lễ and his troops were halted. From cockcrow onward, the cannon on the citadel walls found their targets with increasing accuracy. The Residency building was heavily damaged; ceilings and partitions collapsed entirely, except for the main hall, which still held. Fortunately for the French, our artillery lacked penetrating power. Nevertheless, as day began to break, the French launched a counterattack, and minutes earlier de Courcy had telegraphed Hải Phòng for urgent reinforcements.

At Mang Cá, after the initial terror, Lieutenant-Colonel Pernot and Major Metzinger reorganized their ranks and, once everything was in order, launched an assault to take the Imperial Citadel. They divided their forces into three columns and advanced. Their two warships, guided by the light of the burning houses, shelled the northeastern gate of the Imperial Citadel while their troops crossed to this side of the river, killing every Vietnamese they encountered — old or young, male or female. Cries and lamentations were drowned in the thunder of gunfire and explosions, shaking the very sky. The Vietnamese fought back furiously, shouting to the beat of drums and clappers, charging out to kill the French even as the latter bombarded our administrative buildings, military installations, and homes. Our troops inside the citadel sallied out, contesting every inch of ground with the enemy. When the enemy seized one barricade, another would spring up; bricks, stones, tables, chairs, carts were hurled in chaos to block their path. After about an hour, our forces gradually disintegrated. The French captured six heavy cannon mounted on the ramparts and turned these same guns against our troops.

Though dawn was approaching, the battle continued, but the initiative had passed into French hands. The two places the enemy deliberately shelled first were the Ministry of Personnel (Bộ Lại) and the Ministry of War (Bộ Binh) — the ministries of Tường and Thuyết.

Just before daybreak, our cannon fire began to thin. In the hazy half-light, a column of Arab troops was seen crossing from the Residency to An Hòa Gate, bursting into private homes, killing everyone they met, burning everything burnable, and raping whoever could be raped. By this time, both inside the citadel and outside, our troops had received the order to withdraw. The French tried to enter through Hiền Nhơn Gate but could not break through and had to take a different route. At 8:10 a.m., the French flag was flying from our flagpole — or to be more precise, the French flag at that moment consisted of two scraps of cloth tied together with a belt by an African soldier and hoisted up.

By 9 a.m., quiet had returned. The French entered the Great Interior (Đại Nội). The Imperial Citadel lay cold and deserted. Fearing that the Emperor's palaces might be looted, Pernot stationed a battalion there to stand guard. Outside the palaces, many official residences within the citadel were seized and occupied by French troops.

According to French estimates, the Vietnamese forces that had been active on the night of the 4th–5th of July 1885 numbered about twenty thousand (A. Delvaux says our forces were only six thousand), with between 1,200 and 1,500 killed. We lost a very significant quantity of weaponry: 812 cannon, 16,000 Western rifles in the arsenal, and countless swords and spears in the interior.

The French had 1,387 men, of which 31 were officers, including 4 officers and 19 soldiers killed, 14 seriously wounded, and 50 lightly wounded; many had their clothing burned off (some had to spend the entire day in their underwear)[14].

[Footnote 14: Second Lieutenant Constant was seriously wounded. Second Lieutenant La Croix was shot through the abdomen. Second Lieutenant Heitschel and one soldier were wounded by exploding powder in a thatched hut; both these officers later died.]

Although Thuyết did not succeed as he had wished in his attack on the Mang Cá fort and the French Residency, the French military officers nonetheless praised him for organizing the campaign very thoroughly; his failure was due only to the inferiority of his weapons and his inexperience in military technology. Yet no one can deny that his attack on the French in the Imperial Citadel was motivated by patriotism, apart from the need for self-defense against France's policy at that time of liquidating the entire Vietnamese nation. His two years of military preparations demonstrated his determination to defend the country through a final armed confrontation with French colonialism, even while so many of his colleagues were brooding over defeatist ideas.

The French themselves never disputed the extent of his sacrifice, even to the point of absoluteness, despite their profound hatred of him; yet later, during the colonial period, regrettably, many who were "more royalist than the king"[15] dared to slander him — perhaps out of misunderstanding, or perhaps to curry favor with their colonial masters.

Recently a Southern intellectual, Mr. Diệp Văn Kỳ, put forward an inaccurate judgment as follows: "When Thuận Hóa (Huế) fell, Vietnam lost its sovereignty from that moment." Wrong! Through the two treaties of Quý Mùi (1883) and Giáp Thân (1884), what sovereignty did Vietnam still have? Thuyết's attack on the French in the Imperial Citadel was the final effort of the Vietnamese scholar-gentry and people to recover what had been lost — or at least to avoid the shame of submitting to the enemy.

NOTES

[1] The Patenôtre Treaty of 6 June 1884 (Giáp Thân) confirmed the French protectorate over Annam and Tonkin, replacing the earlier and harsher Harmand Treaty (Quý Mùi, 1883). It left the Nguyễn court nominally sovereign over internal affairs while ceding control of foreign relations and military matters to France.

[2] The date refers to the 24th day of the 4th month of Ất-Dậu, corresponding to 6 June 1885.

[3] The Qing evacuation followed the Sino-French ceasefire signed on 4 April 1885 and the Treaty of Tianjin (9 June 1885), which ended the Sino-French War.

[4] Guerrier was the French official responsible for the formalities of Hàm Nghi's enthronement in 1884, which was marked by tensions and protocol disputes.

[5] Phản-nghĩa: The anti-French volunteer troops, literally "righteous resistance forces," loyal to Tôn Thất Thuyết.

[6] "Hongmen banquet" (Hồng-môn hội-yến): A historical allusion to the famous banquet at Hongmen in 206 BCE during the Chu–Han contention, where an assassination plot was concealed behind a feast.

[7] Nam Kỳ (Cochinchina) had been a French colony since 1862; Bắc Kỳ (Tonkin) became a protectorate in 1884–85.

[8] Tầm thực (蠶食): Literally "silkworm eating," a classical Chinese concept describing gradual territorial encroachment, as a silkworm eats a leaf bit by bit.

[9] The return of gifts was a grave diplomatic insult in Confucian court protocol.

[10] This corresponds to 3 July 1885 (the 21st day of the 5th month of Ất-Dậu).

[11] The Imperial Prince Hoài Đức was a younger brother of Emperor Thiệu Trị (r. 1841–1847) and thus a senior member of the imperial clan.

[12] Bishop Gaspar (also spelled Caspar) was a French missionary whose extensive network among Vietnamese Catholics made him a valuable intelligence asset for the French.

[13] The Bridge of the Assassination Attempt (Pont de l'attentat) was the name the French later gave to Thanh Long Bridge in memory of the surprise attack.

[14] The condition of French troops — many fighting in their underwear — illustrates the complete surprise achieved by the Vietnamese attack.

[15] "Plus royaliste que le roi" — the French expression refers to those who, during the colonial period, outdid the colonial authorities themselves in denigrating Vietnamese national heroes.

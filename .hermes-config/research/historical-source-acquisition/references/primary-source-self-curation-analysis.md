# Analyzing Self-Curation in Historical Figures' Writings

When a user asks about a historical figure's own writings — letters, memoirs, proclamations — the real question is usually: **what does this tell me about the person, and what was the person trying to make me believe?**

All historical figures who write self-accountingly (memoirs, autobiographies, apologia, deathbed dictations) practice some degree of self-curation. The skill is in reading both what they show and what they suppress, and knowing which sources reveal the unguarded mind vs. the curated legend.

## Source Typology by Guard Level

| Source Type | Guard Level | Reliability for Mind of Author |
|---|---|---|
| **Private letters** (spouse, close friend, sibling) | Lowest | High — but still subject to mood, performance, audience |
| **Official correspondence** (dispatches, instructions) | Medium | Shows priorities, decision-making, rhetorical framing for subordinates |
| **Proclamations / bulletins / public addresses** | Highest | Pure public image — reveals what author WANTS to seem true |
| **Memoirs written in power** | High | Active image-shaping at peak |
| **Memoirs dictated in defeat/exile** | Highest | Last-chance legacy control — most heavily curated |
| **Deathbed wills / testaments** | High | Final statement, but often deliberately crafted for posterity |
| **Conversations recorded by others** | Unknown | Filtered through the recorder's agenda (see: Las Cases' Memorial) |

The gold standard for seeing the "unguarded" mind is **cross-referencing private letters against public proclamations** — the gap between them is where the real person lives.

## The Two-Quiver Method

When given a figure's writings, keep two parallel lists:

### Quiver A: What They Emphasize
- What do they claim as their greatest achievements?
- What origin story do they tell about themselves?
- What external forces do they credit (destiny, God, luck, the people)?
- What internal qualities do they highlight (decisiveness, mercy, vision)?
- What opposition do they characterize as villainous/incompetent?

### Quiver B: What They Minimize, Qualify, or Omit
- What defeats or failures do they mention only to blame on others?
- What morally ambiguous actions do they omit entirely?
- What inconvenient allies do they stop talking about?
- What human costs do they render abstract (numbers, not faces)?
- What decisions do they refuse to discuss?
- What personal qualities shown by private letters are absent from the memoirs?

The gap between A and B is where the meaningful analysis lives — it tells you what image the author was constructing and what they were running from.

## Assessing Writing Quality

When asked whether a historical figure was a "good writer," distinguish:

1. **Literary/poetic quality** — mastery of the written language as an art form
2. **Rhetorical power** — ability to move readers to action or emotion
3. **Clarity/concision** — ability to communicate complex ideas efficiently
4. **Psychological vividness** — ability to convey their inner state

A figure may score high on 2-4 and low on 1. Napoleon is the classic example: imperfect French, dictating to secretaries, yet rhetorically extraordinarily effective.

**Key questions for assessment:**
- Did they write in a learned language or their native tongue? (Many figures wrote in languages they learned secondarily — Latin, French, English — which constrains literary quality)
- Did they dictate or write by hand? Dictation produces a different rhythm
- Were they writing for contemporaries or posterity? The intended audience shapes the register
- What was their education? A military academy education produces different prose than a classical literary education

## What to Look For in the Gap

Concrete patterns that reveal self-curation in memoirs/autobiographies:

| Pattern | What It Means | Example |
|---------|--------------|---------|
| Blaming subordinates for failures | Protecting own judgment | Napoleon blaming Marmont for Paris's fall, generals for Waterloo |
| Attributing defeats to "nature" (weather, terrain, disease) | Dodging strategic responsibility | Napoleon blaming the Russian winter (he knew winter was coming) |
| Claiming to have been betrayed | Externalizing loss of control | Napoleon's St. Helena refrain: "the English oligarchy assassinated me" |
| Omitting specific casualty numbers | Hiding human cost | Most commanders in memoirs |
| Describing morally questionable acts as "necessary" | Justification without apology | Napoleon's execution of prisoners at Jaffa |
| Elevating minor successes near defeats | Re-framing the narrative | Detailed post-Waterloo tactical "near-miss" analysis |
| Over-detailing strategic rationale | Protesting too much | Lengthy justification for decisions few criticize |
| Silence on specific people | Erasing inconvenient relationships | Talleyrand, Fouché — barely mentioned |

## Procedure

1. **Identify the available primary corpus** — letters, memoirs, proclamations, conversations recorded by others
2. **Separate private sources from public ones** — private letters are the least curated
3. **For memoirs, check the circumstances of composition** — written in power? in exile? under pressure? intended audience?
4. **Compare public claims with documented facts** — independent sources vs. the figure's version
5. **Identify recurring themes in the public presentation** — what does the figure bring up unprompted? That's what they want known
6. **Identify topics the figure avoids or hedges** — that's what they want hidden
7. **Read the will/testament/last statement** — the final act of self-curation, often the most concentrated
8. **Present both sides** — never endorse the self-curated image uncritically, but also don't dismiss it as mere propaganda. The figure's self-image IS historically significant data, even where it's inaccurate

## Iron Rule (No Editorializing)

Per the iron rule: no characterizing the figure's politics, ideology, or moral character with value judgments ("troubling / controversial / problematic"). State facts about what they wrote, what they omitted, and what independent sources show. Let the reader draw conclusions about the gap.

Good: "The memoirs devote three pages to the Egyptian campaign's successes and eight lines to the Jaffa massacre, which he describes as a military necessity."

Bad: "His troubling decision to execute prisoners at Jaffa reveals his ruthless character."

## Colonial-Era Application

For the user's core work (French colonial texts on Indochina), this method applies directly:

- **Paul Chack** (colonial adventure writer) — emphasizes French civilising mission and heroism, omits colonial violence and his own collaborationist politics
- **Military memoirs** — emphasize tactical brilliance and French sacrifice, minimise atrocities and Vietnamese resistance
- **Administrative reports** — emphasize order and progress, minimise resistance and exploitation

Cross-reference the public memoir against the private correspondence when available. The gap reveals the colonial mindset at work.
